# M7-L1 – Reflection, Refraction and Total Internal Reflection {#L1}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Electromagnetic waves, angle measurements and trigonometry. [Previous lesson](M6-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive Snell’s law from phase continuity and calculate critical angles.

## Light at an Interface {#S1}

A plane wave arrives from a homogeneous isotropic medium of refractive index $n_1$ at a second medium $n_2$. Its frequency is preserved at a stationary interface. In nonmagnetic, weakly absorbing materials the phase speed is approximately $v_{\mathrm{ph}}\approx c/n$. Propagation angles are measured from the surface normal, not the surface itself. Thus the angle of incidence $\theta_1$ is between the incident ray and the normal. The reflected ray satisfies $\theta_r=\theta_1$. The transmitted ray can change direction because the wavelengths $\lambda_i=\lambda_0/n_i$ differ at fixed frequency. Rays represent energy flow only in the geometrical-optics limit; the full field consists of wave amplitudes whose energy fractions follow from Fresnel theory.

## Derivation from Phase Matching {#S2}

Write the phase of the incident field as $\phi_1=\boldsymbol{k}_1\cdot\boldsymbol{r}-\omega t$. Because the spatial and temporal phase must agree at every point on the interface, tangential wave-vector components coincide: $k_1\sin\theta_1=k_2\sin\theta_2$. Using $k_i=n_i\omega/c$ yields Snell's law immediately:

$$
n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2.
$$

The corresponding condition for the reflected vector yields the law of reflection. If $n_1>n_2$, no real propagating transmission angle is possible beyond a sufficiently large $\theta_1$. The critical angle obeys $\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1$. Total internal reflection then occurs, but an evanescent field exists on the low-index side. A second nearby interface can couple to that field, so perfect isolation is not a universal assertion.

## Worked Example: Air and Glass {#S3}

For $n_1=1.00$, $n_2=1.50$, and $\theta_1=30^\circ$, we obtain $\sin\theta_2=(1/1.5)\sin30^\circ=1/3$ and hence $\theta_2\approx19.47^\circ$. In the reverse direction, from glass to air, the critical angle is $\theta_c=\arcsin(1/1.5)\approx41.81^\circ$. A $50^\circ$ incidence angle in glass gives total internal reflection. Always draw the normal before indicating ray directions and angles. The diagram also shows why a critical angle exists only when passing from larger to smaller refractive index. Low-loss guiding in a fibre requires suitable geometry and modes in addition to the critical-angle condition.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the refracted angle for $n_1=1$, $n_2=2$, $\theta_1=30^\circ$.
2. Can total internal reflection occur from air ($n=1$) into glass ($n=1.5$)? Explain.
3. Why is the tangential component of $\boldsymbol{k}$ conserved at the interface?

### Solutions

1. $\sin\theta_2=0.25$ and $\theta_2\approx14.48^\circ$.
2. No; a real refracted direction exists for every incident angle because $n_1<n_2$.
3. Phase matching along the entire stationary interface requires the same tangential wave number.

## Conditions of Validity {#S5}

Snell's scalar law does not by itself specify reflected intensity, polarization or phase jumps. In absorbing materials the refractive index $n$ may be complex, and fields and energy flux require more careful treatment. Strongly structured optics or apertures comparable to a wavelength exceed ray-optics accuracy. Also check that $n$ is evaluated at the frequency corresponding to the specified vacuum wavelength. In a dispersive substance refraction angles depend on frequency, so a white beam can split spectrally.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Reflection and refraction follow from phase continuity. Total internal reflection does not remove the evanescent field. The next lesson studies image formation by lenses.
