# M16-L1 – Measurands, Calibration and Metrological Traceability {#L1}

**Course area:** M16 – Bachelor: Experimental Physics and Measurement Analysis

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M1: units, M2: linear functions, M13: notions of uncertainty. [Previous lesson](M15-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Define a measurand operationally, use a calibration line and explain metrological traceability of a measurement result.

## Measurand and Measurement Model {#S1}

A *measurand* is the quantity a specific experiment intends to determine. Its definition must identify the object, conditions and, where relevant, time: for example, the temperature at the center of a water sample after thermal equilibrium. A sensor reading is not automatically that measurand. In a simple model the instrument provides a signal $U$ related to temperature $T$ by a calibration function $U=aT+b$. Dimensions must agree: $[a]=\mathrm{V/K}$ and $[b]=\mathrm V$. Values are converted into consistent units before calculation. *Calibration* establishes a relation between reference quantity values with associated uncertainties and indications with associated uncertainties; it is not synonymous with instrument adjustment or certification.

## Calibration and Traceability {#S2}

A calibration result documents the relation, its range, uncertainties and operating conditions. Inverting the linear model gives

$$
T=\frac{U-b}{a},\qquad a\ne0.
$$

According to the VIM, *metrological traceability* is a property of a measurement result: a documented unbroken chain of calibrations relates it to a reference, with each link contributing to measurement uncertainty. A national laboratory name printed on an instrument does not itself establish traceability of the result. Traceability also does not guarantee fitness for purpose. That question separately requires judging range, bias, uncertainty and influence quantities.

## Example: Temperature Sensor {#S3}

A hypothetical sensor reads $U_1=0.500\,\mathrm V$ at $T_1=273.15\,\mathrm K$ and $U_2=2.500\,\mathrm V$ at $T_2=373.15\,\mathrm K$. Its slope and intercept are

$$
a=\frac{U_2-U_1}{T_2-T_1}=0.0200\,\mathrm{V/K},\qquad b=U_1-aT_1=-4.963\,\mathrm V.
$$

For $U=1.300\,\mathrm V$, temperature is $T=(1.300+4.963)/0.0200=313.15\,\mathrm K$, or $40.0\,^{\circ}\mathrm C$. The line in the diagram visualizes this teaching example only. In practice reference points and indications have uncertainties; the numerical temperature alone is not yet a complete measurement result.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the slope for $\Delta U=0.80\,\mathrm V$ over $\Delta T=40\,\mathrm K$.
2. Compute $T$ for $U=1.70\,\mathrm V$ using the example calibration.
3. Why is owning a traceably calibrated device not enough to make every result traceable?

### Solutions

1. $a=0.020\,\mathrm{V/K}$.
2. $T=(1.70+4.963)/0.0200=333.15\,\mathrm K=60\,^{\circ}\mathrm C$.
3. Traceability belongs to the specific result and depends on the documented calibration chain, conditions and uncertainties.

## Influence Quantities and Validity {#S5}

A linear model can fail due to temperature drift, hysteresis or saturation. Two points define a line but do not establish linearity or long-term stability. Real calibration needs further checkpoints and an independent uncertainty contribution from the reference. The computed temperature in this example must not be mistaken for a certified calibration. A traceability chain includes references, methods, dated records and uncertainty contributions. State the intended decision: an uncertainty acceptable for trend monitoring may be inadequate for a safety-critical threshold decision.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Calibration links reference values to indications; traceability belongs to a documented result, not merely the instrument.
