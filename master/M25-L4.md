# M25-L4 – Sensor signals, sampling and antialiasing {#L4}

**Course area:** M25 – Bachelor: Interdisciplinary applications and scientific practice

**Level:** Bachelor · elective

**Prerequisites:** M4: harmonic waves; M16: measurement data; M23-L7: sensor acquisition. [Previous lesson](M25-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish an analog signal from samples and reconstruction, and calculate an alias-frequency example.

## Guiding question and observation {#S1}

A sensor initially produces a continuous voltage that is amplified, filtered and sampled before digitization. Even a noiseless instrument can display a spurious low-frequency oscillation when sampled too slowly. Matching discrete sample values does not determine the unique continuous signal between them without a bandwidth restriction. An analog low-pass filter before the analog-to-digital converter should sufficiently suppress unwanted frequencies beyond the usable band.

## Physical model and equations {#S2}

For a sinusoid $x(t)=A\sin(2\pi f_0t+\phi)$, samples are taken at $t_n=n/f_s$. Frequencies differing by integer multiples of $f_s$ can produce the same sampled sequence with a suitable phase. The Nyquist boundary $f_s/2$ limits the uniquely representable positive-frequency band for ideally band-limited signals; a sinusoid exactly at the boundary raises phase and sampling issues. Real filters need a transition band and sufficient stopband attenuation, not merely the symbolic inequality $f_s>2f_{\max}$.

$$
x[n]=A\sin\!\left(2\pi\frac{f_0}{f_s}n+\phi\right),\qquad f_{\mathrm N}=\frac{f_s}{2},\qquad f_{\mathrm{alias}}=|f_0-mf_s|.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

With $f_s=10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $f_0=7\,\mathrm{Hz}$, choosing $m=1$ gives an alias frequency of $3\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Because $\sin(2\pi\cdot7n/10)=-\sin(2\pi\cdot3n/10)$, the samples also match a phase-inverted $3$-Hz oscillation. The illustration shows the continuous $7$-Hz curve, sample locations separated by $0.1\,\mathrm s$, and a compatible lower-frequency alternative. Adequate analog filtering is required to avoid ambiguity about the original frequency content.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the Nyquist limit for $f_s=1000\,\mathrm{Hz}$?
2. What is the alias frequency for $f_0=70\,\mathrm{Hz}$ at $f_s=100\,\mathrm{Hz}$?
3. Why must antialias filtering occur before digitization?

### Solutions

1. $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
2. $|70-100|=30\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
3. After sampling, distinct analog frequencies become indistinguishable from the samples alone, unless additional information is provided.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The alias expression assumes one ideal sinusoidal component. Real acquisition chains have finite roll-off, jitter, quantization noise and bandwidth limitations; this schematic is not a filter design.

## Summary and outlook {#S6}

Sampling turns continuous information into numbers and requires a justified bandwidth limit. Without it, the original signal cannot be uniquely recovered from samples.
