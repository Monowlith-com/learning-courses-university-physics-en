# M13-L6 – Bose–Einstein Statistics and Condensation {#L6}

**Course area:** M13 – Bachelor: Statistical Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Grand canonical modes, quantized energies, and Fermi statistics from M13-L4 and M13-L5. [Previous lesson](M13-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the Bose occupation, understand the chemical-potential bound, and distinguish condensation in an ideal gas.

## Occupancies Without a Pauli Limit {#S1}

Bosons have integer spin and are not subject to the fermionic Pauli occupation limit. An ideal bosonic mode admits occupations $n=0,1,2,\ldots$. At energy $\varepsilon$ and chemical potential $\mu$, its grand canonical weights form a geometric series provided $e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}<1$. This gives

$$
\bar n_B(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}-1}.
$$

The minus one in the denominator distinguishes bosons from fermions. It does not mean that occupation is always large: for $\varepsilon-\mu\gg k_BT$, Bose occupation approaches the classical Boltzmann limit. The ground state becomes particularly important as chemical potential approaches its energy.

## Chemical Potential and the Condensation Threshold {#S2}

For ideal bosons with conserved total particle number, $\mu$ must remain below the lowest one-particle energy $\varepsilon_0$ in the usual grand canonical construction. In a uniform three-dimensional nonrelativistic ideal gas, the excited modes can hold only a finite number of particles at a given temperature. Below a critical temperature the ground state acquires macroscopic occupation. At number density $n$, the thermodynamic-limit result is

$$
T_c=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_B}\left(\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}.
$$

Here $\zeta(3/2)\approx2.612$; spin degeneracy, trapping, and interactions can modify the quantitative relation. Bose–Einstein condensation means macroscopic occupation of a quantum state, not classical condensation of a vapor into a liquid.

## Example: Mean Occupation of a Single Mode {#S3}

For a mode with $\varepsilon-\mu=k_BT\ln2$, the exponential factor is $e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}=2$, hence

$$
\bar n_B=\frac{1}{2-1}=1.
$$

Doubling the scaled separation to $\varepsilon-\mu=2k_BT\ln2$ changes the exponential to $e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}=4$ and gives $\bar n_B=1/3$. The graph shows how $\bar n_B$ increases rapidly as $\varepsilon-\mu\to0^+$, whereas the Fermi occupation remains finite at zero separation. A formal divergence in one grand canonical mode does not by itself establish a condensate in a finite real system. Particle-number conservation and the number of available excited states determine the onset of condensation.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Evaluate $\bar n_B$ for $\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)=\ln3$.
2. By what factor does $T_c$ rise when ideal 3D number density increases eightfold?
3. Why can $\mu$ not exceed $\varepsilon_0$ in an ideal gas of conserved bosons?

### Solutions

1. $\bar n_B=1/(3-1)=1/2$.
2. By $T_c\propto n^{2/3}$, a factor of 4.
3. Otherwise the ground-state grand canonical occupation series fails to converge.

## Photons, Interactions, and Dimensionality {#S5}

Uniform ideal gases and trapped atomic gases have different densities of states, leading to different critical dependencies on temperature and particle number. The ordinary quadratic-dispersion uniform ideal gas does not undergo finite-temperature Bose–Einstein condensation in one or two dimensions in the standard thermodynamic limit. An interacting two-dimensional gas may instead show a Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition, which has a different kind of order. Thermal photon number is not conserved, giving an equilibrium chemical potential $\mu=0$ rather than an independently fixed particle number. Interactions can strongly modify stability, quasiparticles, and critical behavior.
