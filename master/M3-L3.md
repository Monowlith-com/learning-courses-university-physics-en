# M3-L3 – Friction, Normal Forces and Circular Motion {#L3}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Newton’s laws and free-body diagrams from M3-L2. [Previous lesson](M3-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish static and kinetic friction and solve radial force balances for circular motion.

## Contact and Change of Direction {#S1}

A moving body can accelerate even at constant speed: on a circular path the **direction** of its velocity vector changes continuously. For radius $R$ and constant speed $v$, the radial acceleration points toward the centre. “Centripetal force” is not an additional fundamental force; it names the **net radial component** of the actual forces. Tension, gravity, static friction or combinations of forces can supply it.

## Static Friction, Kinetic Friction and Radial Equation {#S2}

In the simple Coulomb model, the magnitude of static friction is bounded, whereas kinetic friction during sliding is approximately proportional to the normal force:

$$
|F_{\mathrm H}|\le\mu_{\mathrm H}N,\qquad
|F_{\mathrm G}|\approx\mu_{\mathrm G}N.
$$

$\mu_{\mathrm H}$ and $\mu_{\mathrm G}$ are dimensionless and depend on surfaces and conditions. Determine $N$ from the force balance normal to the surface, rather than assuming it always equals $mg$. For uniform circular motion,

$$
a_r=\frac{v^2}{R}=R\omega^2,\qquad \sum F_{\mathrm{rad}}=m\frac{v^2}{R}.
$$

Static friction opposes impending relative motion; kinetic friction opposes actual relative sliding.

## Worked Example: A Flat Road Curve {#S3}

A car takes a flat curve of radius $R=50\,\mathrm m$. In a simplified model, static friction between tyres and road supplies the radial force. With $\mu_{\mathrm H}=0.60$ and vertical equilibrium $N=mg$, the condition $mv^2/R\le\mu_{\mathrm H}mg$ gives

$$
v_{\max}=\sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm H}gR}
=\sqrt{0.60\cdot9.81\cdot50}\,\mathrm{m/s}
\approx17.2\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

This is about $61.8\,\mathrm{km/h}$. The mass cancels in this idealised model. The diagram shows inward radial acceleration and velocity tangent to the circular path.

*Velocity is tangent to the path; the required acceleration points inward.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find radial acceleration at $v=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $R=25\,\mathrm m$.
2. A block on level ground has $N=100\,\mathrm N$ and $\mu_{\mathrm H}=0.4$. What is the maximum static friction?
3. Why is the static-friction force not automatically $40\,\mathrm N$ for a $5\,\mathrm N$ horizontal pull?

### Solutions

1. $a_r=v^2/R=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
2. $F_{\mathrm H,max}=0.4\cdot100=40\,\mathrm N$.
3. Static friction adjusts up to its limit; if equilibrium is possible, it is $5\,\mathrm N$ opposite the pull.

## Model Limitations and Real-World Safety {#S5}

The calculation is not a driving recommendation: friction coefficient, water film, tyres, banking, road conditions and driver actions vary. Nor does a new real outward force act on the car in the inertial frame. Such language often results from silently switching to a rotating vehicle frame. A reliable solution first draws actual forces, selects “toward the centre” as the radial direction and only then tests the static-friction inequality.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Friction is a contact force with a static limit; circular motion requires a radial force balance. We now turn to energy methods.
