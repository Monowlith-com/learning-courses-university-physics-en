# M14-L3 – Lattice Vibrations and Phonons {#L3}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian dynamics, waves, differential equations and quantum statistics. [Previous lesson](M14-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the dispersion of a monatomic chain and distinguish acoustic phonon modes from their thermal occupations.

## Normal Coordinates of a Crystal {#S1}

A crystal is not a rigid lattice: atoms vibrate around equilibrium positions. For sufficiently small displacements $u_n$, a harmonic expansion of interatomic forces is appropriate. Fourier-transforming the coupled equations of motion yields independent normal modes. Each mode has a wave vector $k$ and frequency $\omega(k)$; upon quantization its energy comes in quanta $\hbar\omega$ called phonons. Phonons are collective excitations, not additional atoms moving through the solid. They contribute to heat capacity, heat transport and electron–phonon coupling. The number of polarization branches depends on the spatial dimension and on the number of atoms in the primitive cell.

## Monatomic Chain and Dispersion {#S2}

For identical masses $m$ at spacing $a$, linked by nearest-neighbor springs of stiffness $K$, Newton's equation is $m\ddot u_n=K(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n)$. Inserting $u_n=Ae^{i(kna-\omega t)}$ yields

$$
\omega(k)=2\sqrt{\frac Km}\left\lvert\sin\frac{ka}{2}\right\rvert.
$$

It suffices to use the first Brillouin zone $-\pi/a\leq k\leq\pi/a$, since wave vectors differing by a reciprocal lattice vector have equivalent phases on lattice sites. For $\lvert ka\rvert\ll1$ the dispersion is linear, $\omega\approx a\sqrt{K/m}\lvert k\rvert$, giving sound speed $v_s=a\sqrt{K/m}$. Group velocity equals $d\omega/dk$ and vanishes in magnitude at the zone edge.

## Example: Zone-Boundary Frequency {#S3}

For $m=4.0\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $K=100\,\mathrm{N/m}$ the maximum angular frequency at $\lvert k\rvert=\pi/a$ is

$$
\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/m}=1.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$

This is an angular frequency, not hertz: $f=\omega/(2\pi)\approx1.59\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. For $a=0.30\,\mathrm{nm}$ the long-wavelength sound speed is $v_s=1.50\times10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\omega_{\max}$ for $K/m=9\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$.
2. What dispersion law holds for $\lvert ka\rvert\ll1$?
3. Why does a monatomic one-dimensional primitive cell lack optical modes?

### Solutions

1. $2\sqrt{K/m}=6\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
2. $\omega\approx v_s\lvert k\rvert$ with $v_s=a\sqrt{K/m}$.
3. One displacement degree of freedom per cell gives only one acoustic branch.

## Quantum Interpretation and Validity {#S5}

At very low temperature only relatively long-wavelength modes are appreciably thermally occupied. The three-dimensional Debye model replaces acoustic modes by a suitably counted set with approximately linear dispersion, giving a low-temperature heat capacity typically proportional to $T^3$. At sufficiently high temperatures, the classical Dulong–Petit limit approaches $3Nk_B$ for three degrees of freedom per atom. A basis of two atoms produces additional optical branches; the present monatomic model has no optical branch. Real forces are anisotropic and anharmonic. Anharmonicity causes thermal expansion and finite phonon lifetimes, so an ideal harmonic chain alone does not determine a finite scattering-limited thermal conductivity.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Dispersion links microscopic forces to collective vibrational frequencies; phonons are the quanta of those modes.
