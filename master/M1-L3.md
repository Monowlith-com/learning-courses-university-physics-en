# M1-L3 – Orders of Magnitude and Fermi Estimates {#L3}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor: Foundations of Physics

**Level:** Bachelor · Introductory

**Prerequisites:** SI units, powers of ten and logarithms. [Previous lesson](M1-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Decompose a complex quantitative question into testable assumptions, calculate an estimate and state its sensitivity.

## How Much Air Is in a Lecture Hall? {#S1}

Often we have neither all the measurements nor the exact geometry. A physically motivated estimate is still possible. The goal is not to produce a number with many decimal places, but to find a plausible scale and the dominant uncertainties. We investigate the mass of air in an approximately rectangular lecture hall.

## Method and Order of Magnitude {#S2}

For a positive quantity $Q$ in a fixed unit, write its numerical value as $q=a\cdot10^n$ with $1\leq a<10$. The nearest power of ten on a logarithmic scale is $10^n$ if $a<\sqrt{10}$, and $10^{n+1}$ if $a>\sqrt{10}$. At a tie a convention is needed. This is not necessarily the same exponent as in scientific notation.

A Fermi estimate consists of decomposing the question into factors, making rough justified assumptions, calculating with consistent dimensions and then checking plausibility. For a room with length $l$, width $b$, height $h$ and assumed uniform air density $\rho$,

$$
V=lbh,\qquad m=\rho V.
$$

For small relative changes of positive quantities, logarithmic differentiation gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm dm}{m}=\frac{\mathrm d\rho}{\rho}+\frac{\mathrm dl}{l}+\frac{\mathrm db}{b}+\frac{\mathrm dh}{h}.
$$

This reveals which assumption has a particularly strong effect on the result. The relation describes local sensitivity, not automatically a statistical measurement uncertainty.

## Example: Mass of Air {#S3}

Assume $l=15\,\mathrm m$, $b=12\,\mathrm m$, $h=4\,\mathrm m$ and $\rho=1.2\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ (illustrative room-air density). Then $V=720\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $m=864\,\mathrm{kg}$. A sensible report is about $9\cdot10^2\,\mathrm{kg}$, or order of magnitude $10^3\,\mathrm{kg}$.

With plausible intervals $l=13$ to $17\,\mathrm m$, $b=10$ to $14\,\mathrm m$, $h=3$ to $5\,\mathrm m$, and $\rho=1.1$ to $1.3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, the monotonic product gives bounds of $429\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $1547\,\mathrm{kg}$. This range is an assumption scenario, not a confidence interval. The order of magnitude is robust.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the order of magnitude of $4.1\cdot10^5\,\mathrm m$ on a logarithmic scale?
2. How does the estimated air mass change if only the room height is halved?
3. Estimate the number of seconds in five years, using 365 days per year.

### Solutions

1. $10^6\,\mathrm m$, since $4.1>\sqrt{10}$.
2. It halves, provided density remains unchanged.
3. $5\cdot365\cdot24\cdot3600=157680000\,\mathrm s$, approximately $1.6\cdot10^8\,\mathrm s$.

## Making Assumptions Explicit {#S5}

Air density depends, among other factors, on pressure and temperature. Furniture and people displace air; a room may have an irregular shape. The calculation therefore does not determine the actual mass. An estimate is particularly useful when it specifies which inputs would need to be measured to improve it. Orders of magnitude also depend on the chosen unit.

## Summary {#S6}

A good estimate shows not only its result but also its assumptions and sensitivity. The next lesson extends the geometric description of quantities to include directions.
