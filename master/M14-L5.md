# M14-L5 – Fermi Gas and Electron Transport in Metals {#L5}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M13-L5, M14-L4, electricity and elementary quantum statistics. [Previous lesson](M14-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate Fermi momentum and energy and assess Drude transport and the influence of scattering.

## Degenerate Conduction Electrons {#S1}

In a metal, electron states are occupied up to a Fermi energy. At $T=0$, noninteracting spin-$1/2$ electrons fill the three-dimensional free-electron gas to a Fermi wave vector $k_F$. Pauli exclusion permits two spin states per momentum state. Metallic conductivity does not mean that every electron responds freely and equally to an applied field: states near the Fermi surface are most relevant to a small shift in occupation. In actual crystals the Fermi surface satisfies $E_n(\mathbf k)=E_F$ and may be far from spherical. The simplest free gas nevertheless gives a useful scale for electronic occupation and energy.

## Fermi Energy and Relaxation Time {#S2}

Counting spin-degenerate states in a sphere in momentum space gives

$$
k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m_e}.
$$

Here $n=N/V$ is the electron density. Classical Drude transport adds an average momentum relaxation time $\tau$ to electric acceleration. In a steady field the drift velocity is $\mathbf v_d=-e\tau\mathbf E/m_e$, yielding the scalar conductivity 

$$
\sigma=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m_e}.
$$

These expressions use an isotropic free-electron band and a single relaxation time. Anisotropic crystals and multiple carrier species require effective masses and frequently a conductivity tensor rather than one scalar.

## Example: Free-Electron Density {#S3}

Take a free-electron density $n=8.5\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Then

$$
k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}\approx1.36\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.
$$

With $\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $m_e=9.109\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ one finds $E_F\approx7.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ after rounding. The diagram shows occupied states as a disk in a two-dimensional section through the three-dimensional Fermi sphere. A weak applied field produces a small displacement of the distribution. This is a conceptual occupation-space diagram, not a depiction of electron trajectories among atoms. The Fermi velocity and the drift velocity are different quantities, and the latter is usually much smaller.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $k_F$ scale if the electron density is doubled?
2. What happens to $\sigma$ when $\tau$ doubles at fixed $n$?
3. Why is a real crystal Fermi surface not always spherical?

### Solutions

1. It gains a factor $2^{1/3}\approx1.260$.
2. Drude conductivity doubles.
3. The band energy $E_n(\mathbf k)$ generally depends on direction because of crystal symmetry and the periodic potential.

## What the Model Explains and Misses {#S5}

Drude theory captures Ohmic conduction and the qualitative meaning of a relaxation time, but not the quantum electronic heat capacity or every Hall measurement. Sommerfeld theory combines Fermi–Dirac occupation with free electrons and explains why the low-temperature electronic heat capacity is approximately linear in $T$. In real metals scattering may arise from phonons, impurities, defects and electron–electron processes. Their contributions are not always simply additive, and one constant relaxation time is only an approximation. The formulas for $E_F$ use the free-electron mass and should not be transferred without qualification to narrow, correlated or anisotropic bands.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Fermi statistics determines available electron states; transport also requires their dynamics and scattering.
