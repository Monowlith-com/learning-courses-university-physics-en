# M14-L1 – Crystal Structure, Lattices and Unit Cells {#L1}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Vector algebra, geometry, Fourier waves and introductory quantum theory. [Previous lesson](M13-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish Bravais lattice from basis, construct primitive cells and obtain the reciprocal lattice of simple crystals.

## Periodicity as a Structural Principle {#S1}

An ideal crystal is invariant under a discrete set of translations. Its Bravais lattice comprises vectors $\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$, where the $n_i$ are integers and the $\mathbf a_i$ are linearly independent primitive vectors. A primitive cell tiles space by those translations. The actual atoms are specified by a basis attached to each lattice point. A cubic lattice may therefore contain a single atomic species or a basis of several atoms. Distinguishing lattice from basis matters: the Bravais lattice encodes translations, not all symmetry operations or chemical details. In three dimensions fourteen Bravais lattices belong to seven crystal systems.

## Primitive and Reciprocal Vectors {#S2}

The primitive-cell volume is $V_c=\lvert\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\rvert$. To Fourier-analyze periodic quantities one defines reciprocal vectors by $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$. One construction is

$$
\mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)}
$$

with cyclic permutations for the other indices. All reciprocal lattice vectors are integer combinations $\mathbf G=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3$. Since $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$, these plane waves share the crystal periodicity. Reciprocal space is not a second array of actual atoms: its points specify spatial Fourier modes and possible diffraction conditions.

## Example: Simple Cubic Crystal {#S3}

For a simple cubic crystal with lattice constant $a=0.40\,\mathrm{nm}$ choose $\mathbf a_1=a\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, $\mathbf a_2=a\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\mathbf a_3=a\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Then $V_c=a^3=0.064\,\mathrm{nm^3}$ and

$$
\lvert\mathbf b_1\rvert=\frac{2\pi}{a}=15.71\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}.
$$

A complicated atomic basis can preserve the reciprocal Bravais lattice while extinguishing particular scattering peaks. Knowing the cell edges alone does not therefore determine the full diffraction intensity.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the primitive-cell volume of a simple cubic crystal with $a=0.50\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. What is the magnitude of a primitive reciprocal vector?
3. Why can different atomic bases share the same Bravais lattice?

### Solutions

1. $V_c=a^3=0.125\,\mathrm{nm^3}$.
2. $2\pi/a=12.57\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$.
3. Translational symmetry can be identical despite different atoms inside each cell.

## Real Crystals and Model Limits {#S5}

The primitive cell need not equal the visually convenient conventional cell: in body-centered and face-centered cubic systems their volumes differ. Miller indices $(hkl)$ label families of lattice planes, rather than fully specifying all atomic contents. Thermal motion, defects, finite crystallite size and surfaces spoil exact periodicity. The reciprocal construction here uses a $2\pi$ Fourier convention; other mathematical conventions move this factor. The next lesson uses an ideal coherent-scattering model and separates lattice selection rules from basis-dependent scattering amplitudes.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Lattice and basis separate periodic translations from atomic contents. Reciprocal vectors simplify diffraction conditions.
