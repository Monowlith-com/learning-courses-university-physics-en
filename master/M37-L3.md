# M37-L3 – Galaxy Rotation and Dynamical Mass Profiles {#L3}

**Course area:** M37 – Master: Astrophysics and Cosmology

**Level:** Master · advanced specialization

**Prerequisites:** M6: Newtonian dynamics; M37-L2: compact sources · [Previous lesson](M37-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Infer an enclosed mass profile from an idealized flat rotation curve and state the symmetry assumption.

## Guiding question and context {#S1}

Stars and gas in galaxies do not orbit solely under the gravity of visible stars. Observed rotation curves are important evidence for additional gravitating matter or modified dynamics, but their interpretation requires explicit modelling. Real galactic disks are not spherical; the following equation first describes a spherical comparison model.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

For circular motion around a spherical enclosed mass $M(<r)$, $v_c^2/r=GM(<r)/r^2$. Hence $M(<r)=rv_c^2/G$. If $v_c(r)\approx v_0$ over some radial range, the enclosed mass in this spherical model grows approximately linearly with $r$. This differs from the Keplerian decline beyond a finite centrally concentrated mass.

$$
M(<r)=\frac{rv_c^2(r)}{G}\qquad\text{(spherical circular-orbit model)}.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

With $v_c=200\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $r=10\,\mathrm{kpc}$, one finds $M(<r)\approx1.85\times10^{41}\,\mathrm{kg}\approx9.30\times10^{10}M_\odot$. At the same orbital speed, $20\,\mathrm{kpc}$ would imply twice the enclosed model mass. The figure compares a flat curve with a Keplerian decline; it is not a galaxy data set.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $M(<r)$ change when $r$ doubles at constant $v_c$?
2. What happens to $M(<r)$ if $v_c$ doubles at fixed $r$?
3. Why should the spherical formula not be applied unchanged to every disk galaxy?

### Solutions

1. It doubles.
2. It quadruples.
3. Disk mass distributions and their gravitational fields are not spherical, and gas motions may be non-circular.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The formula assumes circular orbits, Newtonian gravity and spherical symmetry. Inclination, bars, turbulence, stellar mass and resolution affect real fits. Dark matter is an interpretation supported by multiple observations, not a directly measured quantity in this exercise.
