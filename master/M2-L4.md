# M2-L4 – Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and Normal Modes {#L4}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Matrix multiplication, quadratic equations and harmonic oscillation. [Previous lesson](M2-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate eigenvalues and eigenvectors and interpret normal modes of two coupled oscillators.

## Collective Motion Patterns {#S1}

Two equal masses connected by springs and coupled to one another can oscillate in many ways. Especially simple are motions where both displacements maintain a fixed ratio: **normal modes**. In linear systems these patterns correspond to eigenvectors of a coupling matrix. This can turn coupled differential equations into independent oscillator equations.

## The Eigenvalue Problem {#S2}

An eigenvector $\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0$ satisfies

$$
A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v.
$$

For a square matrix, candidate eigenvalues satisfy $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. For each eigenvalue, determine the null space of $A-\lambda I$. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. This is especially useful for linear oscillation systems with appropriate mass weighting. For the differential equation

$$
\ddot{\mathbf x}+A\mathbf x=\mathbf0
$$

try $\mathbf x(t)=\mathbf v\cos(\omega t+\phi)$; then $A\mathbf v=\omega^2\mathbf v$, so eigenvalues represent squared angular frequencies.

## Example: Two Identical Masses {#S3}

Consider the squared-frequency matrix in units of $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$:

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic equation $\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)^2-1$ yields $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=4$. Corresponding eigenvectors are $\mathbf v_1=(1,1)^\mathsf T$ and $\mathbf v_2=(1,-1)^\mathsf T$. In the first mode both masses move in phase ($\omega_1=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$); in the second they move in opposite directions ($\omega_2=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$). An arbitrary small oscillation is a superposition of the two modes.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the eigenvalues of $\operatorname{diag}(5,7)$.
2. Which mode, $(1,1)^\mathsf T$ or $(1,-1)^\mathsf T$, has higher frequency in the example?
3. Why does $\lambda<0$ in $\ddot x+\lambda x=0$ not describe stable harmonic motion?

### Solutions

1. $5$ and $7$, with the coordinate unit vectors.
2. The out-of-phase mode $(1,-1)^\mathsf T$, with $\omega=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
3. The equation becomes $\ddot x-|\lambda|x=0$, which has exponentially growing solutions.

## Assumptions Behind Diagonalisation {#S5}

This picture assumes linearisation around equilibrium. For different masses the mechanical problem generally reads $K\mathbf v=\omega^2 M\mathbf v$ with stiffness and mass matrices; our simple form is already mass-normalised. Negative eigenvalues of $A$ in $\ddot{\mathbf x}+A\mathbf x=0$ give exponential growth rather than stable oscillations. Damping and driving may require more complicated mode analysis. Degenerate eigenvalues do not select a unique eigenvector basis, and defective matrices need more than ordinary eigenvectors.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Normal modes are eigenvectors of the dynamical matrix; eigenvalues give squared angular frequencies. The next lesson extends differentiation to fields depending on several spatial variables.
