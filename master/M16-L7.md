# M16-L7 – Confidence Intervals and Significance Tests {#L7}

**Course area:** M16 – Bachelor: Experimental Physics and Measurement Analysis

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M13: probability; M16-L2: sample mean and variance. [Previous lesson](M16-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive confidence intervals for a mean, interpret p-values cautiously and separate test decisions from physical relevance.

## Estimation and Sampling Uncertainty {#S1}

A sample mean $\bar x$ estimates a population mean $\mu$. For independent normally distributed readings with unknown variance, $(\bar x-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)$ follows Student's t distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence procedure therefore constructs an interval about $\bar x$ while accounting for the estimate of $s$. Coverage refers to repeated samples under the assumed model; it is not a probability that an already computed fixed interval contains an unknown fixed parameter. Calibration uncertainties or biases are not removed by this statistical interval.

## Interval and Mean Test {#S2}

A two-sided confidence interval for the mean is

$$
\bar x\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$

To test $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$, use $t=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)$. At two-sided significance level $\alpha$, reject $H_0$ when $|t|$ exceeds the critical t quantile. Under the same model, $\mu_0$ lies outside the matching two-sided confidence interval exactly when the test rejects $H_0$. A *p-value* is a probability, calculated assuming $H_0$, of observing data or a test statistic at least as extreme as that obtained. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true or a measure of the physical effect size.

## Example: A Reference Value {#S3}

For $n=9$ independent observations, let $\bar x=10.20\,\mathrm V$ and $s=0.30\,\mathrm V$. For two-sided $95\%$ confidence with eight degrees of freedom, $t_{0.975,8}\approx2.306$. The interval half-width is

$$
h=2.306\frac{0.30}{\sqrt9}\,\mathrm V\approx0.231\,\mathrm V.
$$

The approximate interval is $[9.969,10.431]\,\mathrm V$ and contains the reference value $\mu_0=10.00\,\mathrm V$. Thus the matching two-sided test does not reject $H_0$ at $5\%$. The figure shows the position of the reference relative to an interval, not a probability distribution for the parameter.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute standard error for $s=0.4$ and $n=16$.
2. Interpret $p=0.03$ for a prespecified $5\%$ test.
3. Why does a 95% confidence interval not mean 'the parameter has 95% probability of lying inside it'?

### Solutions

1. $s/\sqrt n=0.10$.
2. The result is significant at 5% under the prespecified null model; this does not mean the null has 97% probability of being false.
3. The 95% describes long-run coverage of the procedure over hypothetical repeated samples.

## Interpretation and Pitfalls {#S5}

Failure to reject a hypothesis does not establish equality. Conversely, a tiny physically irrelevant effect can become statistically significant with a sufficiently large sample. This confidence interval assumes normality or suitable asymptotic conditions, independence and the correct mean model; it is not automatically interchangeable with metrological expanded uncertainty. Post hoc selection among many tests increases misleading findings. Prefer to specify the scientific question, decision rule, smallest relevant effect and analysis before collecting data. Report non-significant findings, uncertainty and model limitations as well.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Intervals and tests answer model-dependent questions and do not replace effect sizes or practical measurement decisions.
