# M7-L6 – Dispersion, Group Delay and Optical Pulses {#L6}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Wave packets, derivatives and frequency spectra. [Previous lesson](M7-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish phase and group velocities and compute group delay from the group index $n_g$.

## From Monochromatic Field to Pulse {#S1}

A real optical pulse does not contain just one frequency. It is a superposition of spectral components with different frequencies and, potentially, different wavenumbers. The phase of one harmonic component $\exp[i(kz-\omega t)]$ travels at $v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\omega/k$. By contrast, the envelope of a narrow-band packet follows group motion. When material response depends on frequency, these two velocities change differently. “Speed of light in a material” is therefore ambiguous without qualification: phase, group, energy and information speeds must be distinguished. The vacuum speed of light $c$ remains the fundamental limit for causal signaling.

## Wavenumber and Group Velocity {#S2}

For an isotropic medium with frequency-dependent phase index $n(\omega)$, $k(\omega)=n(\omega)\omega/c$. Linearizing near carrier frequency $\omega_0$ gives

$$
k(\omega)\approx k_0+k_1(\omega-\omega_0),\qquad k_1=\left.\frac{\mathrm dk}{\mathrm d\omega}\right|_{\omega_0}.
$$

The envelope is approximately delayed by $t=k_1L$, giving 

$$
v_g=\frac{1}{k_1}=\frac{c}{n_g},\qquad n_g=n+\omega\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm d\omega}.
$$

The second derivative $\beta_2=\mathrm d^2k/\mathrm d\omega^2$ specifies group-velocity dispersion. A finite-bandwidth pulse can broaden, or initially compress if suitably prechirped. The Fourier time-bandwidth relationship depends on precisely defined pulse shapes and width conventions.

## Worked Example: Fibre Delay {#S3}

An optical guide of length $L=1.00\,\mathrm{km}$ has group index $n_g=1.47$ at the carrier frequency. Group delay is $t_g=n_gL/c\approx1.47\times1000/(2.99792458\times10^8)\,\mathrm s\approx4.90\,\mathrm{\mu s}$. The same distance in vacuum takes about $3.34\,\mathrm{\mu s}$. This compares pulse group delay in a transparent, weakly distorted band; it does not describe the arrival of a new causal disturbance's leading edge. The figure compares a narrow input profile with an output broadened by different group delays of spectral components. A measurable group delay does not guarantee unchanged pulse shape.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $t_g$ for $L=100\,\mathrm m$ and $n_g=1.5$.
2. Which index sets the group delay, $n$ or $n_g$?
3. Why is “a short pulse is monochromatic” false?

### Solutions

1. $t_g=1.5\cdot100/c\approx0.500\,\mathrm{\mu s}$.
2. $n_g=n+\omega\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm d\omega$.
3. Short pulses require a broad frequency spectrum by the Fourier relation.

## Pulse Spreading and Signal Transport {#S5}

The elementary relation $v_g=(\mathrm dk/\mathrm d\omega)^{-1}$ is especially useful for narrowband, weakly absorbed pulses. In strongly absorbing or resonant regions, unusual and even negative group delays may occur without information traveling backward in time. In long fibres, modal dispersion, polarization and nonlinear effects also matter. A shorter temporal pulse has a broader spectrum and may therefore experience more frequency-dependent group delay. The sign of $\beta_2$ alone cannot predict the evolution of every pulse without its initial shape and phase profile.
