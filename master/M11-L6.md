# M11-L6 – Magnetic Resonance and Precession {#L6}

**Course area:** M11 – Bachelor: Foundations of Quantum Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Spin, magnetic moments, Zeeman splitting and oscillating fields; M6 and M11-L5. [Previous lesson](M11-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain Larmor frequency, resonance, and excitation of a spin ensemble by a transverse radio-frequency field.

## Orientation of a Magnetic Moment in a Field {#S1}

Magnetic resonance exploits the interaction between a magnetic moment and an external magnetic field. A spin ensemble in a constant field $B_0$ develops a preferred macroscopic magnetization direction. A weak oscillating field transverse to $B_0$ can drive transitions between Zeeman levels when its frequency matches their energy difference. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) examines nuclear spins, such as the hydrogen nucleus; electron-spin resonance probes electronic moments. Their gyromagnetic ratios differ, so they resonate at different frequencies for the same static magnetic field.

## Larmor Frequency and Resonance Condition {#S2}

A magnetic moment experiences torque $\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\mu\times\boldsymbol B$. A spin vector precesses in a homogeneous field with Larmor angular frequency

$$
\omega_0=|\gamma|B_0,\qquad f_0=\frac{|\gamma|B_0}{2\pi}.
$$

The sign of $\gamma$ sets the sense of precession, while its magnitude gives the frequency. The Zeeman separation for spin one-half is $\Delta E=\hbar|\gamma|B_0=hf_0$. In a rotating frame, a suitably polarized transverse field of amplitude $B_1$ at resonance can rotate magnetization through $\alpha=|\gamma|B_1t$. This ideal pulse-angle relation assumes resonant uniform driving without relaxation or field inhomogeneity.

## Worked Example: Protons at 1.5 Tesla {#S3}

For protons, $\gamma_p/(2\pi)\simeq42.58\,\mathrm{MHz/T}$. With $B_0=1.5\,\mathrm T$,

$$
f_0\simeq42.58\,\mathrm{MHz/T}\times1.5\,\mathrm T\simeq63.87\,\mathrm{MHz}.
$$

Excitation quantum energy is $hf_0\simeq2.64\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{eV}$. An ideal resonant $90^\circ$ pulse using an effective rotating field $B_1=10\,\mathrm{\mu T}$ would take $t_{90}=\pi/(2|\gamma_p|B_1)$, or approximately $0.587\,\mathrm{ms}$. The figure labels $B_0$, a precession cone and the transverse drive. Real pulses require careful control of waveform, polarization and calibration; a linearly polarized wave has two rotating components.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute proton $f_0$ at $B_0=3.0\,\mathrm T$.
2. What happens to $f_0$ if $B_0$ is halved?
3. Explain the difference between $T_2$ and $T_2^*$.

### Solutions

1. $f_0\simeq127.74\,\mathrm{MHz}$.
2. Larmor frequency halves in the linear model.
3. $T_2$ describes intrinsic transverse coherence loss; $T_2^*$ additionally includes static field inhomogeneity and is usually shorter.

## Relaxation and the Difference between NMR and MRI {#S5}

After excitation, longitudinal magnetization returns toward equilibrium with a $T_1$ time constant; transverse phase coherence decays with $T_2$. Measured $T_2^*$ also includes static field inhomogeneity. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses field gradients for spatial encoding, whereas NMR spectroscopy uses frequency shifts to probe chemical environments. Going from resonance to an image therefore requires Fourier reconstruction, sequence design and safety controls. The spin-half model developed here explains an idealized resonance mechanism, not every feature of clinical MRI systems.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Magnetic resonance links quantized Zeeman energies with moment precession and resonant radio-frequency excitation.
