# M4-L7 – Hydrostatics, Continuity and Bernoulli’s Equation {#L7}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Conservation of energy and elementary field quantities. [Previous lesson](M4-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply hydrostatic pressure, mass conservation and Bernoulli’s equation to ideal flows.

## Pressure at Rest and in Motion {#S1}

Fluids cannot sustain static shear stresses over long times as elastic solids do; they flow. Density $\rho=m/V$ and pressure $p=F_\perp/A$ are basic state variables. In a resting liquid of constant density, pressure rises with depth: $p(h)=p_0+\rho gh$. This hydrostatic contribution does not depend on container shape if the fluid is homogeneous and in equilibrium. Motion introduces velocity fields and transport of matter. Analysis requires an explicit system boundary and assumptions about compressibility and friction.

## Mass Flow and Energy Balance {#S2}

For steady incompressible flow without branches between two pipe cross-sections, mass conservation gives $A_1v_1=A_2v_2$. Along a streamline of steady, inviscid flow at constant density, Bernoulli’s equation reads

$$
p+\tfrac12\rho v^2+\rho gz=\mathrm{const}.
$$

Here $p$ is static pressure, $\tfrac12\rho v^2$ kinetic energy per volume and $\rho gz$ gravitational potential energy per volume. Under more restrictive irrotational conditions, the constant can be shared between streamlines. The relation must not be applied unchanged across pumps, turbines, viscous losses or shocks.

## Worked Example: Flow Through a Constriction {#S3}

Water flows steadily and without friction through a horizontal pipe in an idealisation. Cross-sectional areas are $A_1=8.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and $A_2=4.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, with initial speed $v_1=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Continuity requires $v_2=A_1v_1/A_2=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Using $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ at equal height yields

$$
p_1-p_2=\tfrac12\rho(v_2^2-v_1^2)=6000\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$

In the constriction, speed increases while static pressure drops in the ideal case. The figure depicts the two sections, streamlines and the velocity–pressure comparison.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What additional hydrostatic pressure is produced by $3.0\,\mathrm m$ of water at $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$?
2. A pipe's cross-sectional area is halved; how does $v$ change for steady incompressible flow?
3. Why can the actual pressure loss exceed the ideal value in the example?

### Solutions

1. $\Delta p=\rho gh=1000\cdot9.81\cdot3=29\,430\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
2. Speed doubles if there is no branch flow.
3. Friction, vortex formation and local shape losses dissipate mechanical energy.

## When the Equation Fails {#S5}

Bernoulli’s equation describes an **idealised flow**, not a universal pressure rule for every real pipe system. Friction creates additional pressure losses; strong acceleration may accompany cavitation, flow separation or turbulence. For gases, incompressibility may be inappropriate. Pressure values must distinguish absolute pressure from gauge pressure. The equation is most useful when its domain of validity is checked explicitly.
