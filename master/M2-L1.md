# M2-L1 – Taylor Expansions and Approximations {#L1}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Derivatives, powers and trigonometric functions; foundations from M1. [Previous lesson](M1-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate Taylor polynomials, estimate errors and justify small-angle approximations.

## Why a Polynomial? {#S1}

Complicated force laws and wave functions often become tractable when we make a controlled approximation. Near a known operating point $a$, we replace a sufficiently smooth function $f(x)$ locally by a polynomial. This is not a global replacement: accuracy depends on $|x-a|$ and the derivatives. For example, the pendulum model from M1 uses $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ only for small angles in **radians**. An angle of $10^\circ$ must therefore be converted before substitution for $\theta$.

## Taylor Formula and Remainder {#S2}

The Taylor polynomial of order $n$ about $a$ is

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

For a function with $n+1$ continuous derivatives, the Lagrange remainder can, under the usual assumptions, be expressed as

$$
f(x)-P_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}
$$

for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$. If $|f^{(n+1)}|\le M$ throughout that interval, then $|f-P_n|\le M|x-a|^{n+1}/(n+1)!$. This is a controlled error bound, not just an ellipsis. An expansion about $a=0$ is called a Maclaurin expansion.

## Example: Sine and Small Angles {#S3}

The derivatives cycle through $\sin,\cos,-\sin,-\cos$, yielding

$$
\sin\theta=\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots.
$$

At $\theta=0.20\,\mathrm{rad}$, the linear approximation is $0.200000$, the cubic is $0.198667$, and the actual sine is approximately $0.198669$. The linear error is about $0.001331$, or $0.67\,\%$ of the actual value. Taylor's theorem with $n=2$ gives the bound $|\sin\theta-\theta|\le|\theta|^3/6$. The cubic approximation has a far smaller remainder at this angle (here $\le|\theta|^5/120$). Specify the angle and required accuracy before simplifying a model.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the second-order Taylor polynomial of $e^x$ at $0$ and evaluate it at $x=0.1$.
2. Use the small-angle model to estimate $\sin(0.10)$ and give an absolute error bound.
3. Why is $\sin(20)$ unsuitable when the intended angle is twenty degrees?

### Solutions

1. $P_2(x)=1+x+x^2/2$, so $P_2(0.1)=1.105$; the actual value is about $1.105171$.
2. $0.10$; since $|\sin\theta-\theta|\le|\theta|^3/6$, the absolute error is at most $0.000167$.
3. Without conversion, the function interprets $20$ as radians. The correct input is $20^\circ=\pi/9\,\mathrm{rad}$; the required accuracy must also be assessed.

## Local Validity and Dimensions {#S5}

A formal expansion is not automatically a good approximation. Increasing polynomial order may improve accuracy over a restricted interval, while numerical evaluation can eventually suffer cancellation. For functions with singularities, the domain matters. Arguments of $\sin$, $\exp$ and $\ln$ must be dimensionless; every term in a Taylor polynomial must share the units of $f$. In mechanics, linearised equations are especially useful near stable equilibria.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Taylor polynomials describe local functional changes; the remainder bounds approximation error. Next, complex numbers will simplify harmonic signals.
