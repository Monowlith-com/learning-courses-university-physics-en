# M24-L3 – Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Larmor Frequency and k-Space {#L3}

**Course area:** M24 – Bachelor: Medical Physics and Imaging

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M6: magnetic fields; M11: nuclear spin; M8: Fourier transformation. [Previous lesson](M24-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate Larmor frequency and spatial encoding for an ideal MRI sequence and distinguish k-space samples from image pixels.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

Magnetic resonance imaging detects signals from nuclear spins in a strong static magnetic field. Unlike X-rays and CT, it does not use ionising X-ray radiation. A static field establishes magnetisation, and an appropriate radio-frequency pulse can tip it. While spins precess and relax, their changing magnetisation induces a receive signal in a coil.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

For a spin with gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma$, the Larmor angular frequency is $\omega_0=|\gamma|B_0$. Ordinary frequency in hertz differs by a factor of $2\pi$. A time-dependent magnetic field gradient $\mathbf G$ creates position-dependent phase $\phi(\mathbf r,t)=\gamma\int_0^t\mathbf G(\tau)\!\cdot\!\mathbf r\,d\tau$. Under a suitable sequence definition, this provides Fourier spatial encoding represented by the k-space vector $\mathbf k(t)$. The sign depends on the convention for $\gamma$ and the complex receive signal.

$$
\omega_0=|\gamma|B_0,\qquad f_0=\frac{|\gamma|B_0}{2\pi},\qquad \mathbf k(t)=\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\int_0^t\mathbf G(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

An ideal 2D signal is $s(\mathbf k)=\int\rho(\mathbf r)e^{-i2\pi\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^2r$, where $\rho$ is an effective spin- and sequence-weighted signal density. Real signals depend on T1 and T2 relaxation, susceptibility, motion, coil profiles and noise. k-space represents spatial frequency, not an anatomical image; a suitable inverse Fourier transform produces the simplified spatial image.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

For protons, $|\gamma|/(2\pi)\approx42.58\,\mathrm{MHz/T}$. At $B_0=3.0\,\mathrm T$, $f_0\approx127.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$. A constant gradient $G=1.0\,\mathrm{mT/m}$ maintained for $1.0\,\mathrm{ms}$ gives $k\approx42.6\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ along the relevant axis. ## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find proton $f_0$ at $B_0=1.5\,\mathrm T$.
2. What is $k$ for $G=2\,\mathrm{mT/m}$ applied for $\Delta t=1\,\mathrm{ms}$?
3. Why is angular frequency $\omega_0$ in $\mathrm{rad/s}$ not numerically equal to $f_0$ in hertz?

### Solutions

1. $f_0\approx42.58\cdot1.5=63.9\,\mathrm{MHz}$.
2. $k\approx(42.58\cdot10^6)(0.002\cdot0.001)\approx85.2\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.
3. One revolution corresponds to $2\pi$ radians: $\omega_0=2\pi f_0$.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The Fourier model assumes an ideal sequence, uniform reception and corrected background field inhomogeneity. These calculations do not establish patient safety limits or clinical image performance.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

MRI encodes spatial information with controlled gradient-induced phase. The factor of 2π is essential to interpreting k-space.
