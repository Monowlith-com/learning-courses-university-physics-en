# M4-L6 – Sound Waves and Acoustic Resonance {#L6}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Waves, superposition and boundary conditions. [Previous lesson](M4-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Describe sound as a pressure wave and calculate sound pressure level and air-column resonances.

## Compression Waves in Fluids {#S1}

**Sound** in gases and liquids is a mechanical longitudinal wave: small variations in density and pressure propagate through compression and rarefaction. Individual particles oscillate about their mean positions while the pressure disturbance travels onward. Mechanical sound requires a medium and cannot propagate through an ideal vacuum. Its speed depends on the elastic response and density of the medium. In air, temperature, composition and humidity matter.

## Sound Speed and Logarithmic Level {#S2}

For small pressure disturbances in a fluid, sound speed can be modelled by $c=\sqrt{B/\rho}$, where $B$ is the appropriate bulk modulus and $\rho$ the mass density. In an ideal gas under approximately adiabatic conditions, $c=\sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$ with heat-capacity ratio $\gamma$, universal gas constant $R$, temperature $T$ and molar mass $M$. Acoustic intensity is power per area. Its level is

$$
L_I=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)\,\mathrm{dB}.
$$

Sound pressure level, at a common reference impedance, instead uses $L_p=20\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{rms}}/p_0)\,\mathrm{dB}$. Sound pressure and acoustic intensity are not interchangeable.

## Worked Example: Open–Open Pipe {#S3}

Consider an ideal pipe open at both ends, length $L=0.85\,\mathrm m$. With assumed sound speed $c=340\,\mathrm{m/s}$, pressure nodes occur at both open ends. Allowed frequencies are $f_n=nc/(2L)$, hence $f_1=200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $f_2=400\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The figure shows the first and second spatial **pressure mode shapes**; these must not be confused with air-particle displacement patterns. Real openings require end corrections, so the effective resonator length differs slightly.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the fundamental frequency of an open–open pipe with $L=0.50\,\mathrm m$ and $c=340\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
2. By how many dB does intensity level rise when $I$ increases by a factor of 100?
3. What is the difference between a pressure node and a displacement node?

### Solutions

1. $f_1=340/(2\cdot0.50)=340\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
2. $\Delta L_I=10\log_{10}(100)=20\,\mathrm{dB}$.
3. A pressure node has zero pressure fluctuation; a displacement node has zero particle displacement. In ideal pipe modes they do not occur at the same position.

## Hearing, Intensity and Limits {#S5}

A rise of $10\,\mathrm{dB}$ in intensity level corresponds to ten times the intensity, not ten times the perceived loudness. Human hearing weights frequencies differently; scales such as dB(A) introduce additional frequency-dependent weighting. A one-dimensional pipe model is approximate and requires a geometry and frequency range compatible with plane waves. In rooms, reflections, absorption and multiple modes shape the sound field. Levels should therefore be reported with their reference quantity and measurement conditions.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Acoustic resonators connect waves and material properties. Fluid mechanics additionally describes flow and pressure.
