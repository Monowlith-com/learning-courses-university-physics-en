# M6-L4 – Electric Current, Resistance and Circuits {#L4}

**Course area:** M6 – Bachelor: Electricity and Magnetism

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Electric voltage, energy conservation and algebra. [Previous lesson](M6-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Define current and resistance; analyse simple series/parallel circuits and apply Kirchhoff equations.

## Current and Resistance {#S1}

Electric current is the net charge crossing a section per time, $I=\mathrm dQ/\mathrm dt$. Its sign depends on the chosen current direction; conventional current follows positive charge even though electrons in metals drift the other way. Current density generalises the description locally as $I=\int_A\boldsymbol J\cdot\mathrm d\boldsymbol A$. In an ohmic conductor over an appropriate temperature range $U=RI$. For conductor length $\ell$, cross-sectional area $A$ and resistivity $\rho$, $R=\rho\ell/A$. Resistance in ohms is the voltage-to-current ratio, not a measure of charge quantity. The power of a passive resistor is $P=UI=I^2R=U^2/R$, mostly dissipated as heat.

## Kirchhoff’s Laws {#S2}

Kirchhoff’s **junction rule** expresses charge conservation in a quasistationary network: the signed sum of currents at a node vanishes, $\sum I_k=0$. The **loop rule** balances oriented voltage changes around a closed circuit path, $\sum U_k=0$. This simple form must not ignore a time-varying magnetic flux linking the loop. Ideal series resistors have $R_\mathrm s=\sum_iR_i$ with common current; in parallel $1/R_\mathrm p=\sum_i1/R_i$ with common voltage. An ideal ammeter goes in series and has zero resistance; an ideal voltmeter goes in parallel and draws zero current. Practical instruments perturb a circuit slightly.

## Worked Example: Branched Circuit {#S3}

An ideal $12.0\,\mathrm V$ source supplies a $2.0\,\Omega$ resistor in series with two parallel resistors of $6.0\,\Omega$ and $3.0\,\Omega$. First,

$$
R_\parallel=\left(\frac{1}{6.0}+\frac{1}{3.0}\right)^{-1}\!\Omega=2.0\,\Omega,\qquad R_\mathrm{total}=4.0\,\Omega.
$$

Thus $I_\mathrm{total}=12/4=3.0\,\mathrm A$. The series resistor drops $U_\mathrm s=6.0\,\mathrm V$, leaving the same $6.0\,\mathrm V$ across both branches. Currents are $I_6=1.0\,\mathrm A$ and $I_3=2.0\,\mathrm A$, adding to $3.0\,\mathrm A$. Total power $P=UI=36\,\mathrm W$ agrees with the sum of resistor powers $18+6+12=36\,\mathrm W$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Three resistors of $2\,\Omega$, $3\,\Omega$ and $6\,\Omega$ are connected in parallel. Find $R_\mathrm p$.
2. A $4\,\Omega$ resistor carries $2\,\mathrm A$. Find $U$ and $P$.
3. Why should an ideal voltmeter have infinite resistance?

### Solutions

1. $1/R_\mathrm p=1/2+1/3+1/6=1\,\Omega^{-1}$, so $R_\mathrm p=1\,\Omega$.
2. $U=RI=8\,\mathrm V$ and $P=I^2R=16\,\mathrm W$.
3. It should draw negligible current and thus perturb the circuit as little as possible.

## Measurement and Model Limits {#S5}

The junction rule follows from the complete charge balance; displacement current can matter in strongly time-dependent fields. The usual circuit loop rule is a quasistatic approximation and must not silently omit distributed induced electromotive forces. Nonlinear devices such as diodes and temperature-dependent resistors cannot be described by a single constant $R$. Real measuring instruments require appropriate protection, rating and safe operating limits. Work on mains and high-current sources must follow suitable safety procedures. The example treats ideal DC components only.
