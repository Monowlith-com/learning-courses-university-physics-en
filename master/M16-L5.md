# M16-L5 – Least-Squares Fitting, Residuals and Model Adequacy {#L5}

**Course area:** M16 – Bachelor: Experimental Physics and Measurement Analysis

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M2: linear algebra; M16-L3–L4: uncertainty and covariance. [Previous lesson](M16-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate and weight a linear least-squares fit and assess residuals appropriately.

## Model and Residual {#S1}

Observations with inputs $x_i$ and outputs $y_i$ are modeled by $f(x_i;\boldsymbol\beta)$. A residual $r_i=y_i-f(x_i;\boldsymbol\beta)$ is not automatically the actual measurement error. Least squares selects model parameters that minimize a sum of squared residuals. For a straight line $f(x)=a+bx$, ordinary least squares minimizes

$$
S(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[y_i-a-bx_i]^2.
$$

Provided the $x_i$ vary, the normal equations give $b=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ and $a=\bar y-b\bar x$. This model treats uncertainties in $x_i$ as negligible.

## Ordinary and Weighted Fitting {#S2}

When independent observations have known variances $\sigma_i^2$, weighted least squares often minimizes

$$
\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{[y_i-f(x_i;\boldsymbol\beta)]^2}{\sigma_i^2}.
$$

For a linear model with design matrix $X$, outcome vector $\mathbf y$ and positive weight matrix $W$, the solution is $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X^{\mathsf T}WX)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}W\mathbf y$, provided the inverse exists. For correlated observations the inverse covariance matrix $C^{-1}$ replaces diagonal weights. With independent Gaussian errors of *known* $\sigma_i$, a correct linear model and $p$ fitted parameters, minimized $\chi^2$ has a chi-squared distribution with $n-p$ degrees of freedom under these assumptions. This does not automatically hold for data-dependent estimated weights or after selecting observations.

## Example: A Linear Sensor Model {#S3}

For points $(x,y)=(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)$, $\bar x=1$ and $\bar y=5/3$. The normal equations yield

$$
b=\frac{1}{2},\qquad a=\frac{7}{6},\qquad S_{\min}=\frac{1}{6}.
$$

Predicted values are $7/6,5/3,13/6$, with residuals $-1/6,1/3,-1/6$. The residuals sum to zero because a free intercept was fitted. The diagram shows observations above and below the fitted line; vertical distances indicate residuals. Three observations and two parameters leave only one residual degree of freedom; meaningful model criticism requires more independent information.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the line through $(0,2)$ and $(2,6)$.
2. What weights correspond to independent $\sigma_1=1$ and $\sigma_2=2$?
3. How many residual degrees of freedom remain for $n=12$ and $p=3$?

### Solutions

1. $a=2$, $b=2$.
2. Relative weights $w_1=1$, $w_2=1/4$.
3. $n-p=9$, assuming full model rank.

## Model Criticism and Degrees of Freedom {#S5}

A small $\chi^2/(n-p)$ is not a universal quality certificate: it can result from overestimated uncertainties, correlated readings or overfitting. Residual curvature can suggest an inadequate model. Inspect residuals versus time and input. Significant uncertainty in both coordinates may require errors-in-variables fitting. Extrapolation outside measured inputs can be much less reliable than interpolation. Mechanically deleting inconvenient outliers without documented measurement reasons is not a defensible model improvement.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Fitting estimates parameters; residual structure also matters for model validity and uncertainty statements.
