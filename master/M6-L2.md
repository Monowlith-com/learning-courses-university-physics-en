# M6-L2 – Gauss’s Law and Electric Potential {#L2}

**Course area:** M6 – Bachelor: Electricity and Magnetism

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Superposition and elementary surface integrals; Coulomb field. [Previous lesson](M6-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate electric flux, identify useful Gauss-law symmetries and connect field to potential.

## Electric Flux {#S1}

Electric flux $\Phi_E$ measures the field component crossing an oriented surface. An infinitesimal area element is $\mathrm d\boldsymbol A=\boldsymbol n\,\mathrm dA$ with a chosen normal direction. In general $\Phi_E=\int_A\boldsymbol E\cdot\mathrm d\boldsymbol A$. For a uniform field normal to a planar area, $\Phi_E=EA$; when the angle between field and normal is $\theta$, it is $EA\cos\theta$. On a **closed** surface the normal is outward by convention. A tangential field contributes no local flux. Electric flux is neither a particle flow nor an energy but an integral of the field over area; its SI unit is $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$.

## Gauss’s Law and Potential {#S2}

Gauss’s law for electrostatics in vacuum is

$$
\oint_{\partial V}\boldsymbol E\cdot\mathrm d\boldsymbol A=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0},\qquad\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

Only the **net enclosed charge** determines the net flux. External charges may change $\boldsymbol E$ locally on the Gaussian surface, yet do not change its total flux. For spherical, cylindrical or planar symmetry, the law can determine $E$ directly because field direction and magnitude are constrained by symmetry. The scalar potential $V$ is potential energy per unit charge; electrostatics gives

$$
\boldsymbol E=-\nabla V,\qquad V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\boldsymbol E\cdot\mathrm d\boldsymbol l.
$$

Voltage is a potential difference, $U_{AB}=V(A)-V(B)$ for the stated orientation. Differences, not absolute potential values, are observable.

## Worked Example: Charged Sphere {#S3}

An isolated spherically symmetric charge distribution contains $Q=+4.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ inside a radius $R=0.05\,\mathrm m$. At $r=0.20\,\mathrm m$, spherical symmetry makes $E(4\pi r^2)=Q/\varepsilon_0$. Hence

$$
E(r)=k_e\frac{Q}{r^2}\approx899\,\mathrm{N/C},\qquad V(r)=k_e\frac{Q}{r}\approx180\,\mathrm V,
$$

with the reference choice $V(\infty)=0$. For $Q>0$ the radial field points outward. The illustration shows a spherical Gaussian surface with outward normals. **Inside** the charge distribution the total charge alone does not determine a formula; the spatial density and additional symmetry information are required.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A $200\,\mathrm{N/C}$ uniform field crosses a plane of area $0.50\,\mathrm{m^2}$ at $60^\circ$ to its normal. Find $\Phi_E$.
2. A Gaussian surface encloses $+2.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $-1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. What charge determines net flux?
3. Why can an external point charge change the local field but not the total flux?

### Solutions

1. $\Phi_E=200\cdot0.50\cdot\cos60^\circ=50\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$.
2. $Q_\mathrm{enclosed}=+1.0\,\mathrm{nC}$.
3. Field contributions from outside enter and leave the closed surface; their signed flux contributions cancel.

## Symmetry and Potential Reference {#S5}

Gauss’s law always holds but simplifies field calculations only when symmetry is adequate. A surface of arbitrary shape may yield total flux without giving the local field strength. In electrostatics a scalar potential is available when $\nabla\times\boldsymbol E=\boldsymbol0$, subject to ordinary domain conditions. With time-varying magnetic fields the electric field is generally not conservative and Faraday’s law must be included. Changing the potential reference shifts numerical potential values without altering the field or voltage differences.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Gauss’s law connects field structure to enclosed charge; potential offers a scalar account of work. Capacitors are the next application.
