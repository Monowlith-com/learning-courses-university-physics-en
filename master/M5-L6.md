# M5-L6 – Thermodynamic Potentials and Maxwell Relations {#L6}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Partial derivatives, differentials, first and second laws. [Previous lesson](M5-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain Legendre transforms and correctly derive two Maxwell relations from state functions.

## Natural State Variables {#S1}

For a simple homogeneous compressible closed system of fixed particle number with only $pV$ work, the thermodynamic fundamental relation for reversible changes is $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$. Thus entropy $S$ and volume $V$ are natural variables of internal energy $U(S,V)$. The minus sign in front of $p\,dV$ follows from the chosen first-law work convention. In experiments, temperature and pressure are often easier to hold constant than entropy and volume. A Legendre transform replaces an independent variable by its conjugate derivative. It changes the description of a state without changing the underlying physical system.

## Potentials by Changing Variables {#S2}

Define enthalpy $H=U+pV$, Helmholtz energy $F=U-TS$ and Gibbs energy $G=U+pV-TS$. At fixed particle number,

$$
\begin{aligned}
dU&=T\,dS-p\,dV,\\
dH&=T\,dS+V\,dp,\\
dF&=-S\,dT-p\,dV,\\
dG&=-S\,dT+V\,dp.
\end{aligned}
$$

For example $dF=dU-T\,dS-S\,dT$, so $dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV$. Since mixed second derivatives of smooth state functions agree, Maxwell relations follow, including

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\right)_T=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.
$$

The first follows from $F(T,V)$ and the second from $G(T,p)$.

## Worked Example: Ideal Gas Response {#S3}

For an ideal gas $p=nRT/V$, hence the right side of the first Maxwell relation at fixed $V$ equals $nR/V$. The earlier entropy change $\Delta S=nC_V\ln(T_2/T_1)+nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$ gives at fixed $T$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T=\frac{nR}{V}=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$

The second relation can also be tested: $V=nRT/p$ gives $-(\partial V/\partial T)_p=-nR/p$. In variables $T,p$, the entropy formula likewise gives $(\partial S/\partial p)_T=-nR/p$. The illustration maps each of the four potentials to its natural variables and differential terms.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. State the natural variables of $H$ and $G$ at fixed $N$.
2. Derive $dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV$ from $F=U-TS$.
3. Calculate $(\partial p/\partial T)_V$ for an ideal gas with $n=2.00\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $V=0.050\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

### Solutions

1. $H(S,p)$ and $G(T,p)$.
2. $dF=dU-T\,dS-S\,dT$; substitute $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$ to cancel the $T\,dS$ terms.
3. $nR/V=2\cdot8.314/0.050\approx333\,\mathrm{Pa/K}$.

## Assumptions and Stability {#S5}

If particle number varies, the chemical-potential term $\mu\,dN$ enters; mixtures require a sum over components. Additional work modes, such as magnetic polarisation, change the independent variables and the relevant Legendre transform. Maxwell relations apply locally within smooth phase regions; first-order phase transitions may introduce discontinuities in derivatives. Under appropriate boundary conditions, equilibrium minimises $F$ at fixed $T,V$ and $G$ at fixed $T,p$. These claims require matching reservoirs and allowed processes; saying “energy always becomes minimal” without qualification would be incorrect.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Thermodynamic potentials associate equilibrium conditions with suitable control variables. Their derivatives enable indirect measurements and underpin phase equilibrium.
