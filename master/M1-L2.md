# M1-L2 – SI Units and Dimensional Analysis {#L2}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor: Foundations of Physics

**Level:** Bachelor · Introductory

**Prerequisites:** Powers, fractions; modelling from the first lesson. [Previous lesson](M1-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Convert quantity values, identify SI base units and test equations for dimensional consistency.

## Number and Unit {#S1}

A distance of 120 has no unambiguous physical value without a unit. The statement $120\,\mathrm{cm}=1.20\,\mathrm m$ describes the same length using two numerical values. A physical quantity $Q$ is expressed as a numerical value $\{Q\}$ multiplied by its unit $[Q]$:

$$
Q=\{Q\}[Q].
$$

In this lesson, $\operatorname{dim}Q$ denotes dimension, so it is not confused with the particular unit chosen.

## The Seven Base Units {#S2}

The International System of Units (SI) has seven base units:

| Base quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| time | second | s |
| length | metre | m |
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| electric current | ampere | A |
| thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| amount of substance | mole | mol |
| luminous intensity | candela | cd |

Derived units are formed by products and quotients, for example $1\,\mathrm N=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}$ and $1\,\mathrm J=1\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The SI defines its units through fixed numerical values of seven constants. Examples of exact values are $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $h=6.62607015\cdot10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $k_\mathrm B=1.380649\cdot10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$. Defining a unit is different from realising it with an instrument; an actual measurement has uncertainty.

## Calculating with Units and Dimensions {#S3}

Units are treated like factors. For example,

$$
72\,\frac{\mathrm{km}}{\mathrm h}=72\frac{1000\,\mathrm m}{3600\,\mathrm s}=20\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

Velocity has dimension $LT^{-1}$ and acceleration $LT^{-2}$. An equation must have the same dimension in all added terms. This verifies dimensional consistency of $x=x_0+v_0t+at^2/2$ for constant acceleration, since every term has dimension $L$.

For the period of an idealised pendulum, assume $T_p=C\ell^\alpha g^\beta$ with dimensionless factor $C$. Comparing dimensions gives $L^{\alpha+\beta}T^{-2\beta}=T$. Hence $\beta=-1/2$ and $\alpha=1/2$. Dimensional analysis gives the square-root dependence but not $C=2\pi$; dynamics is required for that.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Convert $3.6\,\mathrm{ms}$ into seconds.
2. What is the dimension of $E=mv^2/2$?
3. Convert $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ into kelvin. What corresponds to a temperature difference of $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$?

### Solutions

1. $3.6\cdot10^{-3}\,\mathrm s$.
2. $ML^2T^{-2}$, SI unit joule.
3. $293.15\,\mathrm K$; the difference is $20\,\mathrm K$. The zero-point offset of $273.15$ applies to temperature values, not to temperature differences.

## Limits of Dimensional Checks {#S5}

Matching dimensions do not guarantee a correct equation. Torque and energy have the same SI dimension but are distinct physical quantities. Angles and pure ratios are dimensionless; the argument of an exponential, logarithmic or trigonometric function must be dimensionless. Take care with prefixes: $1\,\mathrm{cm^2}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, not $10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}$.

SI units make quantity values unambiguous, and dimensional analysis exposes many formula errors. Estimates in the next lesson combine both tools.
