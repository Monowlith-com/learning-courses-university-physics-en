# M8-L2 – Linear Differential-Equation Systems and Eigenmodes {#L2}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Linear algebra, eigenvalues, derivatives and first-order equations. [Previous lesson](M8-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain the matrix exponential as a solution and evaluate stable eigenmodes separately.

## Why Physical Systems Are Coupled {#S1}

A multiple-degree-of-freedom spring system, an electrical network or a kinetic reaction model has several coupled state variables. Collect them in $\boldsymbol{x}(t)$ and use a linear time-invariant model $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}(t)$. Matrix $A$ describes coupling and $\boldsymbol{b}$ describes external drive. Unlike a single scalar equation, changing one component can alter the time derivative of another. An initial-value vector supplies all initial state information. If all state components use comparable units, the entries of $A$ have inverse-time units; for mixed physical variables each row must be checked separately for dimensional consistency.

## Matrix Exponential and Eigenvectors {#S2}

For constant $A$, the convergent series $e^{At}=I+At+(At)^2/2!+\cdots$ defines the matrix exponential. Differentiation gives $\mathrm d(e^{At})/\mathrm dt=Ae^{At}$. The unforced initial-value solution is therefore

$$
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=e^{A(t-t_0)}\boldsymbol{x}(t_0).
$$

If $A$ has enough independent eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_j$ satisfying $A\boldsymbol{v}_j=\lambda_j\boldsymbol{v}_j$, the initial state can be decomposed into modes: $\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\sum_j c_j e^{\lambda_j(t-t_0)}\boldsymbol{v}_j$. Negative real parts of all eigenvalues imply asymptotic decay of the homogeneous system; purely imaginary eigenvalues alone do not demonstrate damping.

## Example: Two Decay Modes {#S3}

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-3\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}(0)=(2,1)^\mathsf{T}$ the modes are

$$
x_1(t)=2e^{-t},\qquad x_2(t)=e^{-3t}.
$$

At one second, $x_1\approx0.7358$ and $x_2\approx0.04979$. The second component decays substantially faster even though both approach zero. The figure compares the two time scales. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1=-1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and $\lambda_2=-3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. A measured physical signal that combines both modes need not resemble a single exponential decay. Projection onto eigenvectors separates the components when the matrix can be diagonalized.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Solve $\dot x=-2x$ with $x(0)=3$.
2. What mode time constant corresponds to eigenvalue $-3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$?
3. Can every real matrix be diagonalized using real eigenvectors?

### Solutions

1. $x(t)=3e^{-2t}$.
2. $\tau=1/3\,\mathrm s$.
3. No. Defective matrices may lack sufficient independent eigenvectors, and complex eigenvalues can also occur.

## Limits of Modal Analysis {#S5}

Not every matrix is diagonalizable. A Jordan block can generate terms such as $t e^{\lambda t}$, and nonnormal matrices can exhibit transient growth even when their eigenvalues are stable. Time-dependent coefficients are not generally solved by $e^{\int A(t)\,\mathrm dt}$, because matrices at distinct times need not commute. An external drive requires a variation-of-constants or Duhamel formula rather than homogeneous eigenmodes alone. Mechanical state vectors may mix positions and velocities; statements about the stability of equations obtained by linearization initially apply only near the equilibrium around which the approximation was constructed.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The matrix exponential generalizes scalar exponential dynamics to coupled linear systems. Periodic signals additionally invite a spectral description.
