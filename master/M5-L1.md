# M5-L1 – Temperature, Equilibrium and Equations of State {#L1}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** SI units, elementary algebra, and pressure as force per area. [Previous lesson](M4-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain the zeroth law, distinguish kelvin and Celsius, and calculate ideal-gas states.

## State and Thermal Equilibrium {#S1}

Thermodynamics describes macroscopic states using measurable quantities such as pressure $p$, volume $V$, temperature $T$ and amount of substance $n$. In **thermal equilibrium** two systems allowed to exchange only heat have no net transfer of energy as heat on average. The zeroth law makes equilibrium transitive: if A is in thermal equilibrium with C and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is the basis for comparing states with a thermometer. Temperature is a state variable, not the total thermal energy stored in a body. A tiny piece of hot metal can have less internal energy than a large volume of moderately warm water.

## Temperature Scales and Gas Equation {#S2}

Absolute temperature is expressed in kelvin. A Celsius reading becomes a kelvin reading by adding $273.15$; the size of one-degree temperature differences is equal on both scales. For a sufficiently dilute gas whose particle interactions can be neglected within the regime studied, the ideal equation of state is

$$
pV=nRT=Nk_{\mathrm B}T,\qquad R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}.
$$

$n$ is measured in moles, whereas $N$ is the number of particles. The gas constant is $R\approx8.314\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$. For a fixed amount of gas, $pV/T=\mathrm{const}$ between equilibrium states. A path in a $p$-$V$ diagram requires further information, such as whether $T$, $p$ or $V$ remains constant during the change. The equation specifies states, not the rate of a process.

## Worked Example: Confined Gas {#S3}

A closed rigid vessel contains $n=2.00\,\mathrm{mol}$ of an approximately ideal gas at $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$ and $p_1=1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Its volume is

$$
V=\frac{nRT_1}{p_1}=\frac{2.00\cdot8.314\cdot300}{10^5}\approx0.0499\,\mathrm{m^3}.
$$

If it is heated to $T_2=360\,\mathrm K$ without changing volume or amount of gas, $p_2=p_1T_2/T_1=1.20\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. A temperature of $360\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ must not be substituted for $360\,\mathrm K$. The figure depicts two systems in thermal contact: a common final temperature is expected only after sufficient time. The actual final temperature depends on their heat capacities.

*The zeroth law identifies equilibrium but does not calculate the final temperature.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Convert $25.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ to kelvin.
2. Find the pressure ratio when a rigid vessel is heated from $250$ to $300\,\mathrm K$.
3. Why does the zeroth law not determine the final temperature of two bodies placed in contact?

### Solutions

1. $T=25.0+273.15=298.15\,\mathrm K$.
2. $p_2/p_1=T_2/T_1=300/250=1.20$.
3. It defines the equilibrium relation; the energy balance and heat capacities determine the final temperature.

## Limits of the Models {#S5}

An ideal gas is a model, not a particular substance. At high density and near phase changes, attraction, molecular size and condensation affect behaviour. The van der Waals model qualitatively includes excluded volume and a pressure correction, with material-dependent constants. The notion of a well-defined temperature at every instant also assumes sufficient local relaxation. During rapid changes or strong flows, a system may be out of equilibrium. Therefore its boundary, equilibrium approximation and fixed variables should be stated before using $pV=nRT$.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Temperature defines thermal equilibrium, and equations of state connect measurable variables within their model assumptions. We next examine a microscopic account of gases.
