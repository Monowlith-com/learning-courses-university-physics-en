# M4-L3 – Coupled Oscillators and Normal Modes {#L3}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Harmonic oscillator, linear algebra and eigenvalues. [Previous lesson](M4-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive normal modes of two coupled masses and interpret initial motion as superposition.

## From One to Several Degrees of Freedom {#S1}

When two systems interact through a spring or another coupling, their motions are no longer independent. One oscillator can transfer energy to the other. For small displacements, many such systems admit linear equations of motion. A **normal mode** is a simple pattern in which all coordinates oscillate at one common natural frequency with fixed amplitude ratios. General motion is a superposition of several normal modes. The same idea extends from two masses to lattice vibrations in solids.

## Eigenvalue Problem for Two Masses {#S2}

Consider identical masses $m$, each attached to a fixed wall by a spring $k$ and connected to the other mass by a coupling spring $\kappa$. Their displacements obey

$$
m\ddot x_1=-(k+\kappa)x_1+\kappa x_2,\qquad
m\ddot x_2=\kappa x_1-(k+\kappa)x_2.
$$

With $x_i(t)=a_i\cos(\omega t)$ these become an eigenvalue problem. The vector $(1,1)$ represents in-phase motion, which does not stretch the coupling spring. For $(1,-1)$ the coupling spring changes length, giving

$$
\omega_+^2=\frac{k}{m},\qquad \omega_-^2=\frac{k+2\kappa}{m}.
$$

The subscripts $+$ and $-$ denote in-phase and out-of-phase modes here, not positive and negative frequencies.

## Worked Example: Symmetric and Antisymmetric Modes {#S3}

Choose $m=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=9.0\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and $\kappa=3.5\,\mathrm{N/m}$. Then $\omega_+=3.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\omega_-=4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. If only mass 1 is displaced by $0.040\,\mathrm m$ and both masses start from rest, the motion is

$$
x_1(t)=0.020[\cos(3t)+\cos(4t)]\,\mathrm m,
$$

$$
x_2(t)=0.020[\cos(3t)-\cos(4t)]\,\mathrm m.
$$

Initially the sum yields $0.040\,\mathrm m$ for mass 1 and zero for mass 2, as prescribed. The figure illustrates the two eigenpatterns, which determine the motion at all times.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What are the eigenfrequencies when $\kappa=0$?
2. Find both frequencies for $m=2\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=8\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and $\kappa=4\,\mathrm{N/m}$.
3. Why does their joint displacement not depend on $\kappa$?

### Solutions

1. Both modes are degenerate, with $\omega_+=\omega_-=\sqrt{k/m}$.
2. $\omega_+=\sqrt{8/2}=2\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\omega_-=\sqrt{(8+8)/2}=\sqrt8\approx2.83\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.
3. Joint displacement does not extend the coupling spring, so its forces vanish.

## Superposition and Energy Transfer {#S5}

When eigenfrequencies are close, slow amplitude envelopes and periodic transfer of energy between the masses can occur. This is often perceived as **beating**. Without damping, total energy remains constant even though the energies of individual masses vary. For $N$ degrees of freedom the calculation generalises to mass and stiffness matrices. Eigenvalues give squared angular frequencies and eigenvectors describe mode shapes. This requires linear coupling about a suitable stable equilibrium.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Several eigenmodes generate complex motion; in continua they lead to spatially extended waves.
