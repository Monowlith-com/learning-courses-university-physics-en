# M5-L5 – Heat Engines, the Carnot Limit and Refrigerators {#L5}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** First and second laws; temperature in kelvin. [Previous lesson](M5-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Set up cyclic energy balances and calculate ideal efficiency and coefficient of performance.

## Cycle and Energy Balance {#S1}

A heat engine draws heat $Q_h>0$ from a hot reservoir, rejects heat of magnitude $Q_c>0$ to a cooler one and delivers work $W>0$ over a cycle. After one period the working substance has returned to its original state, so $\Delta U_{\mathrm{cyc}}=0$. Thus $W=Q_h-Q_c$ and the thermal efficiency is $\eta=W/Q_h$. These values are magnitudes of exchanged energies; the heat leaving the working substance is negative in its signed energy balance. A cycle may appear as a closed loop on a $p$-$V$ plot, and its oriented enclosed area equals net work in the quasistatic limit.

## Carnot Limit and Reverse Operation {#S2}

For a reversible cycle exchanging heat with two reservoirs, entropy balance gives $Q_h/T_h=Q_c/T_c$ using absolute temperatures. The greatest possible engine efficiency between the reservoirs is

$$
\eta_{\mathrm C}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h},\qquad \eta\leq\eta_{\mathrm C}.
$$

In reverse operation, work must be supplied to transport heat from a cooler region to a warmer one. For a refrigerator $\mathrm{COP}_R=Q_c/W$; for a heat pump $\mathrm{COP}_{HP}=Q_h/W$. Reversible upper bounds are

$$
\mathrm{COP}_{R,\mathrm C}=\frac{T_c}{T_h-T_c},\qquad\mathrm{COP}_{HP,\mathrm C}=\frac{T_h}{T_h-T_c}.
$$

A coefficient of performance exceeding one does not violate conservation of energy: some delivered heat comes from a reservoir.

## Worked Example: Engine and Refrigerator {#S3}

An ideal engine runs between $T_h=600\,\mathrm K$ and $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$ and receives $Q_h=1000\,\mathrm J$ per cycle. The reversible limit is $\eta_{\mathrm C}=1-300/600=0.50$. Thus at most $W=500\,\mathrm J$ and at least $Q_c=500\,\mathrm J$ apply at that limit. An ideal refrigerator between the same temperatures has $\mathrm{COP}_{R,\mathrm C}=300/(600-300)=1.00$: removing $100\,\mathrm J$ from the cold side requires at least $100\,\mathrm J$ of work. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\eta_{\mathrm C}$ for $T_h=500\,\mathrm K$ and $T_c=250\,\mathrm K$.
2. An engine receives $1200\,\mathrm J$ and rejects $780\,\mathrm J$. Calculate work and efficiency.
3. Which quantity is maximised by an ideal heat pump: $W/Q_h$ or $Q_h/W$?

### Solutions

1. $\eta_{\mathrm C}=1-250/500=0.50$.
2. $W=420\,\mathrm J$ and $\eta=420/1200=0.35$.
3. The useful coefficient is $\mathrm{COP}_{HP}=Q_h/W$, not its reciprocal.

## Why Real Machines Differ {#S5}

Friction, finite temperature differences during heat transfer, throttling losses and incomplete expansion generate entropy. Consequently real heat engines operate below the Carnot limit for fixed reservoir temperatures. The Carnot expression says nothing about power output, size, material constraints or cost. A refrigerator working over a small temperature difference can have a high theoretical performance coefficient, but it may require large heat exchangers or long transfer times to move heat across a small gradient. Quantitative design also needs mass flow, pressure drops and real material properties.
