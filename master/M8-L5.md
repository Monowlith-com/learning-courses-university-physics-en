# M8-L5 – Partial Differential Equations: Heat and Waves {#L5}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Partial derivatives, Fourier modes, initial and boundary data. [Previous lesson](M8-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish heat and wave equations and obtain a separated modal solution.

## Spatially Distributed States {#S1}

An ordinary differential equation describes finitely many evolving state variables. In a rod or vibrating string, the unknown depends on position $x$ and time $t$. The excess temperature $u(x,t)$ and string displacement $u(x,t)$ satisfy different partial differential equations. Initial data specify the state at $t=0$, while boundary conditions prescribe behavior at $x=0$ and $x=L$. Without these additional conditions, a formal equation may admit many physically distinct solutions. For an insulated rod, the heat flux at the ends vanishes; for ends maintained at fixed reference temperature, the excess temperature at the ends vanishes instead. These models must not be conflated.

## Heat Conduction and Wave Propagation {#S2}

For constant material parameters and one-dimensional heat transport, the diffusion equation is

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\kappa\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2},
$$

with thermal diffusivity $\kappa$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$. Its time derivative is first order, and thermal contrasts smooth out. For an ideal string, by contrast, 

$$
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2}=c^2\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2},
$$

where $c$ is a wave speed. A second time derivative appears, allowing local disturbances to propagate as waves. Both equations permit separation $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ for suitable homogeneous boundary conditions, but this leads to an exponential time factor for heat and oscillatory time dependence for undamped waves.

## Example: A Decaying Temperature Mode {#S3}

Consider a rod of length $L=1\,\mathrm m$ whose ends remain at the reference temperature. For initial data $u(x,0)=U_0\sin(\pi x/L)$, the solution is

$$
u(x,t)=U_0\sin(\pi x/L)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\kappa\pi^2t}{L^2}\right).
$$

With $\kappa=0.01\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$ and $t=10\,\mathrm s$, the maximum excess temperature falls by the factor $e^{-0.01\pi^2\cdot10}\approx0.3727$. The figure shows the same spatial sine mode at three times: its shape is preserved for this single eigenmode, but the amplitude decays. A general initial temperature profile is normally a sum of modes, with higher spatial frequencies decaying faster because of the factor $n^2$.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the SI unit of $\kappa$ in the heat equation?
2. By what factor does the second spatial heat mode decay relative to its initial amplitude after time $t$?
3. Why does the wave equation require two temporal initial conditions?

### Solutions

1. $\mathrm{m^2/s}$.
2. $\exp(-4\kappa\pi^2t/L^2)$.
3. Because it is second order in time; both initial displacement and initial velocity are needed.

## Boundary Conditions and Numerical Cautions {#S5}

The classical heat equation is a continuum diffusion model. Its mathematical solutions can become nonzero at distant points for arbitrarily small positive times, so it is not an exact relativistic signal-propagation theory. Other transport models may be required at very short times or under steep gradients. The undamped wave equation neglects friction and material dispersion. Dirichlet, Neumann and mixed boundary conditions produce different allowable eigenfunctions. Numerically, grid spacing and time step must also satisfy stability requirements involving diffusivity or wave speed; a visually plausible result is not sufficient numerical validation.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Spatial eigenfunctions and time evolution separate for simple linear boundary problems. Sturm–Liouville theory provides their mathematical basis.
