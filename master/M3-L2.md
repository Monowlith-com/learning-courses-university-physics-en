# M3-L2 – Newton’s Laws and Free-Body Diagrams {#L2}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Vector kinematics and component calculations from M3-L1. [Previous lesson](M3-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply Newton’s laws to force systems and construct correct free-body diagrams.

## From Motion to Interaction {#S1}

Dynamics identifies the **interactions** that produce acceleration. Forces are vectors, measured in newtons: $1\,\mathrm N=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$. Newton's first law expresses inertia: in an inertial reference frame, an object keeps constant velocity when the net force vanishes. Thus **zero acceleration** does not imply an absence of forces; several forces may cancel. The second law relates the sum of external forces to the rate of momentum change. For constant mass, this becomes $\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a$.

## Three Laws and the System Boundary {#S2}

For a body of constant mass, Newton's second law reads

$$
\sum\mathbf F_{\mathrm{ext}}=m\mathbf a.
$$

Newton's third law pairs forces as $\mathbf F_{A\to B}=-\mathbf F_{B\to A}$. These two forces act **on different bodies**; they must not be cancelled in the free-body diagram of one body. A free-body diagram isolates the chosen object, shows every external force with its direction and point of application and represents contacts by forces. Choosing suitable axes then gives $\sum F_x=ma_x$ and $\sum F_y=ma_y$. Near Earth's surface the weight has magnitude $mg$; a normal force is a contact force and need not equal $mg$.

## Worked Example: Crate on a Smooth Floor {#S3}

A crate of mass $m=5.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ is pulled to the right across a horizontal, frictionless floor by $F=20\,\mathrm N$. Vertically, the forces balance because the crate does not lift off:

$$
N-mg=0,\qquad N=49.05\,\mathrm N.
$$

Horizontally the applied force is the net force:

$$
a_x=\frac{F}{m}=\frac{20\,\mathrm N}{5.0\,\mathrm{kg}}=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

The free-body diagram shows $N$ upward, $mg$ downward and $F$ to the right. Acceleration is not an additional force on the diagram. Starting at rest, the crate reaches $12\,\mathrm{m/s}$ after $3.0\,\mathrm s$.

*Normal force balances weight vertically; the pull accelerates horizontally.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A horizontal net force of $15\,\mathrm N$ acts on $3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$. Calculate the acceleration.
2. A block rests on a table. Which external forces belong in its free-body diagram?
3. Are $N$ and $mg$ a Newton's-third-law pair? Explain.

### Solutions

1. $a=15/3.0=5.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
2. Weight downward and the normal force upward, assuming no other contact forces.
3. No. Both forces act on the same object. The reaction to the normal force acts on the table; the reaction to the gravitational force acts on Earth.

## Common Modelling Errors {#S5}

A free-body diagram contains only forces acting on the selected object, not arrows for its velocity or forces exerted by it on its surroundings. The mass may be treated as constant in $F=ma$ only if the definition of the system supports that assumption. Accelerating reference frames require additional inertial-force terms in their description; the basic form of Newton's laws assumes an inertial frame. Friction, tension and drag must be introduced as separate force models when relevant.
