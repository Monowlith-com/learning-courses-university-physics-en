# M5-L2 – Kinetic Gas Theory and the Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution {#L2}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Ideal gas equation, averages, and three-dimensional velocity. [Previous lesson](M5-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Interpret gas pressure through momentum transfer and calculate velocity characteristics of an equilibrium distribution.

## Microscopic Origin of Pressure {#S1}

An ideal monatomic gas is approximated by moving particles whose collisions with walls and with each other are treated as elastic. A collision changes the normal component of momentum; averaging many impacts produces a force and hence pressure. For isotropic velocities, $\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v_y^2\rangle=\langle v_z^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle/3$. Averaging over many particles explains why macroscopic pressure appears steady even though each molecule makes irregular impacts. This is an equilibrium model; an individual collision cannot define the pressure or temperature of an entire gas.

## Speed Distribution {#S2}

Momentum flux gives the pressure of $N$ particles of mass $m$ as

$$
pV=\tfrac13Nm\langle v^2\rangle.
$$

Comparison with $pV=Nk_{\mathrm B}T$ yields $\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle=\tfrac32k_{\mathrm B}T$. In classical equilibrium the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of speed $v\geq0$ is

$$
f(v)=4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_{\mathrm B}T}\right)^{3/2}v^2\exp\!\left(-\frac{mv^2}{2k_{\mathrm B}T}\right),\qquad\int_0^\infty f(v)\,dv=1.
$$

It describes a probability distribution, not one unique speed. At higher $T$ the distribution broadens and shifts to faster speeds. The most probable speed is $v_{\mathrm{mp}}=\sqrt{2k_{\mathrm B}T/m}$, while the root-mean-square speed is $v_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{3k_{\mathrm B}T/m}$.

## Worked Example: Characteristic Speed {#S3}

For nitrogen molecules at $T=300\,\mathrm K$ take approximately $m=4.65\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $k_{\mathrm B}=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$. Then

$$
v_{\mathrm{mp}}\approx422\,\mathrm{m/s},\qquad v_{\mathrm{rms}}\approx517\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

The latter is larger because the average of $v^2$ places more weight on fast particles. Both characteristic speeds grow as $\sqrt T$ and, at fixed temperature, decrease with $1/\sqrt m$. The diagram compares two distributions at different temperatures for the same molecular mass. Both enclosed areas are one; a higher, narrower curve does not mean that more molecules are present.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $v_{\mathrm{rms}}$ change if $T$ is quadrupled?
2. Find $v_{\mathrm{rms}}/v_{\mathrm{mp}}$ for a classical gas.
3. Why does the width of the speed distribution matter at higher temperature?

### Solutions

1. It doubles because $v_{\mathrm{rms}}\propto\sqrt T$.
2. $v_{\mathrm{rms}}/v_{\mathrm{mp}}=\sqrt{3/2}\approx1.225$.
3. Temperature characterises a spread of speeds; transport and collision rates depend on that distribution, not merely its peak.

## Range and Limitations {#S5}

Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics assumes classical, nondegenerate particles in thermal equilibrium. Electrons in metals and ultracold quantum gases require different occupation statistics. Molecules may store energy in rotational and vibrational modes; the relation for **translational** kinetic energy remains useful, but the full internal energy is then not simply $\tfrac32Nk_{\mathrm B}T$. Rapid nozzle flows and strong gradients can also produce anisotropic, nonthermal distributions. Connecting microscopic motion to temperature therefore depends on a justified equilibrium approximation.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Macroscopic gas pressure is an average of microscopic momentum transfers. Heat and work next connect microscopic energy with macroscopic balances.
