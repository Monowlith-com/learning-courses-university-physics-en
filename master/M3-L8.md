# M3-L8 – Stress, Strain and Linear Elasticity {#L8}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Force balance, geometry and SI units. [Previous lesson](M3-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Define stress and strain and apply Hooke’s law to elastic bars.

## From Rigid to Deformable Bodies {#S1}

Earlier models often treated objects as rigid. Real materials change shape under load. For a straight bar under axial tension, **normal stress** measures axial force per cross-sectional area and **strain** measures relative change in length. Both are needed: the same tensile force stresses a thin bar more than a thick one, and the same extension is a larger fraction of the length of a short bar. Linear elasticity describes small reversible deformations, not the full response up to fracture.

## Stress, Strain and Young’s Modulus {#S2}

For a uniform bar undergoing small axial deformation, the engineering quantities are

$$
\sigma=\frac{F}{A},\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta L}{L_0},\qquad
\sigma=E\varepsilon.
$$

Stress $\sigma$ has units of pascals ($\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{N/m^2}$); strain $\varepsilon$ is dimensionless, so Young's modulus $E$ also has units of pascals. Hooke's law gives

$$
\Delta L=\frac{FL_0}{EA},
$$

provided $F$, $E$ and $A$ may be treated as approximately uniform along the bar. This uniaxial linear model assumes a homogeneous material, small strains and loading within the elastic range.

## Worked Example: Extension of a Metal Bar {#S3}

A bar has initial length $L_0=2.0\,\mathrm m$ and cross-section $A=100\,\mathrm{mm^2}=1.00\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. It is pulled axially with $F=10\,\mathrm{kN}$. For a material with $E=200\,\mathrm{GPa}$,

$$
\sigma=\frac{10^4}{10^{-4}}\,\mathrm{Pa}=100\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac{10^8}{2\cdot10^{11}}=5.0\cdot10^{-4}.
$$

The extension is $\Delta L=\varepsilon L_0=1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. The diagram illustrates the initially linear relation between stress and strain; outside that range the material may behave differently. The actual strength limit of a specific material has **not** been determined here.

*The initial linear slope is Young’s modulus E; behaviour beyond it is material dependent.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. An axial force $F=4.0\,\mathrm{kN}$ acts on $A=2.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. Find normal stress.
2. A $1.0\,\mathrm m$ bar extends by $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. Find strain.
3. Why does $E$ not equal the maximum allowable stress?

### Solutions

1. $A=2.0\cdot10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so $\sigma=4.0\cdot10^3/(2.0\cdot10^{-4})=20\,\mathrm{MPa}$.
2. $\varepsilon=0.0005/1.0=5.0\cdot10^{-4}$.
3. $E$ is the slope of the linearly elastic stress–strain relationship; strength is a separate property.

## Material Limits and Other Loading Modes {#S5}

The linear model does not predict fracture or permanent plastic deformation. Higher loads may cause yielding, cracking, time-dependent creep or buckling; temperature and loading history can matter as well. Tension, compression, shear and volumetric compression involve different measures and material constants. General three-dimensional stress states require tensor descriptions. Exercises should therefore specify cross-section, load direction and the assumed validity range.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Forces produce stress and strain in deformable bodies. Linear elasticity relates them only within a limited material range.

Subsequent modules deepen mechanics and introduce other areas of physics.
