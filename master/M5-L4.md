# M5-L4 – Entropy, Second Law and Irreversibility {#L4}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** First law, ideal gas and logarithmic integration. [Previous lesson](M5-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate reversible entropy changes and explain irreversible entropy production.

## Direction of Spontaneous Processes {#S1}

Conservation of energy alone allows many hypothetical process directions. Gas initially confined to one side of an isolated vessel spontaneously fills the available space; the macroscopic reverse does not occur although the total energy may be unchanged. The **second law** selects a direction: the entropy of an isolated total system increases for real irreversible changes and remains constant in the ideal reversible limit. “Disorder” can be an analogy but is not a precise definition. Entropy is a state variable measured in $\mathrm{J/K}$. A thermodynamic entropy balance distinguishes entropy transported across the boundary from entropy produced internally.

## Entropy as a State Function {#S2}

The entropy difference between equilibrium states can be computed using a hypothetical reversible reference path:

$$
dS=\frac{\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T},\qquad\Delta S=\int_1^2\frac{\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}.
$$

This equation must not be applied to arbitrary irreversible heat transfer after dropping the “rev” subscript. For an ideal gas with constant heat capacity,

$$
\Delta S=nC_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}.
$$

Starting with $dU=nC_VdT$ and $\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}=dU+p\,dV$, insert $p=nRT/V$ and integrate. For an isolated total system without entropy transfer, $\Delta S=S_{\mathrm{gen}}\geq0$. Reversible and irreversible paths may join the same end states; the state change $\Delta S$ is then identical.

## Worked Example: Free Expansion {#S3}

An ideal gas of $n=1.00\,\mathrm{mol}$ expands freely and in isolation from $V_1$ to $V_2=2V_1$. In the vacuum experiment $Q=0$ and $W=0$ approximately, hence $\Delta U=0$ and $T_2=T_1$ for an ideal gas. The actual expansion is irreversible, but the entropy difference can be evaluated along a **hypothetical reversible isothermal path**:

$$
\Delta S=nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=8.314\ln2\approx5.76\,\mathrm{J/K}.
$$

The entropy increase was not carried in by heat: it was generated by irreversibility. The illustration separates the suddenly opened partition from the computational comparison between states. Real gases can show small temperature changes because their internal energy is not only a function of $T$.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $\Delta S$ for free doubling of volume of $2.00\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ideal gas with the same final temperature.
2. Can a refrigerator reduce entropy in its interior?
3. Why is $\Delta S=Q/T=0$ invalid for free expansion?

### Solutions

1. $\Delta S=2R\ln2\approx11.52\,\mathrm{J/K}$.
2. Yes; it exports heat and entropy, while the total entropy including the surroundings does not decrease.
3. $dS=\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T$ is a reversible-path relation, not the entropy change along the actual irreversible expansion.

## Local Production and Caveats {#S5}

Conduction between bodies at different temperatures, friction and mixing produce entropy. Strongly nonuniform or rapidly evolving states should not be assigned equilibrium variables uncritically; a **local** entropy balance is often used when local thermal equilibrium is a justified approximation. A subsystem can export entropy and therefore show a negative entropy change; the second law concerns the system **together with its surroundings**, not necessarily each subsystem separately. Microscopic equations that are reversible in time do not contradict macroscopic entropy growth: statistical initial conditions and the huge number of microscopic states matter.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Entropy is a well-defined state variable; nonnegative entropy production characterises irreversible operation. This direction constraint limits heat engines.
