# M12-L2 – Time Evolution and the Schrödinger Equation {#L2}

**Course area:** M12 – Bachelor: Advanced Quantum Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Differentiation, complex exponentials, eigenvalues, and M12-L1. [Previous lesson](M12-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive unitary time evolution from the Hamiltonian and distinguish stationary from changing states.

## Dynamics of Probability Amplitudes {#S1}

Following preparation, an isolated quantum state evolves continuously. This is not a classical point-particle trajectory but the changing complex probability amplitude. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation reads

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle=H|\psi(t)\rangle
$$

The self-adjoint Hamiltonian $H$ determines energy and dynamics. For a one-dimensional nonrelativistic particle a common Hamiltonian is $H=-\hbar^2\partial_x^2/(2m)+V(x)$; the position representation satisfies the corresponding partial differential equation. Specifying a potential is a physical modelling step rather than a mathematical formality.

## Hamiltonian and Unitary Propagator {#S2}

For time-independent $H$, the evolution is

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle,\qquad U(t)=\exp(-iHt/\hbar)
$$

Since $U^\dagger U=I$, normalization is preserved. An energy eigenstate $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$ acquires only the phase $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, so its probability density remains unchanged in isolation. A superposition $\sum_n c_n|n\rangle$ gains relative phases $e^{-i(E_n-E_m)t/\hbar}$. These can affect observables that fail to commute with $H$. With time-dependent Hamiltonians, the evolution generally requires time ordering, rather than a single naive matrix exponential.

## Example: Relative Phase of Two Levels {#S3}

For levels $E_0=0$ and $E_1=\hbar\omega$, prepare $|\psi(0)\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Then

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=(|0\rangle+e^{-i\omega t}|1\rangle)/\sqrt 2
$$

The energy-measurement probabilities stay at $1/2$ each. Measurements in the alternative basis $|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ instead give $P(+)=\cos^2(\omega t/2)$ and $P(-)=\sin^2(\omega t/2)$. At $t=\pi/\omega$ an initially certain $+$ outcome becomes a certain $-$ outcome. The illustration shows rotating relative phase while energy-basis populations remain fixed.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $P(E_1)$ change for the above superposition?
2. When does $P(+)$ first become zero?
3. What does unitary evolution guarantee?

### Solutions

1. $P(E_1)=1/2$ remains constant.
2. At $\omega t=\pi$, hence $t=\pi/\omega$.
3. Norms and inner products are preserved.

## Conserved Quantities and Limitations {#S5}

For a time-independent observable $A$ with no explicit time dependence, Schrödinger evolution implies the Ehrenfest relation $d\langle A\rangle/dt=(i/\hbar)\langle[H,A]\rangle$. If $A$ commutes with $H$, its expectation value is conserved. This statement assumes suitable states and operator domains; integrations by parts cannot discard nonvanishing boundary terms. A real subsystem that interacts with an environment generally does not undergo unitary evolution by itself. Relativistic quantum fields require a different formal framework.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Unitary dynamics changes relative phases and observables while conserving total probability.
