# M17-L4 – Linear Systems and Eigenvalues {#L4}

**Course area:** M17 – Bachelor: Computational Physics and Simulation

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M2: vectors/matrices; M8: linear operators. [Previous lesson](M17-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Solve a linear physical model and explain conditioning and the interpretation of eigenvalues.

## Discrete Models and Matrices {#S1}

Electric circuits, coupled springs and stationary heat transport often produce systems $A\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol b$ after discretization. The entries of $A$ describe couplings and material properties, while $\boldsymbol b$ represents sources or boundary constraints. A numerical solver cannot replace a correct derivation of $A$. Equations with strongly disparate scales may need normalization. A misplaced boundary condition can be more consequential than algorithmic roundoff.

## Solutions, Conditioning and Eigenvalues {#S2}

Gaussian elimination can be checked by hand for small systems. Practical numerical solvers usually employ factorizations with suitable pivoting rather than explicitly forming the inverse. For invertible $A$, its condition number $\kappa(A)=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|$ describes worst-case amplification of relative perturbations. Eigenvalues satisfy $A\boldsymbol v=\lambda\boldsymbol v$ and may characterize normal modes or relaxation rates. A general matrix need not be symmetric and may have complex eigenvalues; physical meaning depends on the originating dynamical equation.

$$
A\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol b,\qquad \kappa(A)=\|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|.
$$

$$
\det(B-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=0.
$$

## Example: Coupled Linear Equations {#S3}

The equations $3x+y=9$ and $x+2y=8$ have determinant $3\cdot2-1\cdot1=5$ and solution $x=2$, $y=3$. Substitution gives $3\cdot2+3=9$ and $2+2\cdot3=8$. For the symmetric matrix $B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$, with orthogonal eigenvectors along $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. This illustrates decoupled normal directions of a symmetric coupling matrix. Physical vibration problems often involve a generalized eigenvalue equation with a mass matrix.

**Executable Python example:**

```python
a,b,c,d = 3.0,1.0,1.0,2.0
u,v = 9.0,8.0
det = a*d-b*c
x = (u*d-b*v)/det
y = (a*v-u*c)/det
print(x,y)
print(a*x+b*y-u, c*x+d*y-v)
```

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Solve $3x+y=9$ and $x+2y=8$.
2. Find the eigenvalues of $B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
3. Why may the solution error be large even with a small residual?

### Solutions

1. $x=2$, $y=3$.
2. Solving $(2-\lambda)^2-1=0$ yields $\lambda=1,3$.
3. Poor conditioning amplifies input and numerical perturbations.

## Conservation and Plausibility {#S5}

Check the residual $\|A\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol b\|$ separately from sensitivity to perturbations. A small residual does not guarantee an accurate solution for ill-conditioned $A$. Sparse large-scale problems require attention to memory and iterative methods. For eigenmodes also verify sign conventions, normalization, boundary conditions and degeneracies. Arbitrary eigenvector signs or ordering can make equivalent results look different, so compare invariant quantities or eigenspaces.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Linear algebra yields numerical states and modes; conditioning and residual are distinct quality measures.
