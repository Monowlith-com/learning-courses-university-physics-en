# M22-L3 – Membranes, Osmosis and Electrochemical Equilibrium {#L3}

**Course area:** M22 – Bachelor: Biophysics

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M5: ideal gases; M6: electricity; M13: chemical equilibrium. [Previous lesson](M22-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Estimate osmotic pressure, derive the Nernst relation and distinguish transport from equilibrium.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

A cell membrane separates solutions with different solute concentrations. Depending on which substances can cross it, water experiences osmotic pressure differences, whereas ions respond to both chemical and electric gradients. The membrane is not simply a mechanical filter: selective permeability and transport proteins determine which equilibria are accessible.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

For an ideal dilute solution with effective concentration $c$ of osmotically active solute particles, van 't Hoff's approximation gives the osmotic pressure. For a single permeant ionic species of charge number $z$, equating electrochemical potentials, $RT\ln c_{\mathrm{in}}+zF\phi_{\mathrm{in}}=RT\ln c_{\mathrm{out}}+zF\phi_{\mathrm{out}}$, gives the Nernst voltage

$$
\Pi\simeq cRT,\qquad E_{\mathrm{in}}-E_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{c_{\mathrm{out}}}{c_{\mathrm{in}}}\right).
$$

The sign convention is explicitly $\phi_{\mathrm{in}}-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}$. If multiple ionic species permeate simultaneously, a coupled transport model is required and one ion's Nernst value is not automatically the actual membrane voltage. Simple diffusion in a homogeneous medium without other driving forces is approximated by $J=-D\,dc/dx$.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

For an ideal dilute solute at $c=0.10\,\mathrm{mol/L}=100\,\mathrm{mol/m^3}$ and $T=298\,\mathrm K$, $\Pi\approx100\cdot8.314\cdot298\,\mathrm{Pa}=2.48\cdot10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. For a monovalent positive ion with $c_{\mathrm{out}}/c_{\mathrm{in}}=10$, $\phi_{\mathrm{in}}-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}\approx+59.1\,\mathrm{mV}$. The figure distinguishes an osmotic driving force from an ionic equilibrium voltage.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Estimate ideal osmotic pressure for $c=50\,\mathrm{mol/m^3}$ at $T=300\,\mathrm K$.
2. Find the Nernst voltage for $z=+1$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}/c_{\mathrm{in}}=1$.
3. Why is the K$^+$ Nernst value insufficient for a membrane permeable to both K$^+$ and Na$^+$?

### Solutions

1. $\Pi\approx50\cdot8.314\cdot300\,\mathrm{Pa}\approx125\,\mathrm{kPa}$.
2. $\ln1=0$; the equilibrium voltage is zero in the single-ion model.
3. Both species contribute through their permeabilities and electrochemical driving forces.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The osmotic relation assumes an ideal dilute solution with correctly counted osmotic particles; activity and membrane-reflection effects are excluded. The Nernst equation requires electrochemical equilibrium for one ion, whereas active pumping and simultaneous fluxes require a more complete model.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Osmotic pressure arises from collective solute concentration. An equilibrium ionic voltage instead balances chemical and electrical potential differences.
