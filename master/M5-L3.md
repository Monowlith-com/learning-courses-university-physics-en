# M5-L3 – First Law, Heat and Volume Work {#L3}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Ideal gas equation of state and elementary mechanics. [Previous lesson](M5-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Balance energy transfer as heat and work and distinguish isochoric and isobaric processes.

## System Boundary and Signs {#S1}

Heat and work are ways of transferring energy across a system boundary; they are not substances stored inside a body. **Internal energy** $U$, by contrast, is a state variable. For a closed system with no change in macroscopic kinetic or potential energy choose the sign convention $Q>0$ for heat supplied to the system and $W>0$ for work done by the system on its surroundings. Energy conservation then reads $\Delta U=Q-W$. Other textbooks take work done on the system as positive, so the sign convention must be explicit. Adiabatic means $Q=0$, not necessarily constant temperature.

## Energy Balance and Process Paths {#S2}

For a quasistatic expansion against the system pressure, the volume-work increment is $\delta W=p\,dV$ and along a path $W=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p\,dV$. A real irreversible change normally requires the actual external pressure at the moving boundary. For an ideal gas with fixed amount, internal energy depends only on temperature; when heat capacity is constant,

$$
\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T,\qquad Q=\Delta U+W.
$$

At constant volume $dV=0$, so $W=0$ and $Q_V=\Delta U$. At constant pressure for an ideal gas, $W=p\Delta V=nR\Delta T$, giving $Q_p=n(C_V+R)\Delta T=nC_p\Delta T$. The relation $C_p-C_V=R$ holds for molar ideal-gas heat capacities. In a $p$-$V$ diagram, the area below a quasistatic path represents its volume work.

## Worked Example: Constant-Pressure Heating {#S3}

A sample of $n=1.00\,\mathrm{mol}$ of an ideal monatomic gas is heated at constant pressure by $\Delta T=100\,\mathrm K$. Using $C_V=\tfrac32R$ and $C_p=\tfrac52R$ gives

$$
\Delta U=\tfrac32nR\Delta T\approx1247\,\mathrm J,\qquad W=nR\Delta T\approx831\,\mathrm J,\qquad Q\approx2079\,\mathrm J.
$$

The sum of internal-energy change and work done agrees with the heat supplied. For the same temperature increase at constant volume the model needs only about $1247\,\mathrm J$ of heat. The figure shows an isobaric path as a horizontal line and the rectangle representing work. Constant heat capacities and only three translational degrees of freedom are assumed.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A closed system receives $Q=500\,\mathrm J$ and performs $W=180\,\mathrm J$. Find $\Delta U$.
2. For an isothermal state change of an ideal gas, $\Delta U=0$. What follows about $Q$ and $W$?
3. Explain why the area below a $p$-$V$ curve only gives actual work with the appropriate pressure definition.

### Solutions

1. $\Delta U=Q-W=320\,\mathrm J$.
2. With our convention $Q=W$, whether or not the path is reversible; the numerical amounts remain path dependent.
3. During irreversible expansion, internal pressure can differ from the external boundary pressure, which determines the mechanical work.

## State and Process Quantities {#S5}

$U$, $p$, $V$ and $T$ depend on the state, whereas $Q$ and $W$ depend on the path. One may therefore define $\Delta U$ between states but not a unique “heat contained in a state.” Free expansion of an ideal gas into vacuum does no boundary work if the external pressure vanishes; despite $\Delta V>0$, it can have $W=0$. Open flowing systems additionally require enthalpy, flow work and streams of kinetic energy. The simple closed-system balance does not replace a general control-volume calculation. Efficiency claims also require the second law.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The first law accounts for energy but does not select a preferred process direction. The second law introduces entropy and irreversibility.
