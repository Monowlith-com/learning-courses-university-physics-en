# M17-L8 – Verification, Validation and Documented Simulation {#L8}

**Course area:** M17 – Bachelor: Computational Physics and Simulation

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M16: uncertainty budgets and reporting; M17-L1–L7: numerical methods. [Previous lesson](M17-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Measure convergence order, distinguish numerical verification from physical validation and design a reproducible simulation report.

## Separating Verification and Validation {#S1}

Verification asks whether the implementation solves the intended mathematical equations with adequate accuracy. Validation asks whether those equations and parameters represent the physical situation well enough. These questions are independent. Numerically solving an incorrect equation exactly does not validate nature. Conversely, agreement with observations may result from compensating errors. A testing program includes analytic limit cases, independent implementations, unit and conservation checks and comparisons with measurements including their uncertainties.

## Richardson Reasoning and Convergence Rate {#S2}

When a method's asymptotic error behaves as $E(h)\approx Ch^p$, its order can be estimated from known errors at $h$ and $h/2$. The formula requires the same quantity of interest and computations already within the asymptotic range. Without an exact solution, differences between successive grid results may support error estimates, but only under justified convergence assumptions. For PDEs refine spatial and temporal steps separately; otherwise compensating errors can hide deficiencies.

$$
D_h=\frac{e^h-e^{-h}}{2h}=\frac{\sinh h}{h},\qquad E(h)=|D_h-1|.
$$

$$
p\approx\log_2\!\left(\frac{E(h)}{E(h/2)}\right).
$$

## Example: Central Difference {#S3}

Approximate the derivative of $f(x)=e^x$ at $x=0$ using a centered finite difference. The exact derivative is $f'(0)=1$ and the discrete approximation is $D_h=\sinh(h)/h$. For $h=0.2$, $D_h\approx1.0066800$; for $h=0.1$, it is $1.0016675$. Halving $h$ therefore reduces the error by about four, indicating order $p\approx2$. The figure shows a reference and error trend, not measured observations. For sufficiently small $h$, cancellation and roundoff eventually obscure formal convergence.

**Executable Python example:**

```python
import math
def error(h):
    return abs((math.exp(h)-math.exp(-h))/(2*h)-1)
e1, e2 = error(.2), error(.1)
print(e1, e2, math.log2(e1/e2))
```

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Determine observed order from $E(h)=0.008$ and $E(h/2)=0.002$.
2. Approximate $D_{0.1}=\sinh(0.1)/0.1$.
3. Give an example of successful verification without physical validation.

### Solutions

1. $p=\log_2(0.008/0.002)=2$.
2. $D_{0.1}\approx1.0016675$; absolute error about $0.0016675$.
3. A convergent ideal-pendulum solver can ignore real friction and thus fail long-term physical data.

## A Reproducible Final Report {#S5}

Record the research question, governing equations, boundary data, units, source-code revision, dependencies, inputs, random seed where relevant, numerical parameters, tests and uncertainties. Report results with labeled axes, physical units and a distinction between numerical and empirical errors. Failed or unexpected tests belong in the record rather than being silently removed from plots. 

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
Numerical verification, physical validation and reproducible reporting are distinct components of simulation.
