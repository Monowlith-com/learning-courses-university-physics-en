# M13-L8 – Ising Model and Phase Transitions {#L8}

**Course area:** M13 – Bachelor: Statistical Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Boltzmann weights and partition functions from M13-L3; entropy and magnetic spins from M13-L1 and M11. [Previous lesson](M13-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Interpret the Ising Hamiltonian, calculate a two-spin partition function, and distinguish 1D and 2D transitions.

## Local Interactions Can Produce Collective Order {#S1}

The Ising model replaces magnetic degrees of freedom by discrete spins $s_i=\pm1$ on a lattice. Each spin couples to its neighbors, and a field $h$ favors one orientation. With ferromagnetic coupling $J>0$ the energy is

$$
H=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}s_is_j-h\sum_i s_i.
$$

The pair notation $\langle i,j\rangle$ counts every nearest-neighbor pair once. For $J>0$ aligned spins lower the energy, whereas the large number of disordered configurations favors entropy at high temperature. Magnetization $m=\langle\sum_i s_i\rangle/N$ is a possible order parameter. Competition between energy and entropy can create collective behavior in large systems that does not occur in a single two-state unit.

## Dimension, Thermodynamic Limit, and Criticality {#S2}

A phase transition is associated with nonanalytic behavior of the appropriate thermodynamic potential in the thermodynamic limit. A partition function made from finitely many regular finite-temperature terms remains analytic at any finite positive temperature. In zero field, an infinite one-dimensional Ising chain with short-range interactions has no spontaneous ferromagnetic order at finite temperature. The square two-dimensional Ising lattice, by contrast, has a critical point. For the isotropic model at $h=0$, the exact relation is

$$
\sinh(2\beta_cJ)=1,\qquad \frac{k_BT_c}{J}=\frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}\approx2.2692.
$$

This number is not universal for arbitrary lattices or anisotropic couplings; it belongs to the specified model. The schematic contrasts a disordered configuration with a locally ordered one.

## Example: Two Coupled Spins {#S3}

For two spins coupled by one open-boundary bond and with no external field, $H=-Js_1s_2$. The two aligned states have energy $-J$, while the two opposite states have $+J$. Thus

$$
Z_2=2e^{\beta J}+2e^{-\beta J}=4\cosh(\beta J).
$$

Differentiating with respect to $\beta J$ gives the pair correlation $\langle s_1s_2\rangle=\tanh(\beta J)$. At $\beta J=0.5$ this is about $0.4621$. This finite pair demonstrates coupling-induced correlations but has no true thermodynamic phase transition. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $Z_2$ for $J=0$.
2. Evaluate $\langle s_1s_2\rangle$ at $\beta J=0$.
3. Why can two spins not exhibit a genuine thermodynamic phase transition?

### Solutions

1. $Z_2=4$.
2. $\tanh(0)=0$.
3. A finite-system partition function is analytic at finite temperature and lacks the thermodynamic singularity.

## Real Materials and Critical Exponents {#S5}

The Ising model is intentionally minimal: it has neither continuous spin directions nor a complete account of the magnetic interactions in any particular real solid. Critical exponents and universality classes explain how microscopically different systems can share scaling laws near a continuous transition. Limits such as $N\to\infty$, $h\to0$, and $T\to T_c$ cannot be interchanged freely when defining spontaneous symmetry breaking. Numerical simulations must specify boundary conditions, system size, and thermal equilibration. The exact critical temperature above is a special property of the isotropic two-dimensional square-lattice model.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
Statistical mechanics links microscopic counting to collective order.
