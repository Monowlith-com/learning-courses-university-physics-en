# M7-L2 – Geometrical Optics: Lenses and Imaging {#L2}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Refraction, similar triangles and the paraxial approximation. [Previous lesson](M7-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply the thin-lens equation and determine magnification and image type from signs.

## Paraxial Ray Model {#S1}

In the geometrical-optics limit directed rays describe narrow optical bundles. A thin converging lens redirects rays near and parallel to the optical axis toward a point on the image side at focal length $f>0$. A thin diverging lens has $f<0$. Standard construction rays are: one entering parallel to the axis and leaving toward the image-side focus; one entering through the object-side focus and exiting parallel; and one passing approximately undeviated through the optical centre. For a real image the actual rays meet. For a virtual image only the backward extensions intersect; it cannot be collected directly on a screen.

## Lens Equation and Signs {#S2}

We adopt $g>0$ for a real object before the lens and $b>0$ for a real image behind it. Similar triangles for paraxial rays give

$$
\frac{1}{f}=\frac{1}{g}+\frac{1}{b},\qquad m=-\frac{b}{g}.
$$

Here $m=h'/h$ is signed transverse magnification; $m<0$ means that the image is inverted. Dimensionless magnification should not be confused with a length. For $g>f>0$ one finds $b>0$; for $0<g<f$, $b<0$ and the image is virtual and upright. Imaging is treated to first order around the axis. In multiple-lens systems object and image distances must be reconstructed between elements; focal lengths cannot simply be added without conditions.

## Worked Example: Object in Front of a Lens {#S3}

A converging lens has $f=0.100\,\mathrm m$. An object of height $h=2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is at $g=0.300\,\mathrm m$. We obtain $1/b=1/0.100-1/0.300=6.667\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so $b=0.150\,\mathrm m$. Magnification is $m=-0.150/0.300=-0.50$, giving image height $h'=-1.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. The image is real, reduced and inverted. The drawing marks object height, principal axis, focal points and two construction rays that intersect. Move the object between the front focus and the lens in a thought experiment: the emerging physical rays no longer intersect.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $b$ and $m$ for $f=10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $g=20\,\mathrm{cm}$.
2. Determine $b$ and the image type for $f=10\,\mathrm{cm}$, $g=5\,\mathrm{cm}$.
3. Why does making the aperture arbitrarily small not improve image sharpness without limit?

### Solutions

1. $b=20\,\mathrm{cm}$, $m=-1$; real, equal in size and inverted.
2. $1/b=1/10-1/5=-1/10\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; hence $b=-10\,\mathrm{cm}$, virtual and upright.
3. Diffraction blur increases as the aperture diameter is reduced.

## Aberrations and Aperture {#S5}

The thin-lens equation neglects thickness, large ray angles and frequency-dependent refraction. Real lenses exhibit spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism and chromatic aberration. The aperture limits rays and changes depth of field as well as diffraction. A smaller aperture can reduce geometrical aberrations while increasing diffraction; there is an optimum rather than an unconditional smaller-is-better rule. In microscopes and cameras magnification, diffraction-limited resolution and detector sampling must be considered together.
