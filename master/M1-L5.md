# M1-L5 – Functions, Derivatives and Integrals in Physics {#L5}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor: Foundations of Physics

**Level:** Bachelor · Introductory

**Prerequisites:** Elementary functions, vectors and the slope of a straight line. [Previous lesson](M1-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Relate position, velocity and acceleration functions through differentiation and integration.

## Motion as a Function {#S1}

The position function $x(t)$ describes where a body is at time $t$ along a specified axis. Two measurement points give only an average velocity. When the interval becomes smaller and smaller, a differentiable trajectory yields the instantaneous velocity. Its geometric meaning is the tangent slope in a position–time plot.

## Local and Accumulated Change {#S2}

For one-dimensional motion,

$$
v(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{x(t+\Delta t)-x(t)}{\Delta t}=\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt},
\qquad a(t)=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}.
$$

The magnitude $|v(t)|$ is speed. Conversely, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$
v(t)=v(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^t a(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau,
$$

$$
x(t)=x(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^t v(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau.
$$

The integral of velocity is signed displacement; the integral of its magnitude is distance travelled. Initial values are needed to reconstruct absolute values from rates of change. A zero net integral does not mean no motion occurred along the way.

## Example: Constant Acceleration {#S3}

A model body has $x_0=1\,\mathrm m$, $v_0=2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and constant acceleration $a=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. For $t_0=0$, integration gives

$$
v(t)=v_0+at,\qquad x(t)=x_0+v_0t+\frac12at^2.
$$

At $t=3\,\mathrm s$, $v=8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $x=16\,\mathrm m$. The displacement is 15 m. In the velocity–time plot the trapezoid area $(2+8)/2\cdot3=15$ in metres gives the same result. As a cross-check, differentiating the position function returns exactly the velocity function.

![Velocity–time graph increasing from 2 to 8 m/s with a trapezoid area.](../../../../media/course/U_PHY_312_EN.svg)

*The area from 0 to 3 s under the velocity line equals 15 m.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. For $x(t)=A t^3$ with $A=2\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$, calculate $v$ and $a$ at $t=2\,\mathrm s$.
2. A body moves for 2 s at $+3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and then for 2 s at $-3\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Calculate displacement and distance travelled.
3. What initial information is needed to reconstruct $x(t)$ from a known $a(t)$?

### Solutions

1. $v=3At^2=24\,\mathrm{m/s}$; $a=6At=24\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
2. Displacement $0\,\mathrm m$; distance travelled $12\,\mathrm m$.
3. Initial position and initial velocity at a specified time.

## Measurement Data and Differentiability {#S5}

Measurements provide only discrete values affected by error. A smaller interval in a difference quotient can amplify measurement noise; it is not automatically better. An idealised abrupt velocity change is not ordinarily differentiable at the jump. For three-dimensional motion, derivatives in a fixed Cartesian basis are taken componentwise.

## Summary {#S6}

Differentiation and integration are complementary tools: slope describes local rate of change, while area gives accumulated change. Physical application requires specified initial conditions. Next we apply model testing to an elementary experiment.
