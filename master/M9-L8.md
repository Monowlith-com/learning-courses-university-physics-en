# M9-L8 – Hamilton–Jacobi Equation and Action Variables {#L8}

**Course area:** M9 – Bachelor: Analytical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Hamiltonian, Poisson brackets, integration and harmonic oscillator. [Previous lesson](M9-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Formulate the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and compute the harmonic oscillator action variable.

## Dynamics in a Characteristic Function {#S1}

Hamilton’s equations are ordinary differential equations for coordinates and momenta. An alternative approach is to find a function $S(q,t)$ whose coordinate derivative gives momentum. This principal or Hamilton–Jacobi action function can serve as a generating function for a canonical transformation to simpler variables. The method is more than a change in notation: in integrable systems it provides a route to periodic motion, action-angle coordinates and later semiclassical approximations. Conceptually, the function of endpoint and time used here must be distinguished from the path functional $S[q]$ introduced in the first lesson.

## Hamilton–Jacobi and Separation {#S2}

Using the convention $p=\partial S/\partial q$, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+H\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right)=0.
$$

For an autonomous one-dimensional system try $S(q,t)=W(q;E)-Et$. The characteristic function $W$ then satisfies 

$$
H\left(q,\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dq}\right)=E.
$$

For $H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$ this gives $\mathrm dW/\mathrm dq=\pm\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}$ in classically allowed regions. Integration over $q$ yields $W$ up to a constant. A complete solution with appropriate integration constants can reconstruct the trajectories. Turning points require changes of square-root branch; one branch alone does not describe a full periodic orbit.

## Example: Harmonic Oscillator Action Variable {#S3}

For a closed phase-space orbit define the action variable

$$
J=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,\mathrm dq.
$$

For the oscillator $H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2q^2/2=E$, the orbit is an ellipse with semiaxes $q_{\max}=\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}$ and $p_{\max}=\sqrt{2mE}$. Its enclosed area is $\pi q_{\max}p_{\max}=2\pi E/\omega$, so 

$$
J=\frac{E}{\omega}.
$$

For $m=1\,\mathrm{kg}$, $\omega=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $E=0.5\,\mathrm J$, we obtain $J=0.25\,\mathrm{J\,s}$. The figure displays the ellipse and its area $2\pi J$. It visualizes an action variable rather than asserting a new energy law.

*The enclosed area equals 2πJ.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. State the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$.
2. Calculate $J$ at $E=1.2\,\mathrm J$ and $\omega=3\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
3. What is the enclosed area of the corresponding $q$-$p$ phase-space ellipse?

### Solutions

1. $\partial_tS+(\partial_qS)^2/(2m)+V(q)=0$.
2. $J=E/\omega=0.4\,\mathrm{J\,s}$.
3. $A=2\pi J=0.8\pi\,\mathrm{J\,s}\approx2.513\,\mathrm{J\,s}$.

## Scope and Open Systems {#S5}

Action-angle variables are especially effective for sufficiently integrable bound motions that can be decomposed into appropriate periodic cycles. Chaotic, open and strongly nonintegrable systems generally lack global action-angle coordinates. Even integrable models may require local treatment near turning points, singularities and multivalued functions. Under slowly varying parameters, $J$ can be approximately adiabatically invariant when additional conditions hold, but resonance and separatrix crossings can invalidate the approximation. The later appearance of $\hbar$ in quantum mechanics does not mean that the present classical integral is quantized without further assumptions.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
Hamilton–Jacobi theory completes the transition from Newton’s force law to a geometric phase-space picture and prepares subsequent dynamical systems and quantum theory.
