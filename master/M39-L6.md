# M39-L6 – Langevin Dynamics, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process and Fokker–Planck {#L6}

**Course area:** M39 – Master: Biophysics, Nonequilibrium and Complex Systems

**Level:** Master · advanced specialization

**Prerequisites:** M39-L1: diffusion; M39-L5: dissipation; M13: statistics · [Previous lesson](M39-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Relate drift, noise and Fokker–Planck probability flux consistently and calculate stationary OU variance.

## Guiding question and context {#S1}

A particle in a harmonic trap is subject to restoring drift and thermal kicks. Instead of an individual random trajectory, one can describe its probability density. Linear drift and additive white Gaussian forcing define the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Its stationary width must be distinguished carefully between variance and standard deviation.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

The Itô equation $dx=-\gamma x\,dt+\sqrt{2D}\,dW_t$ for $\gamma,D>0$ gives the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_t p=\partial_x(\gamma x p)+D\partial_x^2p=-\partial_xJ$, where $J=-\gamma xp-D\partial_xp$. Requiring vanishing flux at infinite boundaries yields $p_{\mathrm{st}}(x)\propto\exp[-\gamma x^2/(2D)]$. Stationary variance equals $\mathrm{Var}(x)=D/\gamma$ and standard deviation is $\sqrt{D/\gamma}$. This assumes constant $D$, no extra drive and a normalizable density.

$$
\partial_t p=\partial_x(\gamma xp)+D\partial_x^2p,\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{st}}(x)=\frac D\gamma.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

Choose illustrative $\gamma=0.5\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $D=2\,\mathrm{\mu m^2s^{-1}}$. Then $D/\gamma=4\,\mathrm{\mu m^2}$, $\sigma_x=2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and mean relaxation time $\tau=1/\gamma=2\,\mathrm s$. Starting at mean $\langle x(0)\rangle=3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ gives $\langle x(2\,\mathrm s)\rangle=3e^{-1}\,\mathrm{\mu m}\approx1.10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The figure shows the normalized stationary density.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is stationary variance when $D$ is doubled?
2. What is relaxation time if $\gamma$ doubles?
3. Which term represents diffusion in the Fokker–Planck equation?

### Solutions

1. $8\,\mathrm{\mu m^2}$.
2. $1\,\mathrm s$.
3. $D\partial_x^2p$.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The OU stationary distribution is Gaussian only under the stated conditions. State-dependent diffusion, nonlinear drift, absorbing boundaries and jump processes can have different stationary densities. A Fokker–Planck equation requires initial and boundary conditions; zero current is not a universal boundary choice.
