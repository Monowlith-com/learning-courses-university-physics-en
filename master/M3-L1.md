# M3-L1 – Kinematics in One and Two Dimensions {#L1}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Vectors, derivatives, elementary integrals and SI units. [Previous lesson](M2-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish vector position, velocity and acceleration and calculate projectile motion.

## Describe Motion Without Specifying Its Cause {#S1}

**Kinematics** describes how a body's position changes with time; the forces responsible are addressed later in dynamics. In the point-particle model, position is a vector $\mathbf r(t)$, with components depending on the selected reference frame. The displacement $\Delta\mathbf r=\mathbf r(t_2)-\mathbf r(t_1)$ is different from the distance travelled: after one complete circle the displacement vanishes, but the path length does not. **Velocity** and **acceleration** are the time derivatives of position and velocity, respectively. Motion in two or three dimensions can therefore be analysed component by component.

## Differentiating and Integrating Motion {#S2}

In a chosen inertial frame, the fundamental relations are

$$
\mathbf v(t)=\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf r}{\mathrm dt},\qquad
\mathbf a(t)=\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf v}{\mathrm dt},\qquad
\Delta\mathbf r=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathbf v(t)\,\mathrm dt.
$$

For constant acceleration $\mathbf a$, integrating twice gives

$$
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_0+\mathbf a t,\qquad
\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0t+\tfrac12\mathbf a t^2.
$$

The expression uses $t=0$ as the chosen starting time; for a different starting instant, replace $t$ by $t-t_0$. Dimensional analysis checks the terms: both $\mathbf v_0t$ and $\tfrac12\mathbf a t^2$ have units of length. A negative component specifies direction relative to an axis, not necessarily a reduction in speed.

## Worked Example: Oblique Projectile {#S3}

A ball is launched from $x=y=0$ at $v_0=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\alpha=30^\circ$. Let $x$ point horizontally and $y$ upward. Neglect drag and assume constant $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$. Then

$$
x(t)=v_0\cos\alpha\,t,\qquad
y(t)=v_0\sin\alpha\,t-\tfrac12gt^2.
$$

Setting $y(T)=0$ gives the nonzero flight time $T=2v_0\sin\alpha/g\approx2.04\,\mathrm s$. The range is $R=v_0\cos\alpha\,T\approx35.3\,\mathrm m$; maximum height occurs at $T/2$. The curved path does not require a horizontal force. It results from the superposition of constant horizontal velocity and accelerated vertical motion.

*In ideal projectile motion horizontal velocity is constant while vertical velocity decreases.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Position is $x(t)=2t^2-3t+1$ metres with $t$ in seconds. Find $v(2)$ and $a(2)$.
2. For the projectile above, find the initial velocity in the $y$ direction and the instant when it vanishes.
3. Why is the magnitude of average velocity not always the average speed?

### Solutions

1. $v(t)=4t-3$ and $a(t)=4$, giving $v(2)=5\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $a(2)=4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
2. $v_{0y}=20\sin30^\circ=10\,\mathrm{m/s}$; $v_y=10-9.81t=0$ at $t\approx1.02\,\mathrm s$.
3. Average vector velocity uses displacement, whereas average speed uses total distance travelled.

## Scope and Choice of Frame {#S5}

The constant-acceleration equations do not describe every motion. If $\mathbf a$ varies with time or position, one must integrate the corresponding function or solve an equation of motion. Drag, wind and changing altitude modify projectile results. Measurements should therefore begin by specifying axes, origin, units and modelling assumptions. A passenger's velocity relative to a train differs from their velocity relative to the platform; the reference frame is part of a meaningful physical statement.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Kinematics links observations to mathematical models of motion. Next we explain acceleration in terms of forces.
