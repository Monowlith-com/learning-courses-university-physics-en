# M3-L6 – Rotation, Torque and Moment of Inertia {#L6}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Forces, circular motion, work and energy. [Previous lesson](M3-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Model rotational motion with moment of inertia and torque and apply the rolling constraint.

## Angular Rather Than Linear Quantities {#S1}

For rigid rotation about a fixed axis, the angular position $\varphi$, angular velocity $\omega=\mathrm d\varphi/\mathrm dt$ and angular acceleration $\alpha=\mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dt$ describe motion. A point at distance $r$ from the axis moves at speed $v=r\omega$. Points farther from the axis therefore move faster at the same angular velocity. **Torque** depends on both force and lever arm: a tangential force produces more torque than an equal radial force at the same point.

## Torque, Inertia and Energy Balance {#S2}

The torque of a force about a reference point is

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F,\qquad
I=\int r_\perp^2\,\mathrm dm,\qquad
K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12 I\omega^2.
$$

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed principal axis with constant $I$, $\sum\tau=I\alpha$. More generally $\sum\boldsymbol\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}=\mathrm d\mathbf L/\mathrm dt$. Moment of inertia is not a universal number attached to a mass: it depends on the distribution of mass relative to the chosen axis. For a uniform solid disk about its symmetry axis, $I=\tfrac12MR^2$, giving $K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac14MR^2\omega^2$.

## Worked Example: Torque and Acceleration {#S3}

A tangential force $F=3.0\,\mathrm N$ acts at the rim of a uniform disk with $M=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $R=0.20\,\mathrm m$. Ignore bearing friction. The torque is $\tau=RF=0.60\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The moment of inertia about the centre is

$$
I=\tfrac12MR^2=0.040\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},\qquad
\alpha=\frac{\tau}{I}=15\,\mathrm{rad/s^2}.
$$

Starting from rest, the disk reaches angular velocity $30\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ after $2.0\,\mathrm s$ while the force remains constant. A body rolling without slipping also satisfies the kinematic condition $v_{\mathrm{CM}}=R\omega$. This does not imply constant $\omega$ in every rolling motion.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A tangential force of $5\,\mathrm N$ acts at $0.3\,\mathrm m$ from an axis. Find the torque.
2. Find the moment of inertia of a uniform solid disk with $M=4\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $R=0.5\,\mathrm m$.
3. A wheel with $R=0.25\,\mathrm m$ rolls without slipping at $v_{\mathrm{CM}}=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find $\omega$.

### Solutions

1. $\tau=rF=1.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}$.
2. $I=MR^2/2=0.50\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.
3. $\omega=v_{\mathrm{CM}}/R=8.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.

## Why the Axis Matters {#S5}

Torques and angular momenta must refer to the same origin or axis; otherwise balances become inconsistent. For a moving axis or asymmetric rotation, the scalar formula $\tau=I\alpha$ may be insufficient; one must use the vector angular-momentum equation and sometimes the inertia tensor. Real wheels also experience deformation, rolling resistance and occasional slip, none of which are included in the ideal rolling constraint.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Torque is the rotational counterpart of force, while moment of inertia measures resistance to angular acceleration. Gravitation follows next.
