# M9-L7 – Poisson Brackets and Canonical Transformations {#L7}

**Course area:** M9 – Bachelor: Analytical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Hamilton’s equations, partial derivatives and Jacobian matrices. [Previous lesson](M9-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Evaluate the Poisson bracket and test canonical structure for a momentum shift.

## Changing Coordinates Without Losing Dynamics {#S1}

Not every change of variables in phase space is equivalent. We seek transformations $(q,p)\mapsto(Q,P)$ that preserve the canonical form of Hamilton’s equations. These are called canonical or symplectic transformations. They can simplify the dynamical description without changing its physical content. The Poisson bracket is a basic tool for analyzing such transformations. It combines functions on phase space and also describes their evolution along Hamiltonian trajectories. The symmetric appearance of position and momentum equations reflects a specific geometric structure that an arbitrary nonlinear substitution can destroy.

## Definition and Basic Identities {#S2}

For one canonical pair the Poisson bracket is

$$
\{f,g\}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q}.
$$

For multiple degrees of freedom sum over $i$. Direct calculation gives $\{q,p\}=1$, $\{q,q\}=\{p,p\}=0$ and $\{f,g\}=-\{g,f\}$. Along a Hamiltonian trajectory, any quantity $f(q,p,t)$ satisfies 

$$
\frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm dt}=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.
$$

A time-independent $f$ is conserved when $\{f,H\}=0$. A regular local transformation is canonical when $\{Q_i,P_j\}=\delta_{ij}$ and $\{Q_i,Q_j\}=\{P_i,P_j\}=0$.

## Example: Canonical Shear {#S3}

Consider the transformation $Q=q$, $P=p+\alpha q$ with constant $\alpha$. Its derivatives are $\partial Q/\partial q=1$, $\partial Q/\partial p=0$, $\partial P/\partial q=\alpha$, and $\partial P/\partial p=1$. Therefore

$$
\{Q,P\}=1\cdot1-0\cdot\alpha=1.
$$

The figure shows how a small rectangle in $(q,p)$ coordinates becomes a parallelogram under this shear. Its area is unchanged because the Jacobian determinant is one. For $\alpha=2$ in compatible units, point $(q,p)=(1,3)$ maps to $(Q,P)=(1,5)$. The inverse is $p=P-\alpha Q$, allowing the Hamiltonian to be re-expressed in the new canonical coordinates.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute $\{q^2,p\}$.
2. Verify $\{Q,P\}$ for $Q=q$, $P=p+3q$.
3. Where does $(q,p)=(2,1)$ map for $P=p+3q$?

### Solutions

1. $\{q^2,p\}=2q$.
2. $\{Q,P\}=1$, so the transformation is canonical.
3. $(Q,P)=(2,7)$.

## Structural Preservation and Caveats {#S5}

Area preservation alone does not characterize a general transformation as symplectic when there is more than one degree of freedom: the full symplectic structure must be preserved. In a two-dimensional phase space, orientation-preserving area preservation has a particularly close relation to canonicity. Time-dependent canonical transformations alter the new Hamiltonian by additional terms. A Poisson bracket is not an ordinary vector cross product; it has bilinearity, a product rule and the Jacobi identity. Liouville’s phase-volume conservation applies to sufficiently regular Hamiltonian flows, not to generic frictional dissipative dynamics.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Poisson brackets describe symplectic evolution. Hamilton–Jacobi theory seeks an especially useful canonical description.
