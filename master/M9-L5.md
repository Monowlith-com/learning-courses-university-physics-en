# M9-L5 – Small Oscillations and Normal Modes {#L5}

**Course area:** M9 – Bachelor: Analytical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Taylor expansion, matrix eigenvalues and harmonic motion. [Previous lesson](M9-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Set up linearized motion using mass and stiffness matrices and calculate two normal modes.

## Motion Near an Equilibrium {#S1}

A mechanical system with many coordinates generally has coupled nonlinear equations of motion. For small displacements $\boldsymbol\eta=\boldsymbol q-\boldsymbol q_0$ from a stable equilibrium $\boldsymbol q_0$, approximate the potential by a Taylor expansion. The linear term vanishes at equilibrium, while the Hessian of the potential determines local stiffness. Inertia is represented by a mass matrix. Normal-mode analysis resolves the coupled small motions into characteristic independent patterns. A normal mode is a fixed spatial pattern whose amplitude oscillates harmonically in time. The method explains molecular vibrations, mechanical resonators and elastic structures within the linear regime.

## Mass and Stiffness Matrices {#S2}

For small displacements the Lagrangian is approximately $L=\tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}^{\mathsf T}M\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}-\tfrac12\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}K\boldsymbol\eta$ with symmetric $M$ and $K$. Euler–Lagrange yields

$$
M\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta}+K\boldsymbol\eta=0.
$$

A harmonic trial solution $\boldsymbol\eta=\boldsymbol a\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ gives $(K-\omega^2M)\boldsymbol a=0$. Nonzero amplitudes exist when 

$$
\det(K-\omega^2M)=0.
$$

For positive-definite $M$ and positive-semidefinite $K$, squared eigenfrequencies are nonnegative. Under appropriate conditions the mode vectors can be chosen orthogonal in the mass inner product. A zero mode corresponds to marginal or free motion, not a positive oscillation frequency.

## Example: Two Coupled Masses {#S3}

Two equal masses $m$ are attached to outer springs of stiffness $k$ and connected to each other by a spring of stiffness $\kappa$. For displacements $x_1,x_2$ from equilibrium,

$$
M=m\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{pmatrix}k+\kappa&-\kappa\\-\kappa&k+\kappa\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The in-phase mode $\boldsymbol a_+=(1,1)^\mathsf T$ does not stretch the coupling spring, so $\omega_+^2=k/m$. The out-of-phase mode $\boldsymbol a_-=(1,-1)^\mathsf T$ stretches it, so $\omega_-^2=(k+2\kappa)/m$. For $m=1\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=4\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and $\kappa=3\,\mathrm{N/m}$, the angular frequencies are $\omega_+=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_-=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What equation determines the eigenfrequencies?
2. Calculate $\omega_+$ for $m=2\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $k=8\,\mathrm{N/m}$.
3. How does positive coupling stiffness $\kappa$ affect the out-of-phase mode?

### Solutions

1. $\det(K-\omega^2M)=0$.
2. $\omega_+=\sqrt{8/2}=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
3. It increases $\omega_-^2$ by $2\kappa/m$ relative to the in-phase mode.

## Stability and Limits of Linearization {#S5}

Linearization assumes sufficiently small excursions around the chosen equilibrium and, in this example, ideal massless linear springs. At large displacements, nonlinear coupling may exchange energy between modes, invalidating a fixed normal-mode solution. Negative eigenvalues of $M^{-1}K$ produce exponential growth, indicating linear instability rather than a real oscillation frequency. Damping modifies the modes, and nonproportional damping can require complex eigenvalues. Zero modes must be interpreted carefully: they may arise from symmetry, missing supports or a marginally stable configuration.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Normal modes emerge from a linear eigenvalue problem. The same dynamics can instead be expressed using position and momentum.
