# M23-L5 – Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy and Distance Dependence {#L5}

**Course area:** M23 – Bachelor: Applied Physics, Photonics and Nanotechnology

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M12: tunnelling; M14: surface states; M16: measurement. [Previous lesson](M23-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain exponential tunnel-current sensitivity to distance and calculate an example distance change.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

A scanning tunnelling microscope (STM) approaches a conductive tip very close to a metallic or suitable semiconducting surface. With a bias applied, electrons tunnel through the narrow vacuum gap. Current is extremely sensitive to separation, allowing a feedback controller to construct image contrast. Atomic-resolution imaging is not a direct photograph of hard spheres: the current depends on local electronic states as well as the geometry.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

For a one-dimensional rectangular barrier of effective height $\Phi$ above the particle energy, the evanescent wavefunction behaves as $\psi(z)\propto e^{-\kappa z}$. Probability density scales as amplitude squared, and under suitable simplifying assumptions so does the tunnelling current, yielding

$$
I(z)\propto e^{-2\kappa z},\qquad \kappa\simeq\frac{\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}}{\hbar}\quad\text{(rectangular barrier approximation)}.
$$

At small bias and assuming a slowly varying density of states, the formula describes only the dominant distance dependence. Actual currents also reflect bias, tip shape, tunnelling matrix elements and local electronic structure. In constant-current mode, the feedback loop adjusts the tip height to hold current fixed. The resulting height record represents electronic topography within the model, not necessarily atom-by-atom geometric positions. Too small a gap can alter tip or sample.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

For an effective barrier height $\Phi=4.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ with the free electron mass, $\kappa\approx10.25\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. Increasing the gap by $\Delta z=0.10\,\mathrm{nm}$ reduces the current by $e^{-2\kappa\Delta z}\approx0.129$. Thus an atomic-scale distance change can nearly reduce current by an order of magnitude. The figure depicts an ideal exponential and a tip–sample schematic, not real STM data.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $I_2/I_1$ for $\kappa=10\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ and $\Delta z=0.05\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. Find the distance increase reducing current tenfold at $\kappa=10\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$.
3. Why is an STM image not automatically a geometric height map?

### Solutions

1. $I_2/I_1=e^{-1}\approx0.368$.
2. $\Delta z=\ln(10)/(20)\,\mathrm{nm}\approx0.115\,\mathrm{nm}$.
3. Current contrast also depends on electronic states and the condition of the tip.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The calculation models a flat 1D barrier with one effective height. Quantitative STM spectra require a better electronic model and strict control of mechanical noise and tip condition. The illustration is not an actual atomic image.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Exponential distance dependence makes STM highly sensitive, but interpreting its contrast requires distinguishing geometrical and electronic contributions.
