# M26-L5 – Gaussian functional integrals and discretized field models {#L5}

**Course area:** M26 – Master: Advanced mathematical methods and model foundations

**Level:** Master · methodological specialisation

**Prerequisites:** M13: partition functions; M26-L4: stationary phase; linear algebra. [Previous lesson](M26-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Evaluate a finite Gaussian integral, determine its covariance and identify the additional issues posed by continuum functional integrals.

## Guiding question and observation {#S1}

Field theory and statistical physics often speak of integrating over all configurations. Such notation is initially formal. Control can be gained by discretizing a spatial field into finitely many degrees of freedom and working with ordinary multidimensional integrals.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

Let a real field have $N$ discrete degrees of freedom with $S_N(x)=\tfrac12x^TAx$, where $A$ is symmetric positive definite. The Euclidean weight $e^{-S_N(x)}$ defines a normalizable Gaussian measure. Orthogonal diagonalization decomposes the integral into independent normal modes. The matrix $A^{-1}$ gives the two-point covariance. A genuine continuum functional integral requires additional work on measures, limits and often renormalization.

$$
Z_N=\int_{\mathbb R^N}e^{-\frac12x^TAx}\,\mathrm d^Nx=\frac{(2\pi)^{N/2}}{\sqrt{\det A}},\qquad \langle x_ix_j\rangle=(A^{-1})_{ij}.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

Take $A=\operatorname{diag}(2,8)$ with $N=2$. Then $\det A=16$ and $Z_2=(2\pi)/4=\pi/2$. The covariances are $\langle x_1^2\rangle=1/2$, $\langle x_2^2\rangle=1/8$ and $\langle x_1x_2\rangle=0$. The figure shows level sets of $S_2$; the narrow direction has the smaller variance.

![Elliptical level sets of a two-dimensional quadratic action with Gaussian widths differing in two directions.](../../../../media/course/U_PHY_112_EN.svg)

*Diagonalizing a quadratic action separates the integral into independently integrable modes.*

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $Z_1$ for $A=(4)$.
2. Find $\langle x_1^2\rangle$ for $A=\operatorname{diag}(4,9)$.
3. Why does the formula fail when $A$ has a negative eigenvalue?

### Solutions

1. $Z_1=\sqrt{\pi/2}$.
2. $1/4$.
3. The weight grows exponentially in one direction and the real integral diverges.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The formula is a finite Euclidean integral with positive-definite $A$, not a generally defined Minkowski path integral. Functional determinants and continuum limits are beyond this lesson.

## Summary and outlook {#S6}

The Gaussian case clarifies the connection among quadratic actions, normal modes and correlations. Fluctuations can next be modeled as stochastic processes evolving in time. [Next lesson](M26-L6.md).
