# M20-L5 – Galaxy Dynamics, Rotation Curves and Dark Matter {#L5}

**Course area:** M20 – Bachelor: Astrophysics and Cosmology

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M3: circular motion; M9: gravitational potential; M16: measurement models. [Previous lesson: Compact Stars, Black Holes and Accretion](M20-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Translate a rotation curve into an idealised mass model and identify its geometric limitations.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

Stars and gas clouds in spiral galaxies do not orbit a single dominant central mass in the same way as planets in a simple solar-system model. Doppler observations can yield rotational speed as a function of distance from the galactic centre, subject to viewing geometry. In many galaxies, outer rotation speeds remain higher than simple models of the visible stars and gas would predict. A spatially extended gravitating component, often modelled as a dark-matter halo, is a major explanation within the standard cosmological framework.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

For a spherical mass distribution and circular orbits, force balance gives $v_c^2/r=GM(<r)/r^2$, hence
$$
M(<r)=\frac{v_c^2r}{G}.
$$
If enclosed mass became nearly constant at large radii, then $v_c\propto r^{-1/2}$. An approximately flat curve $v_c\approx\mathrm{const}$ instead corresponds to $M(<r)\propto r$ in this spherical approximation. Galactic discs are not spherical: the formula is useful for scale estimates but is not an exact mass-recovery algorithm.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

Take a synthetic rotation curve with $v_c=200\,\mathrm{km/s}$ at $r=10\,\mathrm{kpc}$. Using $1\,\mathrm{kpc}\approx3.0857\times10^{19}\,\mathrm m$, the spherical approximation gives
$$
M(<10\,\mathrm{kpc})\approx1.85\times10^{41}\,\mathrm{kg}\approx9.3\times10^{10}M_\odot.
$$
Both plotted curves are synthetic. A declining baryonic-model curve and an outer flat total-speed curve illustrate the diagnostic principle, not measurements of a particular galaxy.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $M(<r)$ scale for a flat rotation curve?
2. What happens to $M(<r)$ at half the speed and the same radius?
3. Why is the spherical formula not exact for a thin disc?

### Solutions

1. In the spherical model, $M(<r)\propto r$.
2. It falls to a quarter because $M\propto v_c^2$.
3. A disc generates a different spatial gravitational-field geometry.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

Line-of-sight velocities require correction for galaxy inclination. Non-circular motions, gas pressure, warped discs, stellar populations, gas and mass-to-light ratios create further uncertainties. Rotation curves provide strong evidence for a mass discrepancy under specified dynamics; they do not directly reveal the microscopic identity of dark matter or logically settle every alternative dynamical model by themselves.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

A rotation curve is a dynamical measurement; its mass interpretation depends on geometry and modelling.
