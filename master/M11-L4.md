# M11-L4 – Atomic Energy Levels: Bohr Model and Franck–Hertz Experiment {#L4}

**Course area:** M11 – Bachelor: Foundations of Quantum Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Coulomb force, circular motion, photon energy and electronvolts; M3 and M11-L2. [Previous lesson](M11-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate hydrogen energy levels and spectral lines, and explain discrete collision excitation in the Franck–Hertz experiment.

## Discrete Energies Instead of Arbitrary Orbits {#S1}

A dilute hot gas does not emit all wavelengths equally; discrete spectral lines point to energy differences between allowed atomic states. The Bohr model combines Coulomb attraction with an additional angular-momentum quantization condition. It explains hydrogen line frequencies remarkably well, but does not give a complete account of probability distributions or spin. The Franck–Hertz experiment probes the same level structure without relying solely on optical spectroscopy: accelerated electrons collide with gas atoms and can transfer well-defined excitation energies once a threshold is reached.

## Quantization and Spectral Lines {#S2}

For an electron in the proton’s Coulomb field, Bohr assumed $m_\mathrm evr=n\hbar$ for $n=1,2,\ldots$, together with $m_\mathrm ev^2/r=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$. These give $r_n=a_0n^2$ and

$$
E_n=-\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^2}
$$

A transition $n_i>n_f$ emits a photon with $h\nu=E_{n_i}-E_{n_f}$. Its wavelength follows from $hc/\lambda=13.6\,\mathrm{eV}(1/n_f^2-1/n_i^2)$. Absorption reverses the energy flow. Fixed nuclear position and neglected fine structure are approximations: precise line spectra involve reduced mass, relativity and quantum electrodynamics.

## Calculation and Collision Excitation {#S3}

For the hydrogen transition $n_i=3\to n_f=2$, the emitted energy is $\Delta E=13.6\,(1/4-1/9)\,\mathrm{eV}\simeq1.889\,\mathrm{eV}$, corresponding to $\lambda\simeq1240/1.889\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq656\,\mathrm{nm}$: the red H-alpha line. In a typical mercury Franck–Hertz experiment, characteristic collector-current features are separated by roughly $4.9\,\mathrm V$, corresponding to electronic excitation near $4.9\,\mathrm{eV}$. Absolute peak positions depend on contact potentials, retarding voltage, pressure and tube geometry; the *spacing* is usually more informative. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $E_2$ and $E_3$ for hydrogen.
2. What energy corresponds to a $4.9\,\mathrm V$ potential difference for one electron?
3. Why is $n=1$ the lowest circular state in Bohr’s model?

### Solutions

1. $E_2=-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $E_3\simeq-1.51\,\mathrm{eV}$.
2. $e\Delta U=4.9\,\mathrm{eV}$.
3. The condition $mvr=n\hbar$ allows positive integer $n$; $n=0$ is not a stationary circular orbit of the model.

## Why the Bohr Model Is Not the Final Theory {#S5}

Bohr’s circular orbits must not be interpreted as directly observed electron tracks. In Schrödinger quantum mechanics stationary states are described by orbitals and probability densities. Bohr’s model is especially useful for hydrogen-like one-electron systems but misses many details of multielectron atoms. Similarly, the elementary Franck–Hertz account captures discrete energy transfer, while actual currents depend on scattering cross sections and transport. Together these results motivate the state-and-operator description developed in quantum mechanics.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Discrete spectra and collision excitation support atomic energy levels; a complete explanation requires quantum mechanics.
