# M5-L7 – Phase Changes, Latent Heat and Phase Equilibrium {#L7}

**Course area:** M5 – Bachelor: Heat and Thermodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Gibbs energy, entropy change and the first law. [Previous lesson](M5-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Read phase diagrams and derive the relation between coexistence curves and latent heat.

## Equilibrium Between Phases {#S1}

A pure substance may exist as solid, liquid or gas at different combinations of pressure and temperature. Along a **coexistence curve**, two phases are in equilibrium at the same temperature and pressure; their molar Gibbs energies, or chemical potentials, are equal. A phase diagram maps stable equilibrium states; it is not automatically a time history of the transformation. A triple point is a specific pair of $T$ and $p$ at which three phases coexist. During a first-order transition at fixed pressure, temperature stays constant in the idealised quasistatic equilibrium limit even while energy is transferred.

## Latent Heat and Coexistence Slope {#S2}

For a first-order transition the molar latent heat supplied at constant pressure is $L=T\Delta s$, where $\Delta s=s_\beta-s_\alpha$ is the molar entropy difference. Along the coexistence curve $g_\alpha(T,p)=g_\beta(T,p)$ and $dg=-s\,dT+v\,dp$, so differentiation gives

$$
0=-\Delta s\,dT+\Delta v\,dp,\qquad\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{L}{T\Delta v}.
$$

This is the Clapeyron equation. $\Delta v=v_\beta-v_\alpha$ must be a molar volume difference consistent with molar $L$. The familiar approximation $d\ln p/dT\approx L/(RT^2)$ for vaporisation also assumes ideal vapour and negligible liquid volume. 

## Worked Example: Water Vaporisation {#S3}

For water near $100\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and standard pressure, use illustrative values $L\approx40.7\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$, $T\approx373\,\mathrm K$ and $\Delta v\approx0.030\,\mathrm{m^3/mol}$ for liquid to vapour. Clapeyron's equation then gives approximately

$$
\frac{dp}{dT}\approx\frac{40\,700}{373\cdot0.030}\approx3.64\times10^3\,\mathrm{Pa/K}.
$$

This local estimate applies over a narrow temperature interval only. Accurate calculations require vapour tables and temperature-dependent phase properties. The illustration is a schematic $p$-$T$ phase diagram with a triple and a critical point; it is explicitly not to scale. Unlike many substances, water has a negatively sloping solid–liquid coexistence line in the usual $p$-$T$ representation because of its density anomaly.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What does equality of the molar Gibbs energies of two phases mean?
2. Find $\Delta s$ for $L=40.7\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ and $T=373\,\mathrm K$.
3. What is the sign of $dp/dT$ for melting of a material with $\Delta v<0$ and $L>0$?

### Solutions

1. The phases may coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium.
2. $\Delta s=L/T\approx109\,\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$.
3. Clapeyron gives $dp/dT<0$, as for the water melting curve in the relevant region.

## Critical Point and Model Limits {#S5}

Real materials may show nucleation barriers, supersaturation and metastable states that are not captured by a simple equilibrium map. The liquid–gas coexistence curve terminates at the critical point; beyond it, a sharp distinction between liquid and gas disappears. Models such as van der Waals display qualitative coexistence but are often quantitatively poor near real critical points. Latent heat must be specified either per unit mass in $\mathrm{J/kg}$ or per mole in $\mathrm{J/mol}$; confusing the two produces incorrect numbers. A flat transition temperature is conditional on fixed external pressure.
