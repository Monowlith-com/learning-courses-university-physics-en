# M15-L2 – Radioactive Decay and Half-Life {#L2}

**Course area:** M15 – Bachelor: Nuclear and Particle Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M11: quantum states; M13: probability; M15-L1: nuclides. [Previous lesson](M15-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the decay law and activity and interpret statistical statements correctly.

## Random Decay and Decay Modes {#S1}

Radioactive decay is a quantum transition of an unstable nuclear state. Alpha decay emits a $^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$ nucleus; beta-minus decay converts a neutron into a proton while emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. An excited nucleus may emit a gamma photon without changing its mass number. The decay time of an individual nucleus cannot be predicted from how long it has already existed. In an ensemble of identical independent nuclei with time-independent decay constant $\lambda$, the probability of decay in a short time interval is approximately $\lambda\,dt$. When several decay channels exist, their partial decay constants add to the total $\lambda$.

## Differential Equation and Activity {#S2}

The expected remaining count $N(t)$ obeys $dN/dt=-\lambda N$. Separation and integration yield

$$
N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.
$$

Activity is the mean number of decays per second: $A(t)=-dN/dt=\lambda N(t)$, measured in becquerels, $1\,\mathrm{Bq}=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Half-life does not depend on the initial amount. Mean lifetime is $\tau=1/\lambda$, not $T_{1/2}$. Daughter products in a radioactive chain generally require coupled differential equations rather than a single independent exponential.

## Example: Two Half-Lives {#S3}

Take half-life $T_{1/2}=5.0\,\mathrm{h}$ and initial activity $A_0=800\,\mathrm{Bq}$. After $t=10.0\,\mathrm{h}$ exactly two half-lives have elapsed:

$$
A(t)=A_0 2^{-t/T_{1/2}}=200\,\mathrm{Bq}.
$$

The decay constant is $\lambda=\ln 2/(5\,\mathrm h)\approx0.1386\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$. The graph shows normalized activity dropping to $1/2$, $1/4$ and $1/8$ after one, two and three half-lives. These are ensemble expectations; observed counts from a weak source fluctuate around their mean.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $N/N_0$ after three half-lives.
2. Calculate $\lambda$ for $T_{1/2}=2\,\mathrm d$.
3. What is the ideal counting uncertainty for 100 events?

### Solutions

1. $1/8=0.125$.
2. $\lambda=0.3466\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$.
3. $\sigma\approx10$ counts, relative uncertainty about $10\%$.

## Counting Statistics and Model Limits {#S5}

Independent rare detected events approximately follow Poisson statistics over a fixed observation window. If the expected count is $\mu$, the standard deviation is $\sqrt\mu$, before background corrections and under idealized detection. Efficiency, dead time, background, shielding and physical losses influence measurements. A long half-life does not automatically mean small activity at every sample mass or low biological hazard. Exponential decay assumes no replenishment and a constant decay rate. Irradiation-induced nuclear reactions should be distinguished from spontaneous decay.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Decay law describes ensemble expectations; activity and registered rate differ for real detectors.
