# M10-L2 – Time-Dependent Potentials and Gauge Freedom {#L2}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Maxwell equations, divergence, curl and wave equation. [Previous lesson](M10-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Relate scalar and vector potentials and distinguish Lorenz from Coulomb gauge.

## Why One Scalar Is No Longer Enough {#S1}

When magnetic fields change with time, Faraday’s law gives $\nabla\times\boldsymbol E=-\partial_t\boldsymbol B$, generally nonzero. The electric field $\boldsymbol E$ can therefore no longer be reconstructed from a purely static scalar potential. Since $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0$, one may locally write $\boldsymbol B=\nabla\times\boldsymbol A$. Together with a scalar potential $\phi$, this produces a linked pair of field definitions. Potentials are useful mathematical variables, but not merely typography: their global structure can affect observable phases in quantum mechanics. The treatment here is classical and begins within a simply connected region.

## Potentials and Gauge Freedom {#S2}

The complete relations are

$$
\boldsymbol B=\nabla\times\boldsymbol A,\qquad\boldsymbol E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\boldsymbol A.
$$

For any sufficiently smooth function $\chi(\boldsymbol r,t)$, the replacements $\boldsymbol A'=\boldsymbol A+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi$ leave the fields invariant: $\nabla\times\nabla\chi=0$ and the extra mixed derivatives in $\boldsymbol E$ cancel. The Lorenz condition is $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0$. In vacuum with sources, it gives 

$$
\Box\phi=-\rho/\varepsilon_0,\qquad\Box\boldsymbol A=-\mu_0\boldsymbol J,
$$

where $\Box=\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2$. Coulomb gauge instead imposes $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol A=0$, offering different computational properties.

## Example: Two Potential Pairs for the Same Fields {#S3}

Start with $\phi=0$ and $\boldsymbol A=\boldsymbol 0$, so both fields vanish. A gauge function $\chi(x,t)=axt$, for constant $a$ with the appropriate units, gives $\boldsymbol A'=at\,\boldsymbol e_x$ and $\phi'=-ax$. Therefore $-\nabla\phi'=a\boldsymbol e_x$, whereas $-\partial_t\boldsymbol A'=-a\boldsymbol e_x$; in total $\boldsymbol E'=0$. Likewise $\boldsymbol B'=\nabla\times\boldsymbol A'=0$. Two visibly different sets of potentials describe exactly the same classical fields. The diagram contrasts potential representations while retaining an identical physical field.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. State the gauge transformation of $\boldsymbol A$ and $\phi$.
2. For $\chi(x,t)=axt$, test whether transformed $\boldsymbol E$ vanishes.
3. What condition defines Lorenz gauge?

### Solutions

1. $\boldsymbol A'=\boldsymbol A+\nabla\chi$, $\phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi$.
2. Yes: $-\nabla(-ax)-\partial_t(at\boldsymbol e_x)=0$.
3. $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0$.

## Gauges, Causality and Limitations {#S5}

In Lorenz gauge the potentials obey wave equations, for which retarded solutions connect measured fields to sources at earlier times. An apparently instantaneous Coulomb scalar potential does not transmit a signal faster than light: the vector-potential contribution must be included in the physical field. Gauge transformations are not Lorentz changes of inertial frame; the similar names are easy to confuse. In nontrivial topology a single smooth global potential may not exist even if local expressions are valid. Any concrete calculation also requires explicit initial and boundary conditions.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Gauge choices change potential descriptions, not the fields. In source-free space the same Maxwell equations support electromagnetic waves.
