# M10-L3 – Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum {#L3}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Faraday’s law, Ampère–Maxwell law and vector identities. [Previous lesson](M10-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the wave equation from Maxwell and relate polarization, frequency and wavelength.

## Fields with No Local Sources {#S1}

An electromagnetic wave requires no mechanical medium. In a source-free vacuum region, electric and magnetic field changes are coupled in space and time through Maxwell’s equations. For a plane free-space wave, both fields are transverse to the propagation direction. The observation $\boldsymbol E\perp\boldsymbol B$ does not imply two independent oscillators: it describes a single electromagnetic phenomenon. Plane waves are idealized, however. A finite laser beam or pulse has a limited spatial extent and can be described by superposing appropriate modes.

## Deriving the Wave Equation {#S2}

In source-free vacuum, $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E=0$, $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B=0$, $\nabla\times\boldsymbol E=-\partial_t\boldsymbol B$ and $\nabla\times\boldsymbol B=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\boldsymbol E$. Taking the curl of Faraday’s equation gives

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol E)=-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t^2\boldsymbol E.
$$

Using $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E)-\nabla^2\boldsymbol E$ yields 

$$
\nabla^2\boldsymbol E-\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\boldsymbol E=0,\qquad c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}.
$$

The magnetic field $\boldsymbol B$ satisfies the same wave equation.

## Example: Field Amplitude and Wavelength {#S3}

A linearly polarized wave traveling in the $+x$ direction may be written as

$$
\boldsymbol E=E_0\cos(kx-\omega t)\boldsymbol e_y,\quad\boldsymbol B=\frac{E_0}{c}\cos(kx-\omega t)\boldsymbol e_z.
$$

Here $\omega=ck$ and $\lambda=c/f$. At $f=100\,\mathrm{MHz}$, with $c\approx3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$, the wavelength is $\lambda\approx3.00\,\mathrm m$. If $E_0=300\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the magnetic amplitude is $B_0\approx1.00\,\mathrm{\mu T}$. The figure places the two field directions at right angles at a common phase; propagation is along neither $\boldsymbol E$ nor $\boldsymbol B$.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Which Maxwell equation contains the term with $\partial_t\boldsymbol E$?
2. Calculate $\lambda$ at $f=50\,\mathrm{MHz}$.
3. Find $B_0$ when $E_0=600\,\mathrm{V/m}$.

### Solutions

1. The Ampère–Maxwell law.
2. $\lambda=c/f\approx6.0\,\mathrm m$.
3. $B_0=E_0/c\approx2.0\,\mathrm{\mu T}$.

## Transversality and the Approximation {#S5}

Transversality also follows from $\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol E_0=0$ and $\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol B_0=0$. Faraday’s law relates their amplitudes through $\boldsymbol B_0=\boldsymbol k\times\boldsymbol E_0/\omega$. Any linear superposition of appropriate solutions satisfies the source-free linear equations. In matter, dispersion, absorption and longitudinal components can appear; a finite beam is not completely described by a single pair of infinite sinusoidal curves. The vacuum light speed is the propagation speed of free plane waves, not automatically the energy or group velocity in every medium.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Maxwell equations permit wave propagation and fix the field amplitudes. The fields also transport energy and momentum.
