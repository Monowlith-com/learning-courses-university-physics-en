# M1-L1 – Physics as Testable Modelling {#L1}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor: Foundations of Physics

**Level:** Bachelor · Introductory

**Prerequisites:** School mathematics: powers and square roots.

**Learning objective:** Derive a testable model prediction from an observed phenomenon and plan a model test.

## From Observation to Question {#S1}

Two pendulums are displaced at the same time. Which property determines which one first returns to its starting position? Physics does not answer such questions merely by collecting similar observations. It formulates a relationship between measurable quantities and tests it against new observations.

A system is the part of the world we investigate. Its surroundings can interact with it. We must decide which influences to include explicitly in our model and which to neglect initially.

## Model, Prediction and Test {#S2}

An observation describes an event; a model idealises causes and relationships. A hypothesis is a testable conjecture. Quantitative predictions follow from a mathematical model and can be compared with measured data. Agreement supports the model within the tested range but does not establish unrestricted validity.

Consider a simple pendulum: point mass $m$, massless inextensible suspension of length $\ell$, constant local gravitational acceleration $g$, no drive and negligible friction. For the angle $\theta$ measured from the vertical, the tangential component of gravity gives

$$
m\ell\ddot\theta=-mg\sin\theta.
$$

At small angles in radians, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$. Hence $\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\theta\approx0$. The harmonic solution gives the period

$$
T\approx 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}.
$$

Mass cancels. A testable prediction is therefore $T^2\propto\ell$; a graph of $T^2$ against $\ell$ should be close to a straight line through the origin if the assumptions hold.

## Example: Two Pendulum Lengths {#S3}

For $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\ell_1=0.25\,\mathrm m$, we obtain $T_1\approx1.003\,\mathrm s$. At $\ell_2=1.00\,\mathrm m$, $T_2\approx2.006\,\mathrm s$. Within the small-angle model, the ratio is exact:

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\sqrt{\frac{1.00}{0.25}}=2.
$$

To test the model, choose several lengths and time ten complete oscillations together for each length. Determine $T$ from the total time. Comparable small initial angles and the same measurement method make a systematic discrepancy easier to identify. The numbers above are predictions, not measurements.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A pendulum length is tripled. How does the period change?
2. Why is a single matching data point not a convincing test of the length dependence?
3. Name a controllable influence absent from the period formula.

### Solutions

1. $T_2/T_1=\sqrt3\approx1.732$.
2. A single point cannot distinguish many competing functional relationships. Measurements at different lengths test the dependence.
3. The initial displacement angle: as it increases, the small-angle approximation becomes less accurate.

## Domain of Validity {#S5}

Discrepancies can indicate measurement uncertainty, incorrect parameters or missing physics. Model error is not the same as measurement error. At large angles, the nonlinear equation containing $\sin\theta$ must be used. Damping, finite dimensions of the pendulum bob and a moving suspension are other possible extensions. A suitable test separates these influences as far as possible.

## Summary {#S6}

Physical modelling connects system boundaries, assumptions, predictions and measurements. A good prediction has a clear domain of validity and can fail when confronted with independent data. The next lesson introduces the common language of physical quantities.
