# M14-L2 – X-ray Diffraction, Bragg Condition and Structure Factor {#L2}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M14-L1, wave interference and complex amplitudes. [Previous lesson](M14-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Relate Bragg peaks to the Laue condition and derive simple extinction rules from the atomic basis.

## Interference of Scattered Waves {#S1}

Coherent X-rays scatter from the spatially periodic electron density of a crystal. For two parallel lattice planes separated by $d$, the path difference in the ideal mirror geometry is $2d\sin\theta$, with $\theta$ defined between the incident ray and the plane. Constructive interference requires an integer number of wavelengths. The Bragg condition is

$$
2d\sin\theta=n\lambda.
$$

Experiments often report the scattering angle $2\theta$ between incident and outgoing beams. This elementary condition locates possible maxima, not their intensities. A powder pattern combines many randomly oriented crystallites, and measured line profiles also reflect the instrument, finite grains and strain.

## Momentum Transfer and the Basis {#S2}

In vector form the scattering momentum transfer is $\mathbf q=\mathbf k_{\mathrm{out}}-\mathbf k_{\mathrm{in}}$. Elastic scattering implies $\lvert\mathbf k_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert=\lvert\mathbf k_{\mathrm{in}}\rvert=2\pi/\lambda$, and lattice interference requires

$$
\mathbf q=\mathbf G.
$$

For atoms of the basis at positions $\mathbf r_j$, with scattering factors $f_j$, the structure factor $F_{hkl}=\sum_j f_j e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_j}$ gives the amplitude of an allowed reflection. In a simple kinematic treatment its intensity is proportional to $\lvert F_{hkl}\rvert^2$ after geometry and experimental effects are included. Two identical point scatterers at $(0,0,0)$ and $(a/2,a/2,a/2)$ give $F=f[1+(-1)^{h+k+l}]$. Reflections with odd $h+k+l$ then cancel in this ideal model.

## Example: Plane Spacing {#S3}

For a simple cubic lattice the $(100)$ plane separation is $d_{100}=a$. Taking $a=0.30\,\mathrm{nm}$ and monochromatic wavelength $\lambda=0.15\,\mathrm{nm}$, first-order diffraction yields

$$
\theta=\arcsin\frac{\lambda}{2d}=\arcsin(0.25)\approx14.48^\circ.
$$

Under these assumptions a diffractometer records $2\theta\approx28.96^\circ$. The diagram illustrates the beam paths schematically, not to scale. At fixed wavelength, smaller plane spacing shifts a peak toward higher angles. However, an absent peak does not necessarily mean that a plane family is absent: the structure factor may extinguish its intensity.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the first-order Bragg angle for $d=0.20\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda=0.10\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. Is $(110)$ extinguished for the stated two-point basis?
3. What determines peak intensity in the kinematic model?

### Solutions

1. $\theta=\arcsin(0.25)\approx14.48^\circ$.
2. No; $1+1+0=2$ is even, so $F=2f$.
3. Mainly $\lvert F_{hkl}\rvert^2$ together with geometric and experimental factors.

## Limits of Kinematic Diffraction {#S5}

Bragg's construction assumes coherent, elastic scattering at a well-defined wavelength. Thick nearly perfect crystals or strongly interacting waves can require dynamical diffraction with multiple scattering. Thermal motion reduces elastic intensities, for example through the Debye–Waller factor. The formula $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ is specific to cubic lattices, not a universal relation for oblique cells. Diffraction order must not be counted twice when higher reciprocal vectors are already indexed as different $(hkl)$ values. Neutron and electron diffraction also use reciprocal space, although their interactions and scattering amplitudes differ.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Diffraction angles follow reciprocal-lattice conditions; the structure factor adds interference within the basis.
