# M26-L6 – Langevin dynamics, the Fokker–Planck equation and relaxation {#L6}

**Course area:** M26 – Master: Advanced mathematical methods and model foundations

**Level:** Master · methodological specialisation

**Prerequisites:** M13: statistical physics; M17: numerical integration; M22: diffusion. [Previous lesson](M26-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the Fokker–Planck equation, stationary variance and relaxation time from a linear stochastic model.

## Guiding question and observation {#S1}

Microscopic collisions may make a macroscopic signal irregular. Instead of resolving every interaction, a stochastic model separates systematic drift from random fluctuations. A Langevin equation describes individual trajectories; the Fokker–Planck equation describes the changing probability distribution of an ensemble.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

For an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process in the Itô sense, $dX_t=-\gamma X_tdt+\sqrt{2D}\,dW_t$, with $\gamma>0$, $D>0$ and Brownian motion $W_t$. Drift and diffusion produce the forward equation $\partial_tp=\gamma\partial_x(xp)+D\partial_x^2p$. Setting the stationary probability current $j=-\gamma xp-D\partial_xp$ to zero yields a normalized Gaussian distribution. Its variance is $D/\gamma$, while the mean displacement relaxes exponentially.

$$
\mathrm dX_t=-\gamma X_t\,\mathrm dt+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm dW_t,\quad \partial_tp=\gamma\partial_x(xp)+D\partial_x^2p,\quad \operatorname{Var}_{\mathrm{stat}}(X)=\frac D\gamma.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

For $\gamma=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $D=0.5\,u^2\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ where $u$ is an arbitrary measurement unit, the stationary variance is $0.25\,u^2$, the standard deviation $0.5\,u$ and the relaxation time $\tau=1/\gamma=0.5\,\mathrm s$. The plot shows three independently simulated trajectories and the zero line, not experimental measurements.

![Three simulated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck time series fluctuating around a zero mean while relaxing.](../../../../media/course/U_PHY_142_EN.svg)

*Stochastic paths fluctuate individually; the stationary ensemble has a calculable variance.*

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate stationary variance for $D=0.8\,u^2/\mathrm s$ and $\gamma=4/\mathrm s$.
2. What happens to $\tau$ when $\gamma$ doubles?
3. Why can $\mathrm dW_t/\mathrm dt$ not be treated as an ordinary differentiable function?

### Solutions

1. $0.2\,u^2$.
2. The time constant is halved.
3. Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere classically differentiable; stochastic integration requires its own calculus.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The model assumes additive white noise, linear restoring drift and time-independent coefficients. Discrete simulations approximate the stochastic equation; one trajectory does not establish the distribution.
