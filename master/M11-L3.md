# M11-L3 – Matter Waves and Electron Diffraction {#L3}

**Course area:** M11 – Bachelor: Foundations of Quantum Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Momentum, kinetic energy, interference and the Bragg condition; M3 and M7. [Previous lesson](M11-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the de Broglie relation, compute electron wavelengths, and physically interpret diffraction patterns.

## Why an Electron Can Interfere {#S1}

An electron reaches a detector as an individual localized event. Yet under coherent conditions, many electrons passing through a double slit or scattering from a crystal form an interference pattern in the spatial distribution of detection events. These fringes are not drawings of classical trajectories; they represent relative detection frequencies across repeated trials. Even when practically one electron is in the apparatus at a time, the pattern builds statistically. A crystal provides regularly spaced scattering centres. Localized detections alongside distributed amplitudes are a central starting point for quantum mechanics.

## Momentum, Wave Number and Accelerating Voltage {#S2}

De Broglie relates wavelength $\lambda$ to momentum magnitude $p$:

$$
\lambda=\frac hp,\qquad p=\hbar k,\qquad\hbar=\frac h{2\pi}.
$$

For a nonrelativistic electron accelerated from rest through potential difference $U$, energy conservation gives $eU=p^2/(2m_\mathrm e)$. Substitution yields 

$$
\lambda=\frac h{\sqrt{2m_\mathrm e eU}}.
$$

This approximation requires $eU\ll m_\mathrm ec^2\simeq511\,\mathrm{keV}$; higher accelerating voltages call for relativistic energy and momentum. Constructive scattering by ideal parallel lattice planes satisfies $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$. The Bragg condition follows from the path difference of reflected contributions from adjacent planes.

## Example: Electron Diffraction by a Crystal {#S3}

For electrons accelerated through $U=150\,\mathrm V$, the nonrelativistic approximation gives $\lambda\simeq0.100\,\mathrm{nm}$. With lattice-plane separation $d=0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$, first-order diffraction requires $\sin\theta=\lambda/(2d)\simeq0.250$, or $\theta\simeq14.5^\circ$. In the Bragg diagram this is the angle relative to a lattice plane, not directly the deflection angle between incoming and outgoing rays; in the usual reflection geometry that angle is $2\theta$. The figure shows adjacent lattice planes and the additional path $2d\sin\theta$. A real crystal also requires attention to lattice orientation, Ewald construction and scattered intensity.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What happens to $\lambda$ when $U$ rises from $150$ to $600\,\mathrm V$?
2. With $d=0.2\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda=0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ find the first-order Bragg angle.
3. What does one detection event reveal about the full interference distribution?

### Solutions

1. Since $\lambda\propto U^{-1/2}$, wavelength halves to about $0.050\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. $\sin\theta=0.1/0.4=0.25$, hence $\theta\simeq14.5^\circ$.
3. A single detection reveals one position only; the distribution is inferred from many identically prepared measurements.

## Detection Probability and Model Limits {#S5}

In a simple single-particle description, detection intensity is proportional to the modulus square of a complex wavefunction, $P\propto|\psi|^2$. A wavefunction is not a directly visible material wave with a classical measurable height. Interference weakens when paths become distinguishable through their environment and relative phase information is lost. Thus particle and wave aspects are not simply additive classical properties. Strongly interacting electrons, thick samples and inelastic scattering need more advanced models than two paths and a single Bragg condition.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Matter waves link momentum to wavelength; interference patterns emerge from probability amplitudes.
