# M20-L7 – Cosmic Expansion, Redshift and Background Radiation {#L7}

**Course area:** M20 – Bachelor: Astrophysics and Cosmology

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M10: electromagnetic waves; M13: blackbody statistics; M20-L1: distance ladder. [Previous lesson: Exoplanets, Transit Photometry and Detection Limits](M20-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Use small cosmological redshifts as approximate distance indicators and explain the cosmic microwave background.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

Light from distant galaxies reaches us after travelling through an evolving universe. Spectral features are observed at longer wavelengths on average because the cosmic scale factor changes between emission and observation. This is not simply an ordinary object moving through static space. Another key observable is the nearly isotropic cosmic microwave background, with a remarkably thermal spectrum and small anisotropies. Observation, data reduction and cosmological interpretation must be kept distinct.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

Redshift is defined by
$$
1+z=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{em}}}=\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_{\mathrm{em}})}.
$$
For sufficiently small cosmological redshifts one may use $v\approx cz$ heuristically and $v\approx H_0d$ as the local Hubble–Lemaître relation. Combining them gives $d\approx cz/H_0$ only in the low-$z$, approximately linear regime. At high redshift the distance definition depends on a cosmological expansion model. The cosmic microwave background closely resembles blackbody radiation with $T\approx2.73\,\mathrm K$.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

A hypothetical galaxy has $z=0.023$. Taking an **illustrative, not measured here**, Hubble constant of $H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ gives $cz\approx6900\,\mathrm{km/s}$ and $d\approx98.5\,\mathrm{Mpc}$. This is not a precise distance determination and ignores peculiar motions. The illustration shows a synthetic linear relation and a qualitative thermal microwave spectrum associated with $2.73\,\mathrm K$.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $z$ for $\lambda_{\mathrm{em}}=500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}=550\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. Find $d\approx cz/H_0$ for $z=0.010$ and $H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$.
3. Why must $v=cz$ not be treated as an ordinary relative speed at $z\sim1$?

### Solutions

1. $z=550/500-1=0.10$.
2. $d\approx(2998/70)\,\mathrm{Mpc}\approx42.8\,\mathrm{Mpc}$.
3. Because large cosmological redshifts require a time-dependent expansion model.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The chosen Hubble constant is a rounded teaching value, not a claim about the currently preferred measured value. Peculiar motions affect the low-distance approximation. The background spectrum alone does not provide a model-free current age of the universe; parameter inference requires a jointly fitted theory and complementary observations. The plotted curve is synthetic, not a reproduction of an observational catalogue.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Cosmological redshift and the microwave background are central observables, interpreted through an explicit model.
