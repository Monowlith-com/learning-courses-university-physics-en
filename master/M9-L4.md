# M9-L4 – Two-Body Problem, Effective Potential and Kepler Orbits {#L4}

**Course area:** M9 – Bachelor: Analytical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and angular momentum, polar coordinates. [Previous lesson](M9-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Reduce the two-body problem to a reduced mass and characterize bound central-force orbits.

## Relative Motion of Two Masses {#S1}

Two freely gravitating bodies mutually influence their common motion. Instead of following six Cartesian coordinates directly, separate the center of mass $\boldsymbol R=(m_1\boldsymbol r_1+m_2\boldsymbol r_2)/(m_1+m_2)$ from relative position $\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol r_1-\boldsymbol r_2$. The center of mass of the isolated system moves at constant velocity. Relative motion is mathematically equivalent to a single fictitious reduced mass $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ in potential $V(r)=-Gm_1m_2/r$. The reduced mass is not a third physical body but a computational device. It matters especially when both bodies have comparable masses, for which a fixed-central-body approximation is inadequate.

## Radial Dynamics and Effective Potential {#S2}

Planar relative motion has Lagrangian

$$
L=\frac\mu2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\varphi^2)+\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}.
$$

The conserved angular momentum magnitude is $\ell=\mu r^2\dot\varphi$. Substituting $\dot\varphi=\ell/(\mu r^2)$ into the energy gives 

$$
E=\frac\mu2\dot r^2+V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r),\qquad V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=\frac{\ell^2}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}.
$$

The first term is an angular-momentum barrier and the second is gravitational attraction. The minimum is at $r_0=\ell^2/(\mu Gm_1m_2)$, defining a stable circular orbit for fixed $\ell>0$. Bound negative-energy trajectories in the inverse-square field are ellipses with the center of force at one focus.

## Example: Circular Orbit in Gravity {#S3}

When $m_2\ll m_1=M$, the approximate circular speed at radius $r$ is $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$ and the orbital period is

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}.
$$

For $GM=3.986\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $r=7.00\times10^6\,\mathrm m$, $v\approx7.55\times10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $T\approx5.83\times10^3\,\mathrm s$, about $97.1$ minutes. This is an ideal two-body model with no drag, oblateness or perturbing bodies. The figure shows an elliptical orbit with its force center at a focus and a schematic effective-potential minimum below. These represent different aspects of the same central-force system.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\mu$ for $m_1=2\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $m_2=3\,\mathrm{kg}$.
2. At fixed $M$, how does circular orbital period depend on $r$?
3. What is the areal velocity at fixed angular momentum?

### Solutions

1. $\mu=2\cdot3/(2+3)=1.2\,\mathrm{kg}$.
2. $T\propto r^{3/2}$; doubling radius multiplies period by $2^{3/2}\approx2.828$.
3. $\dot A=\ell/(2\mu)$, independent of radius.

## Orbit Types and Model Limitations {#S5}

The Kepler solution presumes Newtonian inverse-square ($\propto 1/r^2$) gravity between point masses in an isolated two-body system. Finite size, tides, additional gravitating bodies, relativistic corrections and nongravitational forces modify the trajectory. With $\ell=0$ the angular-momentum barrier disappears and radial collisions need separate treatment. Positive-energy trajectories are unbound hyperbolae; a parabola is the ideal limiting case at $E=0$. Constant areal velocity $\dot A=\ell/(2\mu)$ follows from any central force, but exact Kepler ellipses specifically require inverse-square attraction.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Center-of-mass reduction and conserved quantities simplify central motion. Linearized motion likewise leads to normal modes.
