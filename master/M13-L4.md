# M13-L4 – Grand Canonical Ensemble and Chemical Potential {#L4}

**Course area:** M13 – Bachelor: Statistical Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Canonical partition functions and Boltzmann factors from M13-L3; chemical potential from M5. [Previous lesson](M13-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Use grand canonical weights, chemical potential, and particle-number derivatives correctly.

## Particle Exchange with a Reservoir {#S1}

Many physical systems exchange both heat and particles with their surroundings. In the grand canonical ensemble a reservoir fixes temperature $T$, volume $V$, and chemical potential $\mu$; particle number $N$ fluctuates. A microstate $i$ in sector $N$ has statistical weight $e^{-\beta(E_{i,N}-\mu N)}$, where $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. Summing over all allowed particle-number sectors and states gives

$$
\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\sum_i e^{-\beta(E_{i,N}-\mu N)}.
$$

If the model has only finitely many occupancy sites, the outer sum is effectively restricted to the allowed particle numbers. Chemical potential governs the energy change upon particle addition under appropriate thermodynamic constraints; it is not an extra spatial force.

## Grand Potential and Occupancies {#S2}

The grand potential is $\Phi_G=-k_BT\ln\Xi$. For a simple homogeneous system it is related to $-pV$ in the thermodynamic limit. Mean particle number follows from a first derivative of the grand partition function, and its fluctuations from a second derivative:

$$
\langle N\rangle=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu},\qquad \mathrm{Var}(N)=\frac{1}{\beta^2}\frac{\partial^2\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu^2}.
$$

These identities hold at fixed $T,V$ and other Hamiltonian parameters. The thermodynamics is consistent with $d\Phi_G=-S\,dT-p\,dV-N\,d\mu$ for the corresponding simple single-component system. In a finite system, the most probable particle number need not equal its mean.

## Example: A Single Fermionic Level {#S3}

A single nondegenerate fermionic level of energy $\varepsilon$ can be empty ($N=0$) or singly occupied ($N=1$), by the Pauli exclusion principle. Thus

$$
\Xi=1+e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)},\qquad \langle N\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1}.
$$

At $\mu=\varepsilon$ the empty and occupied states are equally likely, giving $\langle N\rangle=1/2$. For $\varepsilon-\mu=2k_BT$ one obtains $\langle N\rangle\approx0.1192$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $\langle N\rangle$ of a fermionic level at $\varepsilon-\mu=0$.
2. Find $\Xi$ at $\varepsilon-\mu=k_BT\ln2$.
3. Which quantity fluctuates in the canonical but not in the microcanonical ensemble?

### Solutions

1. $\langle N\rangle=1/2$.
2. $\Xi=1+1/2=3/2$.
3. Energy; particle number remains fixed in both ensembles.

## Why the Three Ensembles Differ {#S5}

The microcanonical ensemble fixes energy and particle number; the canonical ensemble allows energy fluctuations at fixed $N$; the grand canonical ensemble lets both vary. Appropriate local observables often show ensemble equivalence in large systems with short-range interactions, but finite systems and fluctuation predictions still differ. A suitable reservoir coupling and conserved quantity must exist: in ordinary thermal radiation photon number is not conserved and the equilibrium photon chemical potential is zero. Grand canonical modeling is therefore a physical choice, not merely a mathematical convenience. The fermionic single-level occupancy formula must not be reused unchanged for bosons.
