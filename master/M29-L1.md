# M29-L1 – Equivalence principle and local inertial frames {#L1}

**Course area:** M29 – Master: General relativity and cosmology

**Level:** Master · advanced specialization

**Prerequisites:** M10: special relativity; M26: geometry; M28: dynamical models · [Previous lesson](M28-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish locally removable free-fall acceleration from residual tidal effects.

## Guiding question and context {#S1}

Why does a person in free fall feel weightless for a short interval while moving through a gravitational field? The equivalence principle states that physics in a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory agrees locally with physics in an inertial frame without gravity. An observer held at rest on the ground instead has a nonzero proper acceleration measured by an accelerometer.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

Near Earth’s surface a small laboratory can approximate the field as uniform with strength $g$. Two identical bodies initially at rest relative to the ground acquire speed $gt$ after time $t$ when drag is neglected. In a freely falling laboratory their relative acceleration is approximately zero. Comparing an elevator held at rest in gravity to a rocket accelerating upward at $g$ is only a **local** equivalence: the strength and direction of a real field vary across extended regions. These differences cause measurable relative accelerations and cannot be transformed away everywhere. For a small height difference $\Delta z$ in a static weak field, clock rates also differ by approximately $g\Delta z/c^2$.

$$
\frac{\Delta f}{f}\simeq\frac{g\,\Delta z}{c^2}\qquad (|g\Delta z|\ll c^2,\ \text{weak static field}).
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

For $\Delta z=10\,\mathrm m$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $c\approx2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$, we find $\Delta f/f\approx1.09\times10^{-15}$. The stationary clock at higher altitude runs slightly faster in this comparison. The drawing contrasts the local elevator/rocket analogy with a nonlocal setting distinguishable through field gradients.

*Acceleration can mimic uniform gravity locally; tidal effects mark the limit of the analogy.*

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Which of a freely falling clock and a clock supported on the ground has nonzero local proper acceleration?
2. How does $\Delta f/f$ change when $\Delta z$ doubles in the approximation?
3. Why can one coordinate choice not remove Earth’s gravitational effects everywhere?

### Solutions

1. The supported clock (and its accelerometer); the freely falling clock has approximately zero proper acceleration.
2. It doubles to about $2.18\times10^{-15}$.
3. Tidal effects from spatially varying gravity remain.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The calculation assumes small height separations, a static weak field, and stationary observers. The equivalence principle does not assert that gravity and acceleration are globally identical; tidal effects remain.
