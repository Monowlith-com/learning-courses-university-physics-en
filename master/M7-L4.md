# M7-L4 – Interference, Diffraction and Optical Gratings {#L4}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Harmonic waves, phase difference and small angles. [Previous lesson](M7-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish double- and single-slit conditions and analyse a grating.

## Superposition of Two Waves {#S1}

For harmonic fields, optical intensity is proportional to the time-averaged squared field amplitude. When two waves of equal frequency and polarization overlap, **fields** add before intensities do. For individual intensities $I_1$ and $I_2$ and relative phase $\delta$,

$$
I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta.
$$

Equal field amplitudes give an ideal zero minimum and a maximum four times the intensity of a single arm. In a double-slit arrangement with centre spacing $d$, the geometric path difference in far-field direction $\theta$ is $d\sin\theta$. Constructive interference occurs ideally when $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$, $m\in\mathbb Z$. Fringes result from the two sources, while the finite width of either slit adds a diffraction envelope.

## Interference and Diffraction as Phase Calculations {#S2}

For a slit of width $a$, continuously distributed elementary waves interfere. In the Fraunhofer regime the intensity is

$$
I(\theta)=I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,\qquad \beta=\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda},
$$

using the continuous limit at $\beta=0$. Dark minima occur at $a\sin\theta=m\lambda$ for integer $m\ne0$. A grating of many equally spaced slits has narrow principal maxima at $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$; that condition determines the *position* of principal orders, not automatically their strengths. With finite slit widths the grating peaks are modulated by the single-slit envelope. Fluctuating relative phase washes out the interference term in a long exposure.

## Worked Example: Fringe Spacing {#S3}

For two narrow slits, a screen at distance $L$ has position $x\approx L\theta$ at small angles. Fringe spacing is therefore $\Delta x\approx\lambda L/d$. With $\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}$, $L=2.0\,\mathrm m$, and $d=0.50\,\mathrm{mm}$, we obtain $\Delta x=2.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. A grating with $d=2.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ has its first principal order for $\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $\theta_1=\arcsin(0.30)\approx17.46^\circ$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute $\Delta x$ for $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$, $L=1\,\mathrm m$, $d=0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$.
2. Find the first single-slit minimum for $a=10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$.
3. Why are grating maxima not infinitely narrow, arbitrarily high lines?

### Solutions

1. $\Delta x=2.0\,\mathrm{mm}$.
2. $\sin\theta=0.05$, hence $\theta\approx2.87^\circ$.
3. Finite slit number and width produce finite angular widths and constrain peak intensity.

## Coherence and Finite Apertures {#S5}

Constructive interference requires a sufficiently well-defined phase relationship. Sunlight can interfere after spatial and spectral filtering, but two independent lamps cannot automatically be treated like a coherent two-arm source. Far-field formulas assume a detection geometry in which path differences can be expressed by an angular coordinate. A finite grating has peaks of finite width and secondary maxima. Polarization, detector sampling, stray light and slit imperfections also influence the observed pattern. Measured fringe positions must be analysed with both geometry and wavelength in mind.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Superposition yields double-slit fringes and their diffraction envelopes. Fourier optics generalizes this to arbitrary apertures.
