# M8-L4 – Laplace Transforms, Transfer Functions and Convolution {#L4}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Integrating factors, exponential functions, integration and complex numbers. [Previous lesson](M8-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Solve causal linear initial-value problems with Laplace transforms and interpret convolution.

## Causal Signals and the Transform Domain {#S1}

A dynamic experiment often starts at $t=0$: a switch is closed, a detector is pulsed or a driving input steps to a constant value. For these causal signals, the unilateral Laplace transform is useful. It maps a time trace $f(t)$ to $F(s)=\int_0^{\infty}f(t)e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt$. Here $s$ is generally complex, and the integral converges only in a specified region of the complex plane. For $f(t)=e^{-at}$ with $a>0$, the transform is $F(s)=1/(s+a)$ when $\operatorname{Re}(s)>-a$. Laplace and Fourier transforms are related, but they play different roles in initial-value analysis and stationary spectral descriptions.

## Derivative Rule and Convolution Theorem {#S2}

Integration by parts gives $\mathcal L\{f'\}(s)=sF(s)-f(0^+)$ for suitably regular functions. A constant-coefficient linear differential equation thereby becomes an algebraic equation for $F(s)$. For $y'+ay=u$ with $y(0)=0$, we obtain $Y(s)=U(s)/(s+a)$. The transfer function is $H(s)=1/(s+a)$. In the time domain, the convolution theorem states

$$
y(t)=(h*u)(t)=\int_0^t h(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau,\qquad h(t)=e^{-at}\quad(t\geq0).
$$

Thus the output sums delayed responses to all earlier input contributions. The impulse response is not an ordinary finite-duration input pulse, but the mathematically defined response to an ideal delta excitation.

## Example: Response to a Step {#S3}

Solve $y'+2y=1$ for $t>0$ with $y(0)=0$. The transform of the constant right-hand side is $1/s$. Thus $Y(s)=1/[s(s+2)]$. Partial fractions give $Y(s)=\tfrac12(1/s-1/(s+2))$, whose inverse transform is

$$
y(t)=\frac12(1-e^{-2t}).
$$

At $t=1\,\mathrm s$, $y\approx0.43233$ and the long-time value is $0.5$. The same function results by convolving impulse response $e^{-2t}$ with the constant input $u(t)=1$. The diagram contrasts the decreasing impulse response with the increasing step response. They share the time constant $\tau=0.5\,\mathrm s$, though their physical units need not agree under an arbitrary input normalization.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\mathcal L\{e^{-3t}\}(s)$.
2. Solve $y'+4y=1$ with $y(0)=0$.
3. Why is impulse response $h(t)$ useful for arbitrary inputs of linear time-invariant systems?

### Solutions

1. $1/(s+3)$ for $\operatorname{Re}(s)>-3$.
2. $y(t)=(1-e^{-4t})/4$.
3. For a causal system initially at rest and input $u(t)=0$ for $t<0$, the output is $y(t)=\int_0^t h(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau$.

## Poles, Stability and Modeling Conditions {#S5}

Transfer functions describe linear time-invariant systems with specified inputs and outputs. Pole positions and multiplicities encode homogeneous modes, but simplistic stability claims must account for possible pole-zero cancellations and internal states. The unilateral transform includes initial conditions, whereas the bilateral transform has a different definition. Ideal impulses and steps are naturally treated with distributions. Numerical inversion can be difficult, especially with noisy data; a measured frequency response by itself does not automatically yield a reliable causal model. A transfer function is a model representation, not a substitute for experimental validation.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Laplace transformation converts time derivatives and convolution into algebraic operations. Spatial dependence leads next to partial differential equations.
