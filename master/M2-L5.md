# M2-L5 – Partial Derivatives and the Multivariable Chain Rule {#L5}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Single-variable derivatives and vector components. [Previous lesson](M2-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply gradients, total differentials and the chain rule to field quantities.

## A Field at Every Point {#S1}

Temperature in a room depends on more than one coordinate: $T=T(x,y,z,t)$. The rate of change along $x$ may differ from that along $y$, while a moving sensor also experiences temporal change. Partial derivatives distinguish these effects. Taking a partial derivative means holding the other independent variables fixed. The gradient combines spatial rates of change into one vector.

## Gradient, Differential and Chain Rule {#S2}

For a differentiable scalar field $f(x,y,z)$,

$$
\nabla f=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_x f\\\partial_y f\\\partial_z f\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 df=\partial_x f\,dx+\partial_y f\,dy+\partial_z f\,dz.
$$

Along a path $\mathbf r(t)$, allowing explicit time dependence,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t),t)=\nabla f\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}+\partial_t f.
$$

The first term describes motion through spatial variations; the second, change at a fixed location. For a vector function $\mathbf g(\mathbf x)$, the Jacobian collects derivatives $J_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$. The matrix chain rule reads $D(\mathbf g\circ\mathbf h)=D\mathbf g(\mathbf h)\,D\mathbf h$ wherever the derivatives exist.

## Example: Temperature Along a Sensor Path {#S3}

Consider the time-independent, purely illustrative temperature field

$$
T(x,y)=20+2x-y^2,
\qquad (x,y)\ \mathrm{in\ m},\quad T\ \mathrm{in\ ^\circ C}.
$$

The coefficients $2$ and $-1$ carry appropriate units so the terms have dimensions of temperature. The gradient is $\nabla T=(2,-2y)^\mathsf T$ in $\mathrm{K/m}$. If a sensor follows $\mathbf r(t)=(t,1+t)^\mathsf T$ (positions in metres with $t$ measured in seconds), then

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=2\cdot1-2(1+t)\cdot1=-2t\quad\mathrm{K/s}.
$$

At $t=0$ the contributions cancel in this model. Even a time-independent temperature distribution may produce changing readings along a moving path.

*Separate graphical example: for $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$, the level curves are circles and the gradient $\nabla f=(2x,2y)$ is normal to them. These are not the level curves of the temperature field $T(x,y)=20+2x-y^2$ calculated above.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\nabla f$ for $f=x^2+3xy$.
2. Determine $d f/dt$ for $x=t$, $y=t^2$ and $f=x+y$.
3. What is the unit of the gradient of an electric potential in volts?

### Solutions

1. $\nabla f=(2x+3y,3x)^\mathsf T$.
2. $d f/dt=1+2t$.
3. $\mathrm{V/m}$; its negative is the electrostatic field.

## Coordinates, Units and Extrema {#S5}

In Cartesian coordinates, the gradient points in the direction of steepest increase per distance. That interpretation presumes a metric; component formulas change in curvilinear coordinates. A temperature gradient has units kelvin per metre (equivalently degrees Celsius per metre for temperature differences). For an interior local extremum, $\nabla f=0$ is necessary but not always sufficient: the Hessian characterises local curvature, and saddle points are possible.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Partial derivatives describe change along coordinate directions; the chain rule connects them to motion through a field. The next lesson combines spatial derivatives into divergence and curl.
