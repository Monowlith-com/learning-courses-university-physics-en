# M8-L6 – Sturm–Liouville Problems and Orthogonal Eigenfunctions {#L6}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Second-order differential equations, integration by parts and trigonometry. [Previous lesson](M8-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Determine eigenvalues and eigenfunctions with boundary conditions and compute orthogonality.

## Eigenvalues from Boundary Conditions {#S1}

For a stretched string with both ends fixed, not every spatial vibration profile is possible. Boundary conditions select discrete eigenfunctions. The same principle appears in heat conduction, acoustic cavities, electromagnetic resonators and quantum wavefunctions. A typical separated partial differential equation yields the spatial equation $-X''=\lambda X$. The parameter $\lambda$ cannot be chosen arbitrarily if $X(0)=X(L)=0$ and the solution is required to be nonzero. Eigenvalue problems connect differential equations to spectral expansions. Eigenvalues belong to an operator together with its domain and boundary conditions, not merely to the symbolic differential equation.

## A Self-Adjoint Differential Operator {#S2}

A regular Sturm–Liouville problem takes the form

$$
-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\!\left(p(x)\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\right)+q(x)y=\lambda w(x)y,
$$

with suitable real $p>0$, $w>0$ and self-adjoint boundary conditions. Multiply equations for two eigenfunctions $y_m,y_n$ crosswise, subtract and integrate over the interval. Integration by parts makes the boundary term vanish when the conditions are appropriate. It follows that $(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)\int_0^L w(x)y_m(x)y_n(x)\,\mathrm dx=0$. Distinct eigenvalues therefore imply orthogonality with respect to the weighted inner product: 

$$
\int_0^L w(x)y_m(x)y_n(x)\,\mathrm dx=0\quad(m\ne n).
$$

The weight function is an essential part of the problem and must not be silently removed during normalization.

## Example: Fixed Endpoints {#S3}

For $p=w=1$, $q=0$ and $y(0)=y(L)=0$, the problem is $-y''=\lambda y$. Nonzero solutions exist for

$$
\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2,\qquad y_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),\quad n=1,2,\ldots
$$

They are normalized by $\int_0^L y_m y_n\,\mathrm dx=\delta_{mn}$. For $L=2\,\mathrm m$, the fundamental eigenvalue is $\lambda_1=\pi^2/4\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}\approx2.4674\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}$. The figure shows the first three spatial eigenmodes, including their end nodes. The number of interior nodes increases with mode index. In the wave equation, these eigenvalues produce angular frequencies $\omega_n=c\sqrt{\lambda_n}$; in the heat equation, they control decay rates $\kappa\lambda_n$.

*Every eigenmode vanishes at the endpoints.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ for $L=1\,\mathrm m$?
2. Find the interior zeros of mode $n=3$ on $0<x<L$.
3. Why does the general inner product contain the factor $w(x)$?

### Solutions

1. $\lambda_2=4\pi^2\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}$.
2. $x=L/3$ and $x=2L/3$.
3. Orthogonality follows from the eigenvalue equation with weight $w$, not from an unweighted product.

## Weight Functions and Scope {#S5}

The convenient sine basis assumes constant coefficients and simple endpoint conditions. For spatially varying $p$, $q$ or $w$, the eigenfunctions are generally not pure sine waves. Completeness of an eigenbasis is a mathematical theorem with assumptions; it is not a consequence of orthogonality alone. Singular endpoints or non-self-adjoint conditions can produce continuous spectra or complex eigenvalues. Physical interpretation of the norm also depends on the units assigned to the state field. A measured profile can be approximated using eigenfunctions, but noise and finite spatial resolution limit the number of modes that can be reliably reconstructed.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Boundary conditions select an orthogonal set of spatial eigenmodes. Complex analysis provides another tool for evaluating certain integrals: residues.
