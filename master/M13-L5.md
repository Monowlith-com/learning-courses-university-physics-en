# M13-L5 – Fermi–Dirac Statistics and the Degenerate Electron Gas {#L5}

**Course area:** M13 – Bachelor: Statistical Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Grand canonical ensemble from M13-L4; energy levels and spin from M11. [Previous lesson](M13-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply the Fermi distribution, the Pauli principle, and the concept of Fermi energy.

## Why Identical Fermions Cannot Share a State {#S1}

Electrons are fermions of half-integer spin. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two identical fermions from occupying the same one-particle quantum state. Consequently, an ideal noninteracting fermion mode admits occupation numbers $n=0$ or $n=1$. Its grand canonical state weights add up to $1+e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$, giving the Fermi–Dirac mean occupation

$$
f_F(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1}.
$$

Here $\mu$ is the chemical potential, which need not equal the zero-temperature Fermi energy under general conditions. A spatial orbital may contain two electrons of opposite spin projection because these correspond to different one-particle states.

## Fermi Edge and Density of States {#S2}

As $T\to0$, the occupation function approaches a step: states below the Fermi energy are filled, those above it are empty. This is a consequence of Pauli exclusion, not an attractive thermal interaction between electrons. For a homogeneous three-dimensional nonrelativistic ideal electron gas with two spin states and number density $n=N/V$, state counting gives

$$
E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}.
$$

At $T=0$ this equals the ideal gas chemical potential. Such a gas retains finite electron kinetic energy and exerts degeneracy pressure even at zero temperature. The derivation counts approximately free momentum states and is not automatically valid for band electrons in an arbitrary crystal.

## Example: Occupation Near the Chemical Potential {#S3}

Setting one-particle energy equal to $\mu$ gives $f_F(\mu)=1/2$ at any finite temperature. For a level $2k_BT$ above $\mu$,

$$
f_F(\mu+2k_BT)=\frac{1}{e^2+1}\approx0.1192.
$$

Conversely a level $2k_BT$ below $\mu$ has occupation about $0.8808$. The diagram shows the Fermi edge, whose transition width is of order $k_BT$. A strongly degenerate Fermi gas requires $k_BT$ to be small compared with a characteristic Fermi energy. This regime often describes conduction electrons in metals, but a quantitative statement for a particular material requires its band structure and carrier density.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Evaluate $f_F(\mu)$ for $T>0$.
2. Find the occupation when $\varepsilon-\mu=k_BT\ln3$.
3. How does $E_F$ change if $n$ increases eightfold in a 3D ideal Fermi gas?

### Solutions

1. $f_F=1/2$.
2. $f_F=1/(3+1)=1/4$.
3. Since $E_F\propto n^{2/3}$, it rises by $8^{2/3}=4$.

## Limits of the Ideal Fermi Gas {#S5}

The ideal formula assumes independent one-particle modes and equilibrium. Electron–electron interactions, effective masses, band gaps, and disorder all affect real materials. In a semiconductor the chemical potential can lie in a gap containing no electronic states; the mathematical value $f_F(\mu)=1/2$ does not imply that an actual state in the gap is half occupied. One must also distinguish spatial orbitals from complete spin orbitals. The simple zero-temperature expression for $E_F$ cannot be transferred unchanged to relativistic particles, two-dimensional gases, or strongly interacting quasiparticles.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Fermion occupation is limited to one per spin orbital. Bosons follow another distribution and can accumulate macroscopically in one state.
