# M4-L8 – Viscosity, Laminar Flow and Reynolds Number {#L8}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Continuity, force balance and circular pipe geometry. [Previous lesson](M4-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive Poiseuille flow, calculate hydraulic resistance and interpret Reynolds number.

## Internal Friction in Fluids {#S1}

A real fluid transfers shear forces between adjacent layers. In a Newtonian fluid the local shear stress is proportional to velocity gradient, with dynamic viscosity $\eta$ as the proportionality factor. For laminar, fully developed flow, no slip at a stationary pipe wall is generally a good approximation: velocity vanishes at the wall and is largest in the centre. The **Poiseuille model** describes this smooth profile and makes the strong dependence of volume flow on pipe radius explicit.

## Hagen–Poiseuille Law {#S2}

For steady laminar, fully developed flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid through a long straight pipe of radius $R$ and length $L$, axial momentum balance produces a parabolic velocity profile. The volume flow rate is

$$
Q=\frac{\pi R^4\Delta p}{8\eta L},\qquad
v(r)=\frac{\Delta p}{4\eta L}(R^2-r^2).
$$

Here $\Delta p$ is the positive pressure drop across $L$, while $Q$ is volume per time. Hydraulic resistance $\mathcal R=\Delta p/Q=8\eta L/(\pi R^4)$ grows strongly as radius decreases. The formula does not automatically apply to turbulent flow, strongly varying viscosity or entrance regions. The dimensionless quantity $\mathrm{Re}=\rho v_{\mathrm{mean}}D/\eta$ compares inertial and viscous effects.

## Worked Example: Pipe Resistance {#S3}

For an idealised pipe with $R=1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, $L=1.0\,\mathrm m$, $\eta=1.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ and $\Delta p=1000\,\mathrm{Pa}$, Poiseuille's formula gives

$$
Q=\frac{\pi(10^{-3})^4(1000)}{8(10^{-3})(1.0)}\approx3.93\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^3/s}.
$$

Since $A=\pi R^2$, $v_{\mathrm{mean}}=Q/A\approx0.125\,\mathrm{m/s}$. With $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and $D=2R$, this yields $\mathrm{Re}\approx250$. For a typical smooth pipe this lies in the laminar regime, making the model assumptions at least plausible. The figure illustrates the parabolic profile and the zero-velocity wall boundary.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What happens to $Q$ if pipe radius doubles at constant $\Delta p$, $\eta$ and $L$?
2. Find $\mathrm{Re}$ for $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $v_{\mathrm{mean}}=0.20\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $D=0.010\,\mathrm m$ and $\eta=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$.
3. Which assumption is violated by strongly turbulent pipe flow?

### Solutions

1. $Q\propto R^4$; model volume flow becomes sixteen times as large.
2. $\mathrm{Re}=1000\cdot0.20\cdot0.010/0.001=2000$.
3. The assumption of steady, fully developed laminar Poiseuille flow does not hold.

## Reynolds Number Is Not a Universal Threshold {#S5}

Reynolds number expresses a ratio of characteristic terms, not a universal boundary between two states. Transition depends on wall roughness, inlet disturbances, geometry and other conditions. In a straight circular pipe, transitional behaviour often appears at Reynolds numbers of a few thousand, but there is no universally valid single value. Turbulence changes the time-averaged profile and introduces fluctuating quantities. The Hagen–Poiseuille equation should therefore always be used together with its assumptions, characteristic scales and flow regime.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Viscosity and geometry determine hydraulic resistance. Transitions and losses must be considered for real systems.
