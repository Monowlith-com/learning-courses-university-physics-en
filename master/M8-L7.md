# M8-L7 – Complex Analysis and the Residue Theorem {#L7}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Complex numbers, integration, differentiation and rational functions. [Previous lesson](M8-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Identify simple poles, calculate residues and evaluate a real integral by contour closure.

## Why Use the Complex Plane? {#S1}

Physics often involves integrals over frequency, momentum or energy that are cumbersome to evaluate directly on the real axis. Extending the variable to the complex plane lets poles and analytic domains encode important information about the integral. A complex variable $z=x+iy$ has two real components. Holomorphic functions possess a direction-independent complex derivative, permitting stronger integral results than are available for arbitrary real functions of two variables. Rational functions provide a concrete example: zeros of the denominator are isolated singularities in the complex plane. Which of these singularities lies inside an integration contour determines the contour integral. Complex methods do not, however, replace a careful check of limiting operations.

## Analytic Functions and Residues {#S2}

A function has a simple pole at $z_0$ when locally $f(z)=a_{-1}/(z-z_0)+a_0+a_1(z-z_0)+\cdots$. Coefficient $a_{-1}$ is the residue. At a simple pole it is often found from $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0)=\lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z)$. The residue theorem states

$$
\oint_{\mathcal C} f(z)\,\mathrm dz=2\pi i\sum_{z_k\,\mathrm{in}}\operatorname{Res}(f,z_k)
$$

for a positively oriented closed contour with no singularity on the contour and the usual analyticity assumptions. A real integral follows only if any auxiliary contour segment can be controlled and its contribution vanishes in the desired limit or can be included explicitly.

## Example: A Lorentzian Integral {#S3}

Evaluate $I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm dx/(x^2+1)$. Write $f(z)=1/[(z-i)(z+i)]$ and close the real axis with an upper semicircle of radius $R>1$. Only pole $z=i$ is enclosed, with $\operatorname{Res}(f,i)=1/(2i)$. The arc length is $\pi R$ and $|f(z)|\leq1/(R^2-1)$ on that arc, so its integral decreases at least as fast as $1/R$. Hence

$$
I=2\pi i\cdot\frac{1}{2i}=\pi.
$$

The figure marks the positive contour orientation and both poles; only $+i$ is inside the upper contour. Substituting $x=a u$ yields the general result $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a\,\mathrm dx/(x^2+a^2)=\pi$ for $a>0$.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the poles of $1/(z^2+4)$.
2. Compute the residue of $1/(z^2+4)$ at $z=2i$.
3. Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2\,\mathrm dx/(x^2+4)$.

### Solutions

1. $z=2i$ and $z=-2i$.
2. $1/(4i)$.
3. $\pi$, by substitution or residues.

## Choosing a Contour and Avoiding Errors {#S5}

The contour must be chosen for the integrand. In Fourier integrals containing $e^{ikz}$, the sign of $k$ and exponential decay govern whether an upper or lower half-plane contour is appropriate. A pole on the real path requires a separate integration prescription, such as a defined principal value or an explicit pole displacement. Branch points require cuts rather than simple residue sums. Even for rational functions one must verify that the semicircular arc contributes nothing; the present estimate cannot be copied to integrands that decay more slowly. Mathematical conventions determine the orientation and can change apparent signs or factors of $2\pi$.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Residues exploit analytic structure to compute integrals. A related idea for differential operators is to construct their response to a point source as a Green function.
