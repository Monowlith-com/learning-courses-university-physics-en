# M3-L4 – Work, Energy and Potential {#L4}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Newton’s laws, the dot product and integration. [Previous lesson](M3-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Relate work to kinetic energy and use conservative potentials to analyse motion.

## Why Energy Is Another Language of Mechanics {#S1}

Forces describe instantaneous acceleration, while **energy** often connects initial and final states directly. Work is not simply a force magnitude times an arbitrary distance; it is the line integral of the force component along the actual displacement. For a constant-mass point particle, the total work equals the change in kinetic energy. The energy approach may avoid solving a full time-dependent trajectory, although energy alone generally does not give the travel time.

## Work–Energy Theorem and Conservative Forces {#S2}

For a force $\mathbf F$ acting along a path $C$,

$$
W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf r,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{tot}}=\Delta K,\qquad K=\tfrac12 mv^2.
$$

In a suitable domain, a force is **conservative** if its work depends only on the endpoints. Then a potential $U$ exists such that $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$ and $W_{\mathrm{cons}}=-\Delta U$. If all relevant forces are conservative, $E=K+U$ is constant. With nonconservative work, instead $\Delta(K+U)=W_{\mathrm{nc}}$. Power is the rate of doing work: $P=\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$ for the instantaneous force.

## Worked Example: Spring and Turning Points {#S3}

An ideal spring follows $F_x=-kx$ and has potential energy $U(x)=\tfrac12 kx^2$. A mass $m=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ is displaced by $A=0.10\,\mathrm m$ on a spring with $k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and released from rest. Neglecting friction,

$$
E=\tfrac12kA^2=1.00\,\mathrm J.
$$

At equilibrium $x=0$, the potential is $U=0$, so $\tfrac12mv_{\max}^2=E$ and $v_{\max}=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. At the turning points $x=\pm A$, velocity is zero. On a potential-energy diagram, the horizontal total-energy line intersects the potential at precisely those points. Total energy stays constant even while it alternates between kinetic and potential forms.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. A constant force of $10\,\mathrm N$ acts parallel to a displacement of $3\,\mathrm m$. Find its work.
2. A $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ mass falls from rest through $5.0\,\mathrm m$ without drag. Find its speed just before reaching the ground.
3. For the spring example, find $U$ at $x=0.05\,\mathrm m$ and the remaining kinetic energy.

### Solutions

1. $W=Fd=30\,\mathrm J$.
2. From $mgh=\tfrac12mv^2$, $v=\sqrt{2gh}\approx9.90\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
3. $U=\tfrac12\cdot200\cdot0.05^2=0.25\,\mathrm J$, so $K=1.00-0.25=0.75\,\mathrm J$.

## Friction and the Choice of System {#S5}

Mechanical energy is not always conserved. Sliding friction converts some of it into internal energy of the interacting bodies. The **system boundary** is therefore crucial: total energy remains conserved for a suitably closed system even as the mechanical component $K+U$ decreases. The zero of a potential may be chosen freely; physically relevant quantities are potential differences. A potential minimum corresponds to locally stable equilibrium when the remaining model assumptions hold.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Work transfers energy; conservative forces admit a potential description. For collisions, momentum provides another key tool.
