# M2-L8 – Theorems of Gauss and Stokes {#L8}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Divergence, curl, line and surface integrals. [Previous lesson](M2-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Connect flux and circulation integrals using the theorems of Gauss and Stokes.

## From Local Fields to Global Balances {#S1}

Divergence quantifies local sources and sinks, while curl quantifies local circulation. Two fundamental integral theorems connect these local properties to measurements at boundaries: net flux across a closed surface and circulation around the edge of a surface. In physics they connect local field equations to measurable integral statements, for example in conservation laws and electromagnetism.

## Divergence and Stokes Theorems {#S2}

For a sufficiently smooth vector field $\mathbf F$ on a suitable volume $V$ with closed outward-oriented boundary $\partial V$, the divergence theorem states

$$
\iiint_V(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dV
=\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA.
$$

For an oriented smooth surface $S$ with positively oriented boundary curve $\partial S$, Stokes' theorem states

$$
\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dA
=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

The right-hand rule couples the normal and boundary direction in Stokes' theorem. The surface in Gauss' theorem, in contrast, must be closed. Reversing orientation changes the sign.

## Example: Sphere and Disk {#S3}

Take $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)^\mathsf T$ and the ball $V$ of radius $R$. Its divergence is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$, giving the outward flux

$$
\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dA
=3\cdot\frac{4\pi R^3}{3}=4\pi R^3.
$$

For $\mathbf G=(-y,x,0)^\mathsf T$, $\nabla\times\mathbf G=(0,0,2)^\mathsf T$. On the unit disk in the $xy$ plane with normal in the $+z$ direction,

$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf G\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_S2\,dA=2\pi.
$$

This independently confirms the directly evaluated line integral in the preceding lesson.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the outward flux of $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)^\mathsf T$ across the unit cube $0\le x,y,z\le1$?
2. What boundary direction corresponds to a surface normal pointing along $+z$?
3. Can the divergence theorem be applied directly to an open hemisphere without its base disk?

### Solutions

1. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$ and the volume is $1$, so flux is $3$.
2. Counterclockwise when viewed from the positive $z$ axis toward the surface.
3. No. The missing circular base must be added to close the surface, or its contribution accounted for separately.

## Assumptions, Singularities and Limits {#S5}

Applications require compatible orientations, sufficient smoothness and an appropriate domain. A field that looks harmless away from a singularity may behave differently if the singularity lies inside the integration region. For piecewise smooth regions, internal interfaces can contribute boundary terms and physical jump conditions matter. Merely memorising formulas makes it easy to confuse open and closed surfaces. The geometric integral theorems remain valid in curvilinear coordinates, but component formulas require metric factors.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The divergence theorem connects volume sources to flux across a closed boundary; Stokes connects curl to oriented circulation. These global relations underpin applications in mechanics and electrodynamics.

The subsequent modules apply these methods to mechanics and other areas of physics.
