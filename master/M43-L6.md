# M43-L6 – Residuals, Weighted Chi-Square and Model Adequacy {#L6}

**Course area:** M43 – Master: Model Assessment and Scientific Research Practice

**Level:** Master · advanced specialization

**Prerequisites:** M16: statistics; M31: validation; M43-L5: identifiability.

**Learning objective:** Compute standardized residuals and interpret a weighted fit metric under its assumptions.

## Guiding question and context {#S1}

An attractive curve is not a sufficient model check. Observation-minus-model residuals must be compared with measurement precision and possible dependence. For uncorrelated observations with known standard deviations, a weighted sum of squares is useful diagnostic information. A reduced value near one is only a benchmark under appropriate model assumptions and does not prove the physics correct.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

For observations $y_i$, predictions $f_i(\hat\theta)$ and known $\sigma_i>0$, the weighted statistic is $\chi^2=\sum_i[(y_i-f_i)/\sigma_i]^2$. When $p$ independent parameters are fitted to the same $n$ observations and regularity conditions hold, the nominal residual degrees of freedom are often $\nu=n-p$; $\chi^2_\mathrm{red}=\chi^2/\nu$ for $\nu>0$. A chi-square reference distribution requires further assumptions, including an appropriate error covariance and approximately Gaussian measurement errors.

$$
\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{y_i-f_i(\hat\theta)}{\sigma_i}\right)^2,\qquad \chi^2_{\mathrm{red}}=\frac{\chi^2}{n-p}.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

A **synthetic** three-point example uses $y=(2,4,5)$, $f=(2.1,3.8,5.1)$ and $\sigma=(0.1,0.2,0.1)$. Standardized residuals are $(-1,1,-1)$, yielding $\chi^2=3$. If exactly one independently identifiable parameter was estimated from these three readings ($p=1$), then $\nu=2$ and $\chi^2_\mathrm{red}=1.5$. The diagram only displays standardized residuals; it does not establish a formal p-value.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is $\chi^2$ for standardized residuals $(1,2,0)$?
2. How many nominal degrees of freedom result for $n=10$, $p=3$?
3. Why can an unusually small chi-square also deserve scrutiny?

### Solutions

1. $1^2+2^2+0^2=5$.
2. $\nu=10-3=7$.
3. Check overestimated $\sigma_i$, correlations or excessive model flexibility.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

Correlated errors require the full covariance matrix; diagonally weighted residuals may be misleading. Small samples, nonlinear fits, unknown variances and boundary parameters can invalidate naive $n-p$ interpretations. Model adequacy should also be assessed using independent data.
