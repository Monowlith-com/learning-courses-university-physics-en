# M24-L5 – Nuclear Medicine Imaging: PET and SPECT {#L5}

**Course area:** M24 – Bachelor: Medical Physics and Imaging

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M15: nuclear decay and radiation detectors; M13: counting statistics. [Previous lesson](M24-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish PET from SPECT detection and evaluate ideal time-of-flight and counting-statistics examples.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

PET and SPECT use the distribution of radioactive tracers to create images. Both detect emitted radiation instead of transmitting an externally generated X-ray beam through the body. Their images are estimates of activity distributions requiring corrections for decay, attenuation, scatter and detector response. Increased activity is not a diagnosis without the appropriate clinical context.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

In positron emission tomography, a positron slows and annihilates with an electron. When the pair has negligible net momentum, two nearly opposite photons each carry $m_ec^2\approx511\,\mathrm{keV}$. Coincident detector events identify a line of response (LOR). In an ideal time-of-flight model, an arrival-time difference $\Delta t$ corresponds to a displacement $\Delta x\approx c\Delta t/2$ along the LOR. Single-photon emission computed tomography commonly restricts individual gamma-photon directions with a mechanical collimator.

$$
E_\gamma=m_ec^2\approx511\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad \Delta x\approx\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2},\qquad \frac{\sigma_N}{N}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt N}.
$$

PET photons are not exactly back-to-back because of residual particle momentum. Positron range, scatter, random coincidences and detector timing further limit localisation. SPECT photon energy depends on the radionuclide and is not universally 511 keV. For independent Poisson counts of expectation $N$, standard deviation is $\sigma_N\approx\sqrt N$ and relative uncertainty is $1/\sqrt N$. This is a statistical model, not a general benchmark of clinical image quality.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $\Delta x$ for $\Delta t=100\,\mathrm{ps}$.
2. What is $\sigma_N/N$ at $N=400$?
3. Why must the energy $511\,\mathrm{keV}$ not be assigned to every SPECT examination?

### Solutions

1. $\Delta x=3.0\cdot10^8\cdot10^{-10}/2=0.015\,\mathrm m=1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$.
2. $1/\sqrt{400}=0.05=5\,\%$.
3. SPECT detects radionuclide-dependent gamma emissions; 511 keV is characteristic of annihilation photons in PET.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The TOF model assumes ideal photon paths. Real reconstruction and dosimetry require multiple corrections and clinical validation. The calculation is not a recommended protocol.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

PET uses detector coincidences to identify a response line; SPECT often uses collimated single photons. Counting statistics limit precision in both.
