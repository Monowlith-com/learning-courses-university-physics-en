# M14-L6 – Semiconductors, Doping and Charge-Carrier Transport {#L6}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M14-L4 and M14-L5, Boltzmann distributions and electric fields. [Previous lesson](M14-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish electrons and holes and model intrinsic conduction and doping with elementary band theory.

## Valence and Conduction Bands {#S1}

A semiconductor has a filled valence band at sufficiently low temperature, with a conduction band separated by an energy gap $E_g$. Thermal or optical excitation can lift electrons into the conduction band, leaving positively charged holes in the valence band. In equilibrium, the Fermi level sets the occupation probabilities rather than necessarily indicating one particular electron energy. Donor doping can raise the electron concentration, while acceptor doping raises the hole concentration. Whether dopants are ionized depends on their binding energies and temperature. Carrier mobilities and effective masses, together with carrier densities, influence conductivity.

## Carrier Densities and Mass Action {#S2}

For nondegenerate semiconductors near thermal equilibrium one may use $n=N_c e^{-(E_c-E_F)/(k_BT)}$ and $p=N_v e^{-(E_F-E_v)/(k_BT)}$. Multiplying eliminates the Fermi energy under the stated assumptions:

$$
np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=n_i^2.
$$

Intrinsic material has $n=p=n_i$. For fully ionized extrinsic n-type material with $N_D\gg n_i$, the approximate carrier densities are $n\approx N_D$ and $p\approx n_i^2/N_D$. The conductivity is 

$$
\sigma=e(n\mu_n+p\mu_p).
$$

Effective densities of states $N_c$ and $N_v$ depend on temperature and band structure. Heavy doping can violate the nondegenerate approximation and require full Fermi–Dirac statistics.

## Example: Intrinsic Carrier Concentration {#S3}

Consider an idealized material with $n_i=1.0\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and fully ionized donors $N_D=1.0\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. Mass action gives

$$
p\approx\frac{n_i^2}{N_D}=1.0\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.
$$

Meanwhile $n\approx1.0\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, so the majority-to-minority carrier ratio is $10^{12}$. At an actual pn junction mobile carriers create a depletion region and internal electric field. In thermal equilibrium the Fermi level is spatially constant, although band edges bend.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $p$ for $n_i=10^{9}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $n=10^{15}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.
2. What happens to $p$ if $n$ increases tenfold at fixed $n_i$?
3. Name two reasons why equilibrium mass action cannot directly describe a driven semiconductor.

### Solutions

1. $p=n_i^2/n=10^3\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.
2. It falls by a factor of ten.
3. For example, illumination and strong carrier injection with separate quasi-Fermi levels.

## Recombination, Defects and Devices {#S5}

Mass action is an equilibrium result. Under illumination, applied forward bias or strong injection, separate quasi-Fermi levels are often necessary. Recombination rates are not determined by the band gap alone: defect states, direct versus indirect transitions and nonequilibrium processes also matter. Quoting an intrinsic carrier density without material and temperature is incomplete. At low temperature donors may freeze out, whereas heavy doping can form degenerate electron gases. Semiconductor–metal contacts depend on interface states and band bending. These issues limit the use of simple band sketches for quantitative diode, solar-cell or transistor predictions.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Band gap, carrier occupation and mobility explain the main difference between semiconductors and metals.
