# M17-L5 – Numerical Solution of Ordinary Differential Equations {#L5}

**Course area:** M17 – Bachelor: Computational Physics and Simulation

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M3: Newtonian dynamics; M8: differential equations; M17-L1: step size. [Previous lesson](M17-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Apply Euler and Runge–Kutta methods to an initial-value problem and distinguish stability from accuracy.

## Initial-Value Problem and Time Step {#S1}

Many dynamical physics models can be expressed as $\dot{\boldsymbol y}=\boldsymbol f(t,\boldsymbol y)$. Initial values $\boldsymbol y(t_0)$ together with the equations define an initial-value problem. Numerical integrators replace continuous evolution by steps of length $h$. An appropriate method must match both the desired accuracy and the physical time scales. In coupled variables such as position and velocity, updates must not inadvertently mix incompatible time levels. Record units, initial states and expected conservation laws before integration.

## Euler, Runge–Kutta and Stability {#S2}

Explicit Euler uses the slope at the start of an interval and has global error $O(h)$ for regular smooth problems. The classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4) combines four slope estimates and attains global $O(h^4)$ under appropriate smoothness assumptions. High formal order does not guarantee stability: for $y'=-\lambda y$ with $\lambda>0$, the Euler update factor is $1-\lambda h$ and its homogeneous discrete sequence is asymptotically stable only when $0<\lambda h<2$. Widely separated time scales yield *stiff* systems, often better served by implicit methods.

$$
y_{n+1}^{\mathrm{Euler}}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n).
$$

$$
k_1=f(t_n,y_n),\;k_2=f(t_n+h/2,y_n+hk_1/2),\;k_3=f(t_n+h/2,y_n+hk_2/2),\;k_4=f(t_n+h,y_n+hk_3).
$$

$$
y_{n+1}^{\mathrm{RK4}}=y_n+\frac h6(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4).
$$

## Example: Exponential Decay {#S3}

For $y'=-y$ with $y(0)=1$, the exact solution is $y(t)=e^{-t}$. At $h=0.1$, ten Euler updates give $y_{10}=0.9^{10}=0.34867844$, whereas $e^{-1}=0.36787944$; the absolute error is approximately $0.0192010$. RK4 has a much smaller error at that step size because it combines slope information across the interval. This contrast characterizes this smooth, nonstiff benchmark only. Plot both the time evolution and error versus $h$ to make convergence visible.

**Executable Python example:**

```python
import math
h = 0.1
euler = rk4 = 1.0
for _ in range(10):
    euler *= 1-h
    k1 = -rk4
    k2 = -(rk4+h*k1/2)
    k3 = -(rk4+h*k2/2)
    k4 = -(rk4+h*k3)
    rk4 += h*(k1+2*k2+2*k3+k4)/6
print(euler, rk4, math.exp(-1))
```

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate two explicit Euler steps for $y'=-y$, $y_0=1$, $h=0.1$.
2. What explicit Euler stability condition applies to $y'=-5y$?
3. Besides position error, what should a long-duration pendulum simulation monitor?

### Solutions

1. $y_1=0.9$, $y_2=0.81$.
2. $0<5h<2$, hence $0<h<0.4$ for asymptotic stability.
3. For example total-energy drift and phase error.

## Adaptive Steps and Invariants {#S5}

Adaptive step-size control estimates local error by comparing approximations and refines the grid around rapid changes. Error tolerances for different state components must reflect physical scales. In long-term Hamiltonian motion a symplectic scheme may be preferable to a higher-order nonsymplectic method because long-term conservation behavior matters. A simulation report includes initial values, time horizon, solver, tolerances, benchmarks and the evolution of relevant invariants. Stability, convergence and physical validity are distinct questions.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Integrator order cannot replace separate stability and invariant checks.
