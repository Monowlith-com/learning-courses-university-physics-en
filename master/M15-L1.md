# M15-L1 – Nuclear Structure, Isotopes and Binding Energy {#L1}

**Course area:** M15 – Bachelor: Nuclear and Particle Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Mass and energy, atomic models, electrostatics, and introductory quantum physics. [Previous lesson](M14-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Identify nuclides using A and Z and connect the mass defect to binding energy.

## Nuclide Notation and Nuclear Forces {#S1}

A nuclide is written $^{A}_{Z}X$. Atomic number $Z$ counts protons, mass number $A=Z+N$ counts all nucleons, and $N$ is the neutron count. Isotopes have identical $Z$ but different $N$. Protons repel electrically; a predominantly attractive short-range residual strong interaction nevertheless binds many nuclei. Its range is of order a few femtometres and it is not a simple Coulomb potential. Nuclear radii are approximately $R=r_0A^{1/3}$ with $r_0$ about $1.2\,\mathrm{fm}$. This empirical scaling indicates roughly constant nucleon density, not a hard surface or a precise radius for every nucleus.

## Mass Defect and Binding Energy {#S2}

A bound nucleus is less massive than its free constituent nucleons. With nuclear masses $m_p,m_n,m_{\mathrm{nucleus}}$, the mass defect is $\Delta m=Zm_p+Nm_n-m_{\mathrm{nucleus}}$. The energy needed to separate all nucleons is

$$
B=\Delta m\,c^2.
$$

When neutral atomic masses are used instead, one may calculate $\Delta m=Zm(^{1}H)+Nm_n-m_{\mathrm{atom}}$: electron rest masses approximately cancel, though electronic binding-energy differences matter at high accuracy. Binding energy per nucleon $B/A$ is a useful stability indicator but does not alone decide whether a decay can occur. Possible final states must also satisfy conservation laws and interaction dynamics.

## Example: Helium-4 {#S3}

For $^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$ use approximate atomic masses $m(^{1}H)=1.007825\,\mathrm{u}$, $m_n=1.008665\,\mathrm{u}$ and $m(^{4}He)=4.002603\,\mathrm{u}$. The mass defect is $\Delta m=2(1.007825)+2(1.008665)-4.002603=0.030377\,\mathrm{u}$. Using $1\,\mathrm{u}\,c^2\approx931.494\,\mathrm{MeV}$ gives

$$
B\approx28.30\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad B/A\approx7.07\,\mathrm{MeV}.
$$

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How many neutrons does $^{14}_{6}\mathrm C$ contain?
2. Find $B$ for $\Delta m=0.010\,\mathrm u$.
3. Why does $B/A$ alone not determine a decay rate?

### Solutions

1. $N=A-Z=8$.
2. $B\approx9.315\,\mathrm{MeV}$.
3. Conservation laws, barriers and transition matrix elements control channels and rates.

## Stability and Limitations {#S5}

The liquid-drop model combines volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry and pairing contributions. It explains broad systematics but not the detailed nuclear shell structure. Particularly stable proton and neutron counts reflect quantized nuclear levels and spin–orbit coupling rather than average binding energy alone. Actual nuclei can be excited, deformed or short-lived. A spontaneous reaction needs positive available energy in an allowed channel; an energetically possible process is not necessarily fast. Atomic and nuclear masses must be distinguished in high-precision calculations.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Mass defect and binding energy describe the same mass difference; stability also depends on dynamics.
