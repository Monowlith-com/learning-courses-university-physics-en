# M2-L7 – Line Integrals, Work and Circulation {#L7}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Vector fields, dot products, integration and parametrisation. [Previous lesson](M2-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Evaluate line integrals along parameterised paths and identify path dependence.

## A Path Through a Force Field {#S1}

When a body moves from a start to an endpoint, the work done generally depends on force and trajectory. A vector line integral adds the tangential field component along a path. Orientation matters: reversing a path reverses the sign of an oriented vector line integral. This differs from a scalar line integral such as weighted length $\int_C\rho\,ds$.

## Definition and Parameterisation {#S2}

Let $\mathbf r(t)$ for $t\in[a,b]$ parameterise a piecewise smooth path, and let $\mathbf F$ be continuous along it. Then

$$
\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_a^b\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt.
$$

The derivative $\mathbf r'(t)$ contains the tangent and traversal direction. The integral is invariant under direction-preserving monotone reparameterisations. Around a closed curve $C$, $\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ is called circulation. If $\mathbf F=\nabla\phi$, the chain rule gives

$$
\int_C\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf r
=\phi(\mathbf r(b))-\phi(\mathbf r(a)).
$$

This is the fundamental theorem for line integrals and explains why conservative fields are path-independent.

## Example: Rotational Field on a Circle {#S3}

Take $\mathbf F=(-y,x)^\mathsf T$ and the counterclockwise unit circle $\mathbf r(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)^\mathsf T$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. Then $\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t)^\mathsf T$ and $\mathbf r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t)^\mathsf T$. Their dot product is constantly $1$, so

$$
\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi.
$$

The nonzero value shows that the field is not conservative on the disk. Reversing the orientation gives $-2\pi$. Circulation has dimensions of field magnitude times length, for example joules for a force field.

*Circulation of the rotational field on the unit circle equals two pi.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute $\int_C (2x,0)\cdot d\mathbf r$ from $(0,0)$ to $(2,0)$ along the $x$ axis.
2. What is the value if the traversal direction is reversed?
3. What work is performed by $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$ if $U(A)=8\,\mathrm J$ and $U(B)=3\,\mathrm J$?

### Solutions

1. $\int_0^2 2x\,dx=4$ in units consistent with the field.
2. $-4$ in the same units.
3. $W=8\,\mathrm J-3\,\mathrm J=5\,\mathrm J$.

## Practical Rules and Limits {#S5}

Work by a physical force uses the actual path and the oriented force component; path length alone is insufficient. For $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$, the work is $W=U(A)-U(B)$, the negative change in potential energy. Path independence is useful but relies on a suitable domain. For applications with discontinuities or singularities, field regularity and the admissibility of the path must be assessed separately.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Line integrals accumulate tangential field components along oriented paths. The relationship between boundary circulation and interior curl leads directly to Stokes’ theorem.
