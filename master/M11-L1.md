# M11-L1 – Blackbody Radiation and Planck’s Quantum {#L1}

**Course area:** M11 – Bachelor: Foundations of Quantum Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Temperature, thermal equilibrium, standing waves and exponential functions; M5 and M7. [Previous lesson](M10-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Interpret blackbody spectra, motivate Planck’s distribution, and apply Wien’s and Stefan–Boltzmann laws.

## Why the Classical Description Fails {#S1}

A blackbody is an ideal absorber whose equilibrium thermal emission depends only on its temperature. A cavity with a small opening approximates the model, while real hot objects and stellar surfaces have additional effects. At a fixed temperature the ideal spectrum is independent of material colour. It is essential to distinguish a spectrum per wavelength interval from one per frequency interval: converting the horizontal axis does not simply turn one peak into the reciprocal of the other, because interval widths change as well. The classical Rayleigh–Jeans law works at long wavelengths but predicts unbounded energy at short wavelengths, the ultraviolet catastrophe. Quantum theory resolves that failure without suggesting that all radiation consists of a single wavelength.

## Quantized Modes and the Spectral Distribution {#S2}

Planck assigned an electromagnetic mode of frequency $\nu$ discrete energies $E_n=nh\nu$, with integer $n\geq0$. For one harmonic mode the geometric partition sum is $Z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-n\beta h\nu}=1/(1-e^{-\beta h\nu})$ where $\beta=1/(k_\mathrm{B}T)$. Hence the mean thermal mode energy is $\langle E\rangle=h\nu/(e^{h\nu/(k_\mathrm{B}T)}-1)$. Combining it with the electromagnetic mode density gives the spectral *exitance* per area and wavelength

$$
M_\lambda(T)=\frac{2\pi hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{\exp(hc/(\lambda k_\mathrm{B}T))-1}.
$$

Units are $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$, not $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$: integration over $d\lambda$ is required for total power per area. Maximizing and integrating respectively yield $\lambda_\mathrm{max}T=b\simeq2.898\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}$ and $M=\sigma T^4$, with $\sigma\simeq5.6704\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-4}}$.

## Example: Thermal Peak Emission {#S3}

For the model temperature $T=5800\,\mathrm K$, Wien’s law gives $\lambda_\mathrm{max}=b/T\simeq5.00\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m=500\,\mathrm{nm}$. The Stefan–Boltzmann relation yields $M=\sigma(5800\,\mathrm K)^4\simeq6.42\times10^7\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. Doubling the absolute temperature of an ideal object multiplies the integrated power per area by $2^4=16$ and halves the peak wavelength. Rescaling both curves to the same height would obscure the emitted-power comparison.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate the peak wavelength at $T=2900\,\mathrm K$.
2. By which factor does $M$ grow from $300$ to $600\,\mathrm K$?
3. Explain why $M_\lambda$ has units containing $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$.

### Solutions

1. $\lambda_\mathrm{max}\simeq2.898\times10^{-3}/2900\,\mathrm m\simeq999\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. $(600/300)^4=16$.
3. $M_\lambda\,d\lambda$ has units $\mathrm{W/m^2}$; dividing by the wavelength interval gives $\mathrm{W/m^3}$.

## What a Measured Curve Means {#S5}

A detector measures finite wavelength bands and selected viewing directions. Exitance here is already integrated over the outward hemisphere; spectral radiance has different units and differs by a factor of $\pi$ for a Lambertian emitter. Real surfaces require a wavelength- and temperature-dependent emissivity $\varepsilon(\lambda,T)$, while intervening media may absorb radiation. Thus the ideal formula is not a universal thermometer without calibration. In the next step, the energy carried in individual light–matter interactions provides a second line of evidence for quantization.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Planck’s quantization explains thermal spectra and yields two temperature laws describing different observables.
