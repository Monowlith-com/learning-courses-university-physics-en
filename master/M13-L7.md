# M13-L7 – Fluctuations, Susceptibility, and Response Functions {#L7}

**Course area:** M13 – Bachelor: Statistical Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Canonical and grand canonical partition functions from M13-L3 and M13-L4; variance and derivatives. [Previous lesson](M13-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive fluctuations from partition functions and distinguish ensemble-dependent variables.

## A Mean Is Not an Individual Result {#S1}

Equilibrium does not mean that every microscopic quantity is constant. A small object in a heat bath exhibits energy fluctuations around its canonical mean $U$. For a discrete random variable $X$, the expectation is $\langle X\rangle=\sum_i p_iX_i$ and the variance is $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\langle X^2\rangle-\langle X\rangle^2$. The standard deviation $\sigma_X=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$ has the same units as $X$, whereas variance has squared units. In a sufficiently large ordinary system, $\sigma_X/\langle X\rangle$ can become small while absolute fluctuations still grow. A static variance curve alone does not describe dynamical autocorrelations or relaxation times.

## Energy and Particle-Number Fluctuations {#S2}

Differentiating $\ln Z$ twice with respect to $\beta$ at fixed Hamiltonian parameters gives the canonical identity

$$
\mathrm{Var}(E)=k_BT^2 C_V.
$$

It connects microscopic energy fluctuations to measurable heat capacity. Similarly, in the grand canonical ensemble at fixed $T,V$, 

$$
\mathrm{Var}(N)=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}.
$$

The second formula describes response to changing chemical potential. Both require equilibrium and the proper choice of controlled parameters. In a strictly canonical ensemble particle-number variance is exactly zero, not because particles stop moving but because $N$ was imposed as a fixed constraint.

## Example: Fluctuating Occupation of a Level {#S3}

A single fermionic level is occupied ($N=1$) or empty ($N=0$). At occupation probability $p$ its mean is $\langle N\rangle=p$, and directly

$$
\mathrm{Var}(N)=p(1-p).
$$

At $p=0.3$, the variance is $0.3\cdot0.7=0.21$ and standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.21}\approx0.458$. The diagram's variance curve has maximum $1/4$ at $p=1/2$ and vanishes at either endpoint. This does not imply that every many-fermion system at half filling has a universal macroscopic fluctuation: correlations, energy dependence, and conservation laws change the variance of total occupation.

*The variance p(1-p) has maximum 0.25 at p = 0.5.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the variance of a Bernoulli level at $p=1/2$.
2. What units does $\mathrm{Var}(E)$ have?
3. Why is $\mathrm{Var}(N)=0$ in a canonical ensemble?

### Solutions

1. $\mathrm{Var}(N)=1/4$.
2. Energy squared, for example $\mathrm{J}^2$.
3. $N$ is fixed by the ensemble definition and cannot fluctuate.

## Linear Response and Ensemble Limits {#S5}

A response coefficient measures how an average changes when a controlled parameter is varied. Magnetic susceptibility, for example, can be related to magnetization fluctuations when the coupling of field to energy and all fixed variables are correctly specified. Susceptibilities may become large near phase transitions, and long-range correlations can invalidate simple sums of independent contributions. Dynamical fluctuation–dissipation relations require additional assumptions about equilibrium, causality, and frequency dependence, and should not be identified with static variance identities. ## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Derivatives of partition functions encode both averages and fluctuations. Extended correlations lead to the study of phase transitions.
