# M4-L5 – Interference and Standing Waves {#L5}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Harmonic waves and trigonometric addition. [Previous lesson](M4-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive interference conditions and determine the natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends.

## Superposition as a Linear Principle {#S1}

When two waves overlap in a **linear** medium, their instantaneous displacements add. For equal frequencies the relative phase determines constructive or destructive interference. A stable phase relation over the observation interval is required for persistent interference. Two equal-amplitude harmonic waves travelling in opposite directions can generate a **standing wave**. Unlike a travelling sinusoid, a standing wave has fixed nodes where displacement vanishes at all times.

## Interference and Boundary Conditions {#S2}

Two harmonic waves with the same amplitude $A$, wavenumber $k$ and frequency $\omega$ add as

$$
A\cos(kx-\omega t)+A\cos(kx+\omega t)=2A\cos(kx)\cos(\omega t).
$$

Their position-dependent amplitude is $2A|\cos(kx)|$, with nodes wherever $\cos(kx)=0$. For an ideal string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, displacement must vanish at $x=0$ and $x=L$. Only certain wavelengths and frequencies are then allowed:

$$
\lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad f_n=\frac{nv}{2L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots
$$

Here $v$ is the wave speed on the string, given by $v=\sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ for an ideal taut string with tension $F_T$ and linear mass density $\mu$.

## Worked Example: Vibrating String {#S3}

A string of length $L=0.80\,\mathrm m$ is under sufficient tension for transverse waves to travel at $v=160\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Its fundamental frequency is $f_1=v/(2L)=100\,\mathrm{Hz}$; the second and third harmonics are at $200$ and $300\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The fundamental has nodes at both ends and an antinode at the centre. Mode two also has a node at $x=L/2$. The figure shows the spatial shapes of the first three modes at an instant of maximum displacement. The allowed mode shapes do not depend on the initial excitation amplitude.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the fundamental frequency of a string fixed at both ends with $L=1.0\,\mathrm m$ and $v=120\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
2. How many interior nodes are present in its fourth normal mode?
3. Two equal-amplitude waves arrive with phase difference $\pi$. What is their sum?

### Solutions

1. $f_1=v/(2L)=60\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
2. There are $n-1=3$ interior nodes, plus nodes at both ends.
3. For equal polarisation and frequency their displacements cancel locally.

## Coherence and Real Systems {#S5}

A real string has losses, finite bending stiffness and imperfect supports. These change frequencies and shorten the lifetime of its motion. Simple superposition fails for strongly nonlinear waves. For interference one must distinguish the added **field amplitudes** from measured intensity: intensity is generally proportional to a time-averaged square and therefore contains cross terms. Boundary conditions determine whether a standing wave can be sustained.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Interference and boundary conditions explain standing waves. Air resonators use related mathematics.
