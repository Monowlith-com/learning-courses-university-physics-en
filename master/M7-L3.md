# M7-L3 – Polarization and Fresnel Coefficients {#L3}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Electric field as a vector, interfaces and angles. [Previous lesson](M7-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish s/p polarization, calculate normal-incidence reflectance and apply Malus’s law.

## Polarization of Electromagnetic Waves {#S1}

For a plane transverse electromagnetic wave, the electric field is perpendicular to propagation. Linear polarization means the field oscillates along a fixed line at a given point. Circular and elliptical polarization describe other paths of the field tip over time. The plane containing the ray and the interface normal is the plane of incidence. The electric field is **s-polarized** if perpendicular to that plane, and **p-polarized** if in the plane. Unpolarized light is commonly modeled as a statistical mixture; it does not have a single deterministic field direction. Reflected and transmitted fractions depend on polarization and incidence angle.

## Field Amplitudes at an Interface {#S2}

Boundary conditions on tangential electric and magnetic fields yield the Fresnel amplitudes. For s-polarization at a lossless, nonmagnetic interface and angles related by Snell's law, the electric-field reflection coefficient is

$$
r_s=\frac{n_1\cos\theta_1-n_2\cos\theta_2}{n_1\cos\theta_1+n_2\cos\theta_2}.
$$

For p-polarization, the sign of the amplitude coefficient depends on the respective polarization basis; the unambiguous physical reflectance is $R_p=|r_p|^2$. At normal incidence both polarizations give 

$$
R=\left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2.
$$

This is an **intensity** reflectance, not a field-amplitude magnitude. For nonabsorbing interfaces, correctly normalized energy flux obeys $R+T=1$. In a boundary between nonmagnetic media, p-polarized reflectance vanishes at the Brewster angle $\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1$.

## Worked Example: Glass and Analyser {#S3}

For air ($n_1=1.00$) and glass ($n_2=1.50$), normal incidence gives $R=(-0.50/2.50)^2=0.040$, or $4.0\%$ of incoming intensity at **one** uncoated surface. With two surfaces, multiple reflections may need consideration. The Brewster angle is $\theta_B=\arctan(1.5)\approx56.31^\circ$. An ideal linear analyser transmits a fraction

$$
I=I_0\cos^2\varphi
$$

of already linearly polarized light. At $\varphi=60^\circ$ between the input polarization and transmission axis, $25\%$ remains. The diagram separates the plane of incidence, s/p axes and analyser axis.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $R$ at a single interface from $n_1=1$ to $n_2=2$.
2. Find $I/I_0$ for an ideal analyser at $\varphi=45^\circ$.
3. Why can $|r_s|$ not directly be called the fraction of reflected intensity?

### Solutions

1. $R=((1-2)/(1+2))^2=1/9\approx11.1\%$.
2. $I/I_0=\cos^2(45^\circ)=1/2$.
3. $r_s$ is a field-amplitude ratio; intensity reflectance is $|r_s|^2$.

## Limits of Ideal Polarizers {#S5}

Malus's law requires an ideally linearly polarized input and an ideal analyser. For initially unpolarized light, a first ideal polarizer transmits half the intensity on average before the second axis follows Malus's law. Absorbing media, anisotropic crystals or magnetic materials generally require more than the simple refractive-index formulas. A phase delay between s and p components can change polarization without eliminating the total field. Practical antireflection coatings exploit interference and complex field amplitudes rather than simple geometric shading.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Polarization describes fields, not just rays. Diffraction and interference next follow directly from superposition of phase.
