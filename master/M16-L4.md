# M16-L4 – Uncertainty Propagation and Covariance {#L4}

**Course area:** M16 – Bachelor: Experimental Physics and Measurement Analysis

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M2: partial derivatives; M16-L3: standard uncertainties. [Previous lesson](M16-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Include derivatives and covariances correctly for derived measurands.

## Measurement Functions and Sensitivities {#S1}

A target quantity is often derived from $y=f(x_1,\ldots,x_m)$, for example velocity from distance and time. To first order in small deviations, $\delta y\approx\sum_i c_i\delta x_i$, where $c_i=\partial f/\partial x_i$ are *sensitivity coefficients*. Their dimensions are $[y]/[x_i]$. Relative uncertainties are convenient for products and powers, but must follow from the measurement model and initially omit correlation. Zero sensitivity at a working point does not imply the input uncertainty is irrelevant for an appreciably nonlinear function.

## Linear Propagation with Covariances {#S2}

Let $\mathbf C$ denote the input covariance matrix and $\mathbf c$ the gradient of $f$. Linear propagation gives

$$
u_c(y)^2=\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\mathbf c=\sum_i c_i^2u(x_i)^2+2\sum_{i<j}c_ic_j\operatorname{cov}(x_i,x_j).
$$

For two quantities one often writes $\operatorname{cov}(x_1,x_2)=\rho\,u(x_1)u(x_2)$, with $-1\le\rho\le1$. Negative correlation can reduce the uncertainty of a sum but increase that of a difference. A covariance matrix must be symmetric and positive semidefinite; otherwise the probabilistic model is inconsistent.

## Example: Difference of Two Readings {#S3}

Two temperature indications have $u(x_1)=u(x_2)=0.20\,\mathrm K$ and shared calibration effects producing $\rho=0.75$. For their difference $d=x_1-x_2$, sensitivities are $c_1=1$ and $c_2=-1$. Therefore

$$
u(d)=\sqrt{0.20^2+0.20^2-2(0.75)(0.20)(0.20)}\,\mathrm K\approx0.141\,\mathrm K.
$$

Incorrectly assuming independence would give $0.283\,\mathrm K$. The illustration shows a tilted covariance ellipse and the direction of the difference. It is schematic and does not substitute for estimating a covariance matrix from real data.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Give the sensitivities for $y=x_1/x_2$.
2. Compute $u(x_1-x_2)$ for $u_1=u_2=1$ and $\rho=0$.
3. What changes for a positively correlated difference?

### Solutions

1. $c_1=1/x_2$, $c_2=-x_1/x_2^2$.
2. $u=\sqrt2\approx1.414$.
3. The covariance contribution enters the variance of the difference with a minus sign, reducing combined uncertainty.

## Limits of Taylor Approximation {#S5}

First-order Taylor propagation works best for small uncertainties and locally smooth $f$. Near singularities, thresholds or hard truncation, it can miss asymmetry in the output distribution. A common calibration source cannot be split into independent uncertainty terms merely because inputs appear in different files. For strongly nonlinear models a Monte Carlo propagation may be documented, together with input distributions and sample size. The result, measurement model, evaluation point and included covariances form one coherent uncertainty assessment.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The covariance matrix is essential for jointly influenced measurands; a naive sum of independent squares is then wrong.
