# M10-L1 – Electrostatic Boundary-Value Problems {#L1}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Gauss’s law, vector calculus and boundary conditions; M8-L8. [Previous lesson](M9-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive Poisson’s and Laplace’s equations, solve a planar boundary-value problem and state uniqueness.

## Potential as a Field Problem {#S1}

An electric field is not set merely by the charge at a chosen point: conductors, geometry and surroundings shape the potential throughout space. In static equilibrium the field is curl-free and may be written as the gradient of a scalar potential, $\boldsymbol E=-\nabla\phi$. Boundary-value problems specify a charge density together with either potential on the boundary (Dirichlet data) or its normal derivative (Neumann data). Where no charge is present the governing equation becomes Laplace’s equation. Its solutions are harmonic functions, which have no strict interior extrema unless constant on a connected domain. Numerical field solvers approximate the same mathematical problem on a mesh.

## From Maxwell to Poisson’s Equation {#S2}

Gauss’s law, $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol E=\rho/\varepsilon_0$, combined with $\boldsymbol E=-\nabla\phi$ yields

$$
\nabla^2\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

This is Poisson’s equation in vacuum. In a charge-free region ($\rho=0$) it reduces to $\nabla^2\phi=0$. In Cartesian coordinates the Laplacian is the sum of second spatial derivatives, $\nabla^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2$. A conductor’s surface is equipotential; in an ideal conductor at electrostatic equilibrium the internal $\boldsymbol E$ vanishes. Drawing field lines does not itself specify boundary data: prescribed potential values or fluxes are required for a definite mathematical solution.

## Example: Parallel Electrodes {#S3}

Two ideal extended plates at $x=0$ and $x=d$ are held at $\phi(0)=V$ and $\phi(d)=0$. Assume no intervening charge ($\rho=0$) and neglect fringing. Translation symmetry reduces Laplace’s equation to $\mathrm d^2\phi/\mathrm dx^2=0$, whose general solution is $\phi(x)=A+Bx$. The boundary values give

$$
\phi(x)=V\left(1-\frac{x}{d}\right),\qquad E_x=\frac{V}{d}.
$$

For $V=12\,\mathrm V$ and $d=0.004\,\mathrm m$, $E_x=3000\,\mathrm{V/m}$. The illustration displays planar equipotentials and a uniform field from higher to lower potential. It ceases to be exact near the plate edges.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. State Poisson’s equation in vacuum.
2. Find $E_x$ for $V=9\,\mathrm V$ and $d=3\,\mathrm{mm}$.
3. Why is $\phi$ determined only up to a constant for pure Neumann data?

### Solutions

1. $\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$.
2. $E_x=9/0.003=3000\,\mathrm{V/m}$.
3. Derivatives do not change under $\phi\mapsto\phi+C$; flux data alone cannot fix the constant.

## Boundary Conditions and Uniqueness {#S5}

If two solutions have identical charge density and Dirichlet boundary values, their difference $u$ is harmonic and vanishes on the boundary. Integrating $u\nabla^2u$ by parts over the volume gives $\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2\,\mathrm dV=0$; therefore $u=0$ throughout the connected region. This illustrates the uniqueness theorem for regular Dirichlet problems. With purely Neumann conditions, an additive constant remains undetermined and the total prescribed flux must agree with the integrated charge. At interfaces, derivative conditions depend on material permittivity. A schematic drawing cannot replace a check of these hypotheses.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Boundary conditions define the static problem. Time-dependent fields additionally require a vector potential and a gauge choice.
