# M15-L3 – Nuclear Reactions, Q Value and Threshold {#L3}

**Course area:** M15 – Bachelor: Nuclear and Particle Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M15-L1, energy and momentum conservation, overview of relativity. [Previous lesson](M15-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Compute reaction Q values from masses and distinguish energetic allowance from reaction rate.

## Reaction Channels and Nucleon Balance {#S1}

In nuclear reactions a projectile and target scatter or transform into products. The notation $a+A\rightarrow b+B$ denotes projectile $a$, laboratory target $A$ and outgoing particles $b,B$ in a two-body channel. Total electric charge and baryon number are conserved in ordinary nuclear reactions, even when weak transformations redistribute protons and neutrons. Energy resides in rest mass, motion and internal excitation. Examples include neutron capture, heavy-nucleus fission and light-nucleus fusion. Nuclear reactions differ from chemical changes: rearrangements of nuclear binding typically involve much larger energies than electronic bonds.

## Q Value and Conservation Laws {#S2}

Reaction Q value is the difference between total initial and final rest energies:

$$
Q=\left(\sum m_{\mathrm{initial}}-\sum m_{\mathrm{final}}\right)c^2.
$$

For nonrelativistic kinetic energies and ground-state products, $K_{\mathrm{final}}=K_{\mathrm{initial}}+Q$. Positive $Q$ is exoenergetic; negative $Q$ is endoenergetic. Momentum conservation and possible Coulomb barriers remain important even for $Q>0$: a collision needs the appropriate energy and interaction. When atomic masses are used, electron numbers and masses must be handled consistently. Excited products reduce the kinetic energy available by the excitation energy.

## Example: Fusion Energy {#S3}

The reaction $^2H+{}^3H\rightarrow{}^4He+n$ has approximate atomic masses $m_D=2.014102\,\mathrm u$, $m_T=3.016049\,\mathrm u$, $m_{He}=4.002603\,\mathrm u$ and $m_n=1.008665\,\mathrm u$. The mass difference is $\Delta m=0.018883\,\mathrm u$, giving

$$
Q\approx17.59\,\mathrm{MeV}.
$$

By momentum conservation most of the released energy goes to the neutron and some to the helium nucleus. This number says nothing about the net engineering energy balance of a reactor: ignition, confinement, losses and materials are separate problems. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What sign does $Q$ have when final rest mass is larger?
2. Find $Q$ for $\Delta m=0.002\,\mathrm u$.
3. Why does $Q>0$ not imply a large reaction cross section?

### Solutions

1. Negative: endoenergetic.
2. $Q\approx1.863\,\mathrm{MeV}$.
3. Coulomb barriers, selection rules and weak matrix elements can suppress a process.

## Thresholds and Physical Limits {#S5}

For endoenergetic reactions on a stationary target, the laboratory projectile threshold generally exceeds $-Q$ in magnitude because the products carry collective momentum. Exact relativistic thresholds follow from invariant energy $s=(p_a+p_A)^2$. Resonances, angular momentum, nuclear spin, tunnelling and target isotopes influence measured reaction rates. Fission and fusion are not interchangeable reverse processes operating at one energy; their channels and barriers differ. A positive Q value does not guarantee a prompt or wholly usable energy release.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Q value is a rest-energy difference; thresholds and rates also depend on kinematics and dynamics.
