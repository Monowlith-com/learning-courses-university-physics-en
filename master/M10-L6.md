# M10-L6 – Electromagnetic Fields in Matter {#L6}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Maxwell equations, complex numbers, plane waves and elementary optics. [Previous lesson](M10-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain constitutive laws, boundary conditions, refraction and absorption in simple linear media.

## Microscopic Response and Macroscopic Field {#S1}

Matter contains bound and free charges. Many applications average microscopic fields over small volumes and introduce polarization $\boldsymbol P$ and magnetization $\boldsymbol M$. The macroscopic auxiliary fields $\boldsymbol D$ and $\boldsymbol H$ separate free from bound sources and enable models with measured parameters. Refractive index is not a universal constant of a substance: it may depend on frequency, temperature, propagation direction and polarization. The simple isotropic linear model is a starting point, not a universal law of matter.

## Constitutive Laws and Interfaces {#S2}

In SI units define $\boldsymbol D=\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol E+\boldsymbol P$ and $\boldsymbol H=\boldsymbol B/\mu_0-\boldsymbol M$. For a homogeneous isotropic linear nondispersive model, take $\boldsymbol D=\varepsilon\boldsymbol E$ and $\boldsymbol B=\mu\boldsymbol H$. Integrated Maxwell equations imply interface jump conditions with unit normal $\boldsymbol n$:

$$
\boldsymbol n\cdot(\boldsymbol D_2-\boldsymbol D_1)=\sigma_f,\quad\boldsymbol n\cdot(\boldsymbol B_2-\boldsymbol B_1)=0,
$$

$$
\boldsymbol n\times(\boldsymbol E_2-\boldsymbol E_1)=0,\quad\boldsymbol n\times(\boldsymbol H_2-\boldsymbol H_1)=\boldsymbol K_f.
$$

The symbols $\sigma_f$ and $\boldsymbol K_f$ denote free surface charge and surface current density.

## Example: Normal Incidence {#S3}

For two nonmagnetic lossless media at normal incidence, the electric-field reflection amplitude is

$$
r_E=\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2},\qquad R=|r_E|^2.
$$

At an air ($n_1\approx1$) to glass ($n_2\approx1.5$) interface, $r_E=-0.2$ and $R=0.04$: approximately four percent of incident power is reflected at one ideal boundary. The negative sign signifies phase inversion of the reflected electric field. A finite window with two boundaries also exhibits multiple reflections. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Which free source determines the jump in normal $D$?
2. Calculate $R$ for $n_1=1$ and $n_2=2$ at normal incidence.
3. Why does $R+T=1$ not hold in every material?

### Solutions

1. Free surface charge density $\sigma_f$.
2. $R=((1-2)/(1+2))^2=1/9\approx11.1\,\%$.
3. Absorption or other loss channels can remove power from the propagating field.

## Dispersion, Conductivity and Model Limits {#S5}

The expression $n=\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r}$ applies directly only to suitably defined homogeneous isotropic models and requires complex frequency-dependent material parameters when dispersion is present. In absorbing media, $n$ is complex and $R+T$ can be less than one; the missing energy is absorbed. Anisotropic crystals may be birefringent, while conducting media can exhibit penetration depth instead of ordinary transparency. At oblique incidence Fresnel coefficients depend on polarization. Ray diagrams thus represent a special limit of electromagnetic wave theory and do not replace Maxwell boundary conditions.
