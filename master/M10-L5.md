# M10-L5 – Retarded Potentials and Dipole Radiation {#L5}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Potentials, wave equation, harmonic motion and Poynting vector. [Previous lesson](M10-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain retarded source relations and the far field of an electric dipole in the nonrelativistic limit.

## Why Radiation Has a Travel Time {#S1}

Changes in a remote charge distribution are not observed instantly. The wave equations for electromagnetic potentials have causal retarded solutions: fields measured at time $t$ depend on earlier source configurations. A static electric dipole possesses an electrostatic field but does not radiate power into the far zone within the ideal model. Time dependence, particularly the second derivative of dipole moment, creates transverse radiation in the nonrelativistic dipole approximation. Near and far fields must be distinguished because they exhibit different distance dependences.

## Retarded Sources and Far Fields {#S2}

For observation point $\boldsymbol r$ and source point $\boldsymbol r'$, retarded time is $t_r=t-|\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r'|/c$. In Lorenz gauge, the scalar potential in vacuum is

$$
\phi(\boldsymbol r,t)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol r',t_r)}{|\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol r'|}\,\mathrm d^3r'.
$$

A corresponding formula holds for $\boldsymbol A$, with $\mu_0\boldsymbol J/(4\pi)$. For a compact source in nonrelativistic motion with electric dipole moment $\boldsymbol p(t)$, the radiation-zone field is 

$$
\boldsymbol E_{\mathrm{rad}}(\boldsymbol r,t)\simeq\frac{\boldsymbol n\times(\boldsymbol p''(t-r/c)\times\boldsymbol n)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r},\qquad\boldsymbol B_{\mathrm{rad}}=\frac{\boldsymbol n\times\boldsymbol E_{\mathrm{rad}}}{c}.
$$

Here $\boldsymbol n=\boldsymbol r/r$.

## Example: Radiation Angle and Frequency {#S3}

Let a dipole along the $z$ axis oscillate as $\boldsymbol p(t)=p_0\cos(\omega t)\boldsymbol e_z$. Then $|\boldsymbol p''|=\omega^2p_0|\cos(\omega t)|$. Far-field amplitude varies with angle $\theta$ from the dipole axis as $\sin\theta$, so mean radiated intensity varies as $\sin^2\theta$. Along the dipole axis ($\theta=0$) ideal dipole radiation vanishes; it is maximal in the equatorial plane ($\theta=\pi/2$). Doubling angular frequency while holding $p_0$ fixed increases mean power by $2^4=16$ in this dipole model. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Define retarded time for separation $R$.
2. In which direction is ideal dipole radiation maximal?
3. How does dipole radiation power change under $\omega\mapsto3\omega$ at fixed $p_0$?

### Solutions

1. $t_r=t-R/c$.
2. Perpendicular to the dipole axis, at $\theta=\pi/2$.
3. By a factor of $3^4=81$.

## Limits of the Dipole Model {#S5}

The dipole approximation requires source size much smaller than the wavelength and nonrelativistic motion. Extended sources demand phase differences, higher multipoles and antenna geometry. Retarded-potential solutions must also be consistent with charge continuity, $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol J=0$. Radiation fields decay as $1/r$ and mean intensity as $1/r^2$, whereas near-field terms can fall faster. Finite propagation time does not imply isotropic radiation, nor does a purely classical point-dipole model describe individual emitted photons; those require quantization.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Retarded fields connect sources to observations causally. Entering matter introduces polarization, dispersion and interface conditions.
