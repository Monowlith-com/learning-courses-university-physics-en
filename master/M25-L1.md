# M25-L1 – Reaction kinetics, activation barriers and temperature {#L1}

**Course area:** M25 – Bachelor: Interdisciplinary applications and scientific practice

**Level:** Bachelor · elective

**Prerequisites:** M5: thermodynamics; M13: probability distributions; logarithms and elementary differential equations. [Previous lesson](M24-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish an empirical rate law from a reaction mechanism, derive a time course and use the Arrhenius equation.

## Guiding question and observation {#S1}

Why can reactions with similar energy changes proceed at very different rates? In a constant-volume vessel, the concentration $c(t)$ of a reactant is measured. The rate is not determined by the free energy change alone: molecules must reach suitable configurations and often cross an activation barrier. Thermodynamic driving force therefore does not fix the rate. Reaction order is inferred from measurements; for multistep mechanisms it cannot generally be read off from stoichiometric coefficients.

## Physical model and equations {#S2}

For an observed first-order rate law, separation of $dc/dt=-kc$ gives $dc/c=-k\,dt$ and integration yields exponential decay. The half-life does not depend on the initial concentration. The Arrhenius approximation represents the temperature dependence of a rate constant for a fixed mechanism. It is a useful local model rather than a universal exact law. $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ absolute temperature, $E_a$ activation energy and $A$ a prefactor. A plot of $\ln k$ against $1/T$ has slope $-E_a/R$ when $E_a$ is constant. The unit $[k]=\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ applies only to first-order kinetics.

$$
\frac{dc}{dt}=-kc,\qquad c(t)=c_0e^{-kt},\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{k},\qquad k(T)=A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right).
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

For $k=0.020\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $c_0=1.0\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, the concentration after $50\,\mathrm s$ is $c_0e^{-1}\approx0.368\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The half-life is $34.7\,\mathrm s$. If $A$ stays fixed and $E_a=40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, $k$ increases between $300$ and $310\,\mathrm K$ by $\exp[(E_a/R)(1/300-1/310)]\approx1.68$. The figure depicts only the first, modeled concentration curve.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $c/c_0$ after one half-life.
2. Find $t_{1/2}$ for $k=0.010\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
3. Why can an exothermic reaction still be slow?

### Solutions

1. $c/c_0=1/2$.
2. $t_{1/2}=\ln2/0.010\approx69.3\,\mathrm s$.
3. A kinetic barrier or a restricted reaction pathway can slow the process; energy release alone does not determine the rate.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

The single-species model assumes constant volume and constant $k$. Reverse reactions, mass transport, catalysis and changing mechanisms require further equations. All values are illustrative.

## Summary and outlook {#S6}

Reaction rates, reaction order and activation barriers address questions different from thermodynamic equilibrium. Exponential time evolution is a testable model prediction.
