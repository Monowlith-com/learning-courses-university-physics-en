# M4-L4 – Travelling Waves and Dispersion {#L4}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Derivatives, trigonometric functions and coupled oscillators. [Previous lesson](M4-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish and apply the wave equation, phase velocity and group velocity.

## A Disturbance Travels {#S1}

A **wave** is a disturbance varying in space and time that can transfer energy and information. In a transverse wave the displacement is perpendicular to the propagation direction; in a longitudinal wave it is parallel. A basic field is $y(x,t)=A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, with wavenumber $k=2\pi/\lambda$ and angular frequency $\omega=2\pi f$. Local oscillation at a fixed position is distinct from the spatial pattern. The material of a medium need not be transported as a whole at the wave speed.

## Phase and Group {#S2}

In an idealised one-dimensional nondispersive medium a scalar displacement $y$ satisfies

$$
\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}.
$$

For a harmonic wave $v=\omega/k=\lambda f$. In a **dispersive** medium the relation between $\omega$ and $k$ is nonlinear. A fixed phase $kx-\omega t=\mathrm{const}$ travels at

$$
v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{\omega}{k},\qquad v_{\mathrm g}=\frac{\mathrm d\omega}{\mathrm dk}.
$$

For narrow-band wave packets, group velocity approximately describes propagation of the envelope. It is not necessarily identical to the information or signal velocity in all circumstances.

## Worked Example: Dispersive Wave Packets {#S3}

Take the mathematical model $\omega(k)=c k+\alpha k^2$ for positive $k$, with $c=3.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\alpha=0.20\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$ and $k=5.0\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Then $\omega=15+5=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, giving $v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\omega/k=4.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{\mathrm g}=c+2\alpha k=5.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The values differ because the dispersion is nonlinear. For a narrow-band packet in this model, the envelope moves faster than individual phase peaks. These numbers illustrate a mathematical dispersion relation, not a specified physical medium.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. With $f=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\lambda=0.40\,\mathrm m$, find the speed $v$ of a nondispersive wave.
2. For the SI-consistent model $\omega=3k^2$, calculate $v_{\mathrm{ph}}$ and $v_{\mathrm g}$ at $k=2\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$.
3. What distinguishes group velocity from phase velocity?

### Solutions

1. $v=\lambda f=20\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
2. $v_{\mathrm{ph}}=3k=6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{\mathrm g}=6k=12\,\mathrm{m/s}$; the coefficient 3 carries the required SI units.
3. Phase velocity $v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\omega/k$ tracks a constant-phase point; group velocity $v_{\mathrm g}=\mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk$ describes the motion of the envelope of a narrow-band wave packet.

## Limits of the Wave Picture {#S5}

In some media different frequency components propagate at different group velocities, so a pulse broadens. The group velocity $v_{\mathrm g}$ does not automatically equal energy-transport velocity in every situation, for example in strongly absorbing or complex dispersive media. Mechanical waves additionally depend on density, stress and boundary conditions; electromagnetic waves require different field equations. The ideal scalar wave equation is therefore a valuable reference model, not a universal law for every wave type.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Waves propagate and can superpose. Superposition leads to interference and standing patterns.
