# M17-L1 – Numerical Models, Floating-Point Arithmetic and Reproducibility {#L1}

**Course area:** M17 – Bachelor: Computational Physics and Simulation

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M2: functions and numbers; M16: measurement uncertainty; basic Python. [Previous lesson](M16-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish floating-point roundoff from model error, determine machine spacing and document reproducible computations.

## A Numerical Experiment as a Model {#S1}

A numerical simulation represents a selected aspect of reality, not reality itself. Its workflow consists of a physical model, initial and boundary conditions, numerical method, parameters, implementation and analysis. Even a dimensionless equation can be fed inconsistent inputs: record the units, validity range and precision of every quantity before execution. For an ordinary differential equation $dx/dt=f(x,t)$, the time axis is sampled at $t_n$ with step $h$. The results are approximations rather than observations. Distinguish model inadequacy, input uncertainty, discretization error and roundoff. Printing many decimal places does not establish accuracy.

## Discretization and Floating Point {#S2}

Binary floating point stores only finitely many significant digits. The gap between $1$ and the next larger Python `float` is `math.ulp(1.0)`, approximately $2.22\times10^{-16}$. Spacing varies with magnitude. Floating-point addition is not associative: reordering terms can discard or preserve a small contribution. Subtracting nearly equal numbers may magnify relative error. With a convergent discretization, reducing $h$ initially reduces discretization error, but accumulated roundoff can eventually dominate. The relevant tolerance depends on the size of the physical effect, not on an isolated software setting.

$$
\operatorname{fl}(a+b)= (a+b)(1+\delta),\qquad |\delta|\lesssim u\quad\text{(normal range)}.
$$

## Example: Cancellation in Sums {#S3}

Consider $10^{16}$, $1$ and $-10^{16}$. Their exact mathematical sum is $1$. Left-to-right summation in IEEE-754 double precision may yield $0$ because the intermediate addition rounds away the unit contribution. A higher-precision accumulation restores $1$ for this example. The numbers deliberately form a severe numerical stress test; real observations also demand assessment of physical measurement uncertainty. A usable result records the value, unit, numerical method, tolerance and software versions.

**Executable Python example:**

```python
import math
values = [1e16, 1.0, -1e16]
naive = 0.0
for value in values:
    naive += value
print(naive)
print(math.fsum(values))
print(math.ulp(1.0))
```

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Why is `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` generally false in binary floating point?
2. What is the exact value of $10^{16}+1-10^{16}$, and what may naïve arithmetic return?
3. Identify two reproducibility requirements beyond source code.

### Solutions

1. These decimal fractions are not exactly represented by finite binary fractions; rounding produces a small difference.
2. Exactly $1$; a floating-point evaluation can yield $0$.
3. For example input parameters and software versions; for stochastic work, generator and seed.

## Reproducibility and Validity {#S5}

A simulation is reproducible when its inputs, dependencies, initial conditions and analysis steps are preserved. Stochastic calculations also require the generator and seed; a fixed seed enables repetition without proving the physical model correct. For each model reduction identify expected conserved quantities, for example energy, charge or normalization. Start with problems admitting analytic solutions, then perform step-size or grid-refinement studies. Agreement for a single parameter choice is not physical validation.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Numerical credibility requires error budgets, tests and recorded run conditions rather than spurious precision.
