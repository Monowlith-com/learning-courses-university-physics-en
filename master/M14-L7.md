# M14-L7 – Magnetic Order and Spin Interactions {#L7}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M6, M11-L5, M13-L8 and thermodynamics. [Previous lesson](M14-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish dia-, para- and ferromagnetism and connect simple spin models to bulk magnetization.

## Different Responses to Magnetic Fields {#S1}

An applied magnetic field can induce magnetization $\mathbf M$ through electron orbital motion and spin. Diamagnetic contributions occur in many materials and oppose an added field weakly. Paramagnetic moments preferentially align with the field, while thermal agitation disrupts their order. Ferromagnets below a characteristic transition temperature exhibit spontaneous ordering even without an applied field and may form domains. Magnetization is magnetic moment per volume, with SI units $\mathrm{A/m}$. A linear isotropic medium approximately follows $\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H$, but a constant field-independent susceptibility $\chi$ does not generally describe the full behavior of ferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic materials.

## Interacting Spins and Order {#S2}

For localized Ising spins $s_i=\pm1$, a minimal model is $H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j-\mu B\sum_i s_i$. Positive $J$ favors neighboring spins aligned in parallel. At high temperature disorder often dominates, whereas long-range order can form at sufficiently low temperature if the dimension and interactions allow it. In the ideal limit of independent two-orientation moments of magnitude $\mu$,

$$
M=n\mu\tanh\!\left(\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}\right).
$$

When $\lvert\mu B\rvert\ll k_BT$ this becomes $M\approx n\mu^2 B/(k_BT)$. Here $n$ is the moment density and $B$ the local magnetic induction; in dilute noninteracting samples one may approximately use $B\approx\mu_0H$.

## Example: The Curie Law Limit {#S3}

Take the dimensionless field $x=\mu B/(k_BT)=0.10$. For the independent two-orientation model,

$$
\frac{M}{n\mu}=\tanh(0.10)\approx0.099668.
$$

The linear Curie approximation gives $0.10$, a relative deviation of about $0.33\%$ in this example. At $x=1$, however, $\tanh1\approx0.762$ is substantially below the linear prediction of $1$. The plot displays the reversible saturation curve for noninteracting moments; it contains no hysteresis loop. Ferromagnetic hysteresis requires additional physics such as domain-wall motion, anisotropy, nucleation and dissipation. Nonlinear magnetization alone therefore does not prove ferromagnetic order.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Calculate $M/(n\mu)$ at $x=0$.
2. What limit holds when $x\gg1$?
3. Why is the drawn paramagnetic curve not a ferromagnetic hysteresis loop?

### Solutions

1. $\tanh0=0$.
2. $M/(n\mu)\to1$ (saturation).
3. It is single-valued and reversible; domain pinning and history dependence are absent.

## Domains, Hysteresis and Material Limits {#S5}

Real magnetic solids involve dipolar interactions, magnetocrystalline anisotropy, disorder and surfaces in addition to exchange. Antiferromagnets often favor alternating opposite moments, while ferrimagnets have unequal opposing sublattice moments with a nonzero net result. Ising spins are discrete and cannot represent arbitrary spin directions or all orbital effects. Long-range order also depends on dimension, symmetry and interaction range. Curie–Weiss behavior is a useful phenomenology, not an exact law for every material. Care is needed to distinguish magnetic field strength $H$, induction $B$ and demagnetizing fields inside finite samples.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Spin interactions can generate magnetic order, while temperature, symmetry and defects determine the observed response.
