# M15-L4 – Interaction of Ionizing Radiation with Matter {#L4}

**Course area:** M15 – Bachelor: Nuclear and Particle Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M11-L2, M15-L2, exponential decay and energy conservation. [Previous lesson](M15-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish photon attenuation from charged-particle tracks and calculate a simple shielding model.

## Photons and Charged Particles {#S1}

X-ray and gamma photons are uncharged. They transfer energy through photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production. Dominance depends on photon energy and material; pair creation in a nuclear Coulomb field requires at least $2m_ec^2$ in photon energy. Charged alpha particles, electrons and protons instead lose energy mainly through many electromagnetic collisions and ionizations. Their ranges and tracks differ considerably. A claim about penetration requires particle type, energy and target material. Radiation protection must distinguish source activity, radiation field, deposited energy and biological effect.

## Narrow-Beam Exponential Attenuation {#S2}

For a narrow, monoenergetic photon beam in which only unscattered primary photons are counted, $dI/dx=-\mu I$ is a useful model. Its solution is

$$
I(x)=I_0e^{-\mu x},\qquad x_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\mu}.
$$

The linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$ is measured in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; NIST often tabulates mass attenuation coefficient $\mu/\rho$, with $\mu=(\mu/\rho)\rho$ for a homogeneous material. Attenuation removes photons from the primary beam but is not synonymous with complete energy absorption. Broad-beam geometry admits scattered photons and needs build-up and transport models. Coefficient $\mu$ varies strongly with photon energy.

## Example: Half-Value Layer {#S3}

For $\mu=0.50\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the half-value layer is $x_{1/2}=\ln2/0.50\approx1.386\,\mathrm{cm}$. A plate of thickness $x=3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ transmits the ideal narrow-beam primary fraction

$$
I/I_0=e^{-1.5}\approx0.223.
$$

The figure plots exponential primary-beam attenuation in a uniform slab and marks the half-value layer. It is not a clinical dose distribution. To convert NIST mass attenuation values into geometrical length, material density must be included. A mixture of energies and scattered components cannot generally be represented by one universal attenuation factor.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $I/I_0$ at $x=2x_{1/2}$.
2. Calculate $\mu$ for $x_{1/2}=2\,\mathrm{cm}$.
3. Why is half-value layer not a dose value?

### Solutions

1. $1/4$.
2. $\mu\approx0.3466\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.
3. It describes removal from a specified primary beam; dose depends on energy deposition in the material.

## Dose, Scattering and Limits {#S5}

Absorbed dose $D$ is deposited energy per mass, $1\,\mathrm{Gy}=1\,\mathrm{J/kg}$; equivalent and effective dose additionally use biological weighting factors and are quoted in sievert. Gray and sievert must not be casually equated. Radiation effects depend on energy, exposure configuration and tissue; practical protection follows established radiation-safety standards rather than an attenuation toy model. Electrons may produce bremsstrahlung, and neutrons need different shielding models. Measurements also depend on background, geometry, efficiency and spectral response.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Attenuation, activity and dose are different quantities; exponential transmission needs precise geometrical assumptions.
