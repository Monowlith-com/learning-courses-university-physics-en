# M4-L1 – Simple Harmonic Oscillations {#L1}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Forces, acceleration, differential equations and energy from M2/M3. [Previous lesson](M3-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive the equation of motion from a linear restoring force and determine period, phase and energy.

## The Linear Oscillator {#S1}

An **oscillation** is motion about a stable equilibrium. For small displacements, the restoring force is often approximately proportional to the displacement. In an ideal mass–spring system $F_x=-kx$; the minus sign indicates that the force points back toward equilibrium. With neither friction nor external driving, **simple harmonic motion** results. Position, velocity and acceleration are periodic; velocity vanishes at turning points even though the magnitude of acceleration may be greatest there. Not every periodic motion is harmonic: the linear equation of motion is essential.

## Equation of Motion and Energy {#S2}

Newton's second law gives $m\ddot x=-kx$. The trial solution $x(t)=A\cos(\omega_0t+\varphi)$ has second derivative $\ddot x=-\omega_0^2x$. Matching coefficients yields

$$
\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}.
$$

The general real solution can also be expressed as a combination of sine and cosine. $A$ denotes amplitude, $\varphi$ initial phase and $\omega_0$ angular frequency in $\mathrm{rad/s}$ rather than hertz. The energy remains constant:

$$
E=\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12kA^2.
$$

This describes the continuous exchange of kinetic and elastic potential energy.

## Worked Example: Mass–Spring System {#S3}

A mass $m=0.50\,\mathrm{kg}$ on an ideal spring with $k=200\,\mathrm{N/m}$ is released at a displacement $A=0.10\,\mathrm m$ from equilibrium. Then $\omega_0=\sqrt{200/0.50}=20\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so $T=2\pi/20\approx0.314\,\mathrm s$ and $f\approx3.18\,\mathrm{Hz}$. With $x(0)=A$ and $\dot x(0)=0$, the trajectory is $x(t)=0.10\cos(20t)\,\mathrm m$. Its peak speed is $A\omega_0=2.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$; the total energy is $1.0\,\mathrm J$. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\omega_0$ and $T$ for $m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $k=50\,\mathrm{N/m}$.
2. The amplitude is doubled with the same spring. How do peak velocity and energy change?
3. Why is the magnitude of acceleration greatest at a turning point?

### Solutions

1. $\omega_0=\sqrt{50/2}=5.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, $T=2\pi/5\approx1.257\,\mathrm s$.
2. $v_{\max}=A\omega_0$ doubles, while $E=\tfrac12kA^2$ quadruples.
3. Since $a=-\omega_0^2x$, $|a|$ is largest when $|x|=A$.

## Model Limits and Phase Space {#S5}

The ideal solution assumes constant mass, a linear spring, no losses and fixed boundary conditions. At large amplitudes, a restoring force may become nonlinear; the small-angle approximation for a physical pendulum is valid only within an appropriate range. In state space, plotting $x$ against $\dot x$, a lossless harmonic oscillator traces a closed ellipse. With friction, a trajectory is no longer closed. For this linear system the **natural frequency** depends on $k$ and $m$, not on the selected amplitude.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Simple harmonic motion is the reference model for oscillations. We next add losses and periodic forcing.
