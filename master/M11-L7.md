# M11-L7 – Lasers, Stimulated Emission and Optical Pumping {#L7}

**Course area:** M11 – Bachelor: Foundations of Quantum Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Photons, atomic energy levels and electromagnetic cavities; M7 and M11-L4. [Previous lesson](M11-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish absorption, spontaneous and stimulated emission, and explain population inversion and cavity conditions.

## Three Light–Matter Processes {#S1}

A laser produces directed, often narrow-band coherent radiation by amplifying photons through stimulated emission in an active medium. A suitable photon interacting with an excited atom or molecule can induce a transition to a lower-energy level, adding another photon to the corresponding electromagnetic mode. Absorption and spontaneous emission compete with this process. Spontaneous emission occurs even without a matching incident photon and can seed the growth of the laser field. Laser light is not necessarily perfectly monochromatic, divergence-free or inherently harmless; real properties depend on the medium, resonator and operating mode.

## Inversion and the Gain Condition {#S2}

For two levels separated by $\Delta E$, resonance requires $h\nu=\Delta E$. Stimulated transition rate per particle is proportional to spectral radiation energy density $\rho(\nu)$. In a simplified equal-degeneracy model, stimulated emission exceeds absorption only if $N_2>N_1$, a population inversion. In general statistical weights matter: gain requires $N_2/g_2>N_1/g_1$. Pure resonant steady pumping of an ideal two-level system cannot sustain a population exceeding half of the particles; practical lasers often use three or four relevant energy levels. In a linear Fabry–Pérot cavity of length $L$ in air, the standing-wave condition $m\lambda=2L$ gives the approximate free spectral range

$$
\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\simeq\frac{c}{2L}.
$$

Lasing starts only when round-trip gain balances losses.

## Example: Laser Wavelength and Mode Spacing {#S3}

For a laser wavelength $\lambda=633\,\mathrm{nm}$, photon energy $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$ is about $1.96\,\mathrm{eV}$, and frequency is $\nu\simeq4.74\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. An air-filled cavity of length $L=0.30\,\mathrm m$ has an ideal longitudinal mode spacing $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\simeq5.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{Hz}$, or about $500\,\mathrm{MHz}$. This does not mean that exactly one mode necessarily oscillates: gain bandwidth, losses and mode selection determine which modes actually lase. ## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find photon energy for $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. Find $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}$ for an air cavity with $L=0.60\,\mathrm m$.
3. Why is an ideal two-level optical pump insufficient for sustained inversion?

### Solutions

1. $E_\gamma\simeq1240/500\,\mathrm{eV}=2.48\,\mathrm{eV}$.
2. $\Delta\nu_\mathrm{FSR}\simeq c/(2L)\simeq250\,\mathrm{MHz}$.
3. Pump light also stimulates downward transitions; steady populations approach equality rather than inversion.

## Optical Pumping and Practical Limits {#S5}

Optical pumping redistributes populations through absorption of selected frequencies followed by relaxation pathways into a state from which laser emission is possible. In other laser types an electrical discharge or current injection provides energy. An output coupler lets some field leave while the remainder continues to circulate and amplify. Thermal loading, optical losses, saturation and pump efficiency limit output power and stability. For safety, wavelength, beam diameter, optical power and pulse duration matter: even modest optical powers can be dangerous to the retina.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
Stimulated emission provides gain only with suitable populations and feedback; lasers require pumping, inversion and controlled losses.
