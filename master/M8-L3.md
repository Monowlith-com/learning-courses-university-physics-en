# M8-L3 – Fourier Series and the Continuous Fourier Transform {#L3}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Trigonometry, integration and complex exponentials. [Previous lesson](M8-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Find Fourier coefficients from orthogonality and distinguish spectra from reconstruction.

## Periodic Signals as Mode Sums {#S1}

A periodic oscillation may look complicated while being assembled from simple harmonic components. For suitably regular periodic functions, a Fourier series represents the signal as a superposition of harmonics with frequencies $n\omega_0$. For period $2\pi$, choose the basis $1,\cos(nx),\sin(nx)$. Fourier coefficients measure contributions of these modes; they do not represent additional physical forces. In experimental physics spectra reveal harmonics, resonances and periodic interference. However, a frequency spectrum must always be read together with its normalization, recording duration and Fourier convention; otherwise even amplitude values may not be directly comparable.

## Coefficients from Orthogonality {#S2}

Orthogonality on $[-\pi,\pi]$ gives $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(nx)\sin(mx)\,\mathrm dx=\pi\delta_{nm}$ for positive integers $n,m$. An analogous statement holds for cosine modes, while mixed integrals vanish. Consequently,

$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\right],
$$

where $a_n=\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(nx)\,\mathrm dx$ and $b_n=\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin(nx)\,\mathrm dx$. At a jump discontinuity, under the usual Dirichlet conditions the series converges to the average of the two one-sided limits. This is a property of convergence, not a failure of integration.

## Example: The Sawtooth Function {#S3}

Take the odd function $f(x)=x$ for $-\pi<x<\pi$ and extend it periodically. Symmetry makes all cosine coefficients vanish. Integration by parts yields $b_n=\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,\mathrm dx=2(-1)^{n+1}/n$. Thus

$$
f(x)\sim 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx).
$$

At $x=0$ the function and series agree, whereas at the jump $x=\pi$ the Fourier series takes the average value zero. The diagram compares the sawtooth with a finite partial sum and displays the overshoot near the discontinuity. Adding more harmonics narrows that overshoot spatially but does not eliminate its limiting relative amplitude.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What cosine Fourier coefficients $a_n$ does the odd function $f(x)=x$ have?
2. Find the sawtooth coefficient $b_2$.
3. What value does the series take at the jump $x=\pi$?

### Solutions

1. All $a_n=0$, including $a_0$.
2. $b_2=2(-1)^3/2=-1$.
3. The average of $+\pi$ and $-\pi$, namely $0$.

## Continuous Spectrum and Limitations {#S5}

Nonperiodic signals often call for a continuous spectrum. One convention is $\hat f(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,\mathrm dx$ with $f(x)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(k)e^{ikx}\,\mathrm dk$. These integrals require suitable integrability or distributional assumptions. A recording of finite duration never yields the ideal spectrum of an infinitely long signal: windowing introduces spectral leakage. Fourier transformation is linear, so the spectrum of a sum is the sum of complex spectra. Interference between contributions can emerge after taking absolute values. A measured power spectrum generally lacks the full phase information required for a unique reconstruction.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Orthogonality produces Fourier coefficients. The next method often handles time-domain initial-value problems more easily in the Laplace domain.
