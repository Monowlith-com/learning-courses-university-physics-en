# M12-L6 – Time-Independent Perturbation Theory and Splitting {#L6}

**Course area:** M12 – Bachelor: Advanced Quantum Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Eigenstates, expectation values, matrix elements and angular momentum coupling; M12-L1 and M12-L5. [Previous lesson](M12-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Calculate first- and second-order energy shifts and correctly handle degeneracy.

## From Solvable Model to Small Correction {#S1}

Many quantum Hamiltonians cannot be diagonalized in closed form. If a solvable reference $H_0$ is available, an additional interaction $V$ can be organized using $H=H_0+\lambda V$. The formal parameter $\lambda$ tracks approximation order; physical smallness must be judged relative to energy gaps and matrix elements. Perturbation theory is neither arbitrary fitting nor a guarantee that all spectra change only slightly. Close to degenerate levels, even weak coupling can strongly rearrange the eigenstates.

## Nondegenerate and Degenerate Formulae {#S2}

For a normalized nondegenerate reference state $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, expansion of energy and wavefunction gives

$$
E_n^{(1)}=\langle n^{(0)}|V|n^{(0)}\rangle
$$

Second-order energy correction for an isolated discrete level is 

$$
E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\langle m^{(0)}|V|n^{(0)}\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}
$$

Thus $E_n\simeq E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2 E_n^{(2)}$. For degenerate levels, a denominator vanishes and this formula cannot be used directly. One must first diagonalize the restriction of $V$ within the degenerate subspace to identify the appropriate combinations.

## Example: Coupled Two-State System {#S3}

Let a degenerate two-level system have $H_0=E_0I$ and $V=\begin{pmatrix}0&v\\v&0\end{pmatrix}$ with real $v>0$. Exact diagonalization of $H=H_0+\lambda V$ gives

$$
E_\pm=E_0\pm\lambda v
$$

The initial degeneracy splits by $2\lambda v$. The eigenstates are symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the original basis states. The figure shows one energy level separating into two branches as coupling increases. Applying the nondegenerate second-order formula here would divide by zero, immediately revealing that its assumptions are violated.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. For $V_{nn}=0.030\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $\lambda=0.1$, find the first-order shift.
2. Find the splitting for $v=0.020\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $\lambda=1$.
3. Why must a degenerate subspace be diagonalized first?

### Solutions

1. $\lambda V_{nn}=0.0030\,\mathrm{eV}$.
2. $2\lambda v=0.040\,\mathrm{eV}$.
3. The nondegenerate formula would contain zero energy denominators.

## When the Approximation Breaks Down {#S5}

Nondegenerate perturbation theory requires coupling to other states to be small compared with relevant energy separations. Continua and resonances may demand scattering methods or regularization. Even with a small parameter, high-order series can be asymptotic rather than convergent. In practice, estimate the next correction or compare the result with numerical diagonalization of a tractable truncated matrix. The sign of a second-order shift is not universally positive: the energy denominator matters.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Perturbation theory systematically treats weak interactions; degeneracies require the right basis first.
