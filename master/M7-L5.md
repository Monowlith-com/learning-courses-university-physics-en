# M7-L5 – Fourier Optics and Resolving Power {#L5}

**Course area:** M7 – Bachelor: Optics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Complex exponentials, integration and diffraction. [Previous lesson](M7-L4.md).

**Learning objective:** Interpret the Fraunhofer field as a Fourier transform of the pupil and calculate diffraction limits.

## The Pupil as a Complex Field {#S1}

Every finite aperture truncates the incident electromagnetic field. A complex pupil function $P(x,y)$ describes this action: its magnitude specifies transmitted field amplitude and its argument the phase shift. Two openings of equal geometrical area can produce different diffraction patterns if their amplitude and phase profiles differ. Coherent imaging propagates field amplitude; incoherent illumination requires averaging intensities under suitable assumptions. A lens can make the spatial-frequency content visible in its focal plane. Fourier optics connects this diffraction process to a mathematical transform of the complex optical field.

## From Spatial Field to Far Field {#S2}

In scalar Fraunhofer theory, the far-field amplitude at angular coordinates $\theta_x,\theta_y$ is proportional to

$$
U(\theta_x,\theta_y)\propto\iint P(x,y)\exp\!\left[-i\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(x\sin\theta_x+y\sin\theta_y)\right]\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy.
$$

The detector measures $I\propto|U|^2$, so intensity alone does not preserve all Fourier phase information. A computation must specify its Fourier-transform convention (sign and normalization). For a circular, uniformly illuminated aperture, an Airy-type diffraction pattern follows. Its first dark angular ring occurs approximately at $\theta\approx1.22\lambda/D$ for small angles, where $D$ is aperture diameter. The Rayleigh criterion considers two point images just resolved when separated by this angle; other criteria produce different thresholds.

## Worked Example: Circular Aperture {#S3}

An ideal telescope with $D=0.10\,\mathrm m$ and $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$ has Rayleigh angle $\theta_R\approx1.22\cdot550\times10^{-9}/0.10=6.71\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{rad}$, or about $1.38$ arcseconds. Doubling the diameter halves this diffraction-angle limit at fixed wavelength. The diagram shows a circular pupil and its broad central diffraction maximum surrounded by weaker rings. In laboratories real point-spread functions differ because of defocus, aberrations and the measured optical bandwidth. The calculated Rayleigh limit is an ideal diffraction-geometric measure, not a guarantee of separation for every possible object.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $\theta_R$ change when $D$ is tripled?
2. Compute $\theta_R$ for $D=0.20\,\mathrm m$, $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$.
3. Why is measured far-field intensity sometimes insufficient to reconstruct the pupil?

### Solutions

1. It decreases to one third.
2. $\theta_R=3.05\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{rad}$.
3. Intensity gives the squared modulus of the Fourier amplitude, but not its phase.

## Resolution Is a Criterion {#S5}

The Rayleigh criterion is not an absolute information bound: noise, contrast, pixel sampling, prior information and reconstruction algorithms influence what can be distinguished. A larger image is not necessarily better resolved; magnification and the physical point-spread function are distinct quantities. Scalar theory fails for strongly focused, high-numerical-aperture vector fields. To compare real instruments, examine the measured point-spread function, modulation transfer and illumination conditions rather than just one formula.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The pupil determines the far field through a Fourier relation. Temporal pulses also have spectra; their propagation leads to dispersion.
