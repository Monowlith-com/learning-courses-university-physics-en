# M3-L7 – Gravitation and Keplerian Orbits {#L7}

**Course area:** M3 – Bachelor: Classical Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Circular motion, potential energy and conservation of energy. [Previous lesson](M3-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive and apply gravitational force, circular-orbit speed and Kepler’s orbital laws.

## Central Force and Orbit Shape {#S1}

Newtonian gravitation is an attractive interaction between masses. In the point-mass model, the force acts along the line joining the bodies and falls with the square of their separation. A small satellite outside an approximately spherically symmetric central mass $M$ can be treated using the point-mass result. A **circular orbit** is a special case; bound Kepler orbits are generally ellipses, with the central mass at one focus. Force and orbital velocity continuously change their vector directions.

## Gravity, Potential and Circular Orbit {#S2}

For masses separated by $r$, the Newtonian model gives

$$
|\mathbf F|=G\frac{Mm}{r^2},\qquad
U(r)=-G\frac{Mm}{r},\qquad
v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}.
$$

Circular speed follows by equating $mv^2/r=GMm/r^2$ and presumes an ideal circular orbit. With potential zero at infinity and neglecting drag, the escape condition $\tfrac12mv^2-GMm/r\ge0$ gives $v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$. A negative value of gravitational potential energy does **not** imply negative total energy in every situation; the zero has been chosen to make binding meaningful.

## Worked Example: Circular Orbit Around Earth {#S3}

Use Earth's gravitational parameter $GM\approx3.986\cdot10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and an orbital radius $r=7.00\cdot10^6\,\mathrm m$ from Earth's centre. A small satellite has

$$
v=\sqrt{\frac{3.986\cdot10^{14}}{7.00\cdot10^6}}
\approx7.55\cdot10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

Its orbital period is $T=2\pi r/v\approx5.83\cdot10^3\,\mathrm s$, or about $97.1$ minutes. Kepler's third law for the same central mass reads $T^2=4\pi^2a^3/(GM)$, where $a$ is the semimajor axis of an elliptical orbit. The drawing shows the central mass at a focus of an ellipse, not at its centre.

![Elliptical orbit with central body at a focus, periapsis and apoapsis](../../../../media/course/U_PHY_139_EN.svg)

*The semimajor axis describes the orbit; the central body is not at the centre.*

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Double the radius of a circular orbit around the same central body. How does speed change?
2. Derive the ratio of escape speed to circular speed.
3. What orbital quantity replaces circle radius in Kepler's third law for an ellipse?

### Solutions

1. Since $v\propto r^{-1/2}$, speed becomes $1/\sqrt2$ of its previous value.
2. $v_{\mathrm{esc}}/v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt2$.
3. The semimajor axis $a$; for a circle, $a=r$.

## Limits of the Two-Body Model {#S5}

The Kepler model assumes an isolated two-body interaction with pointlike or suitably spherically symmetric masses. Real satellite orbits are perturbed by drag, Earth's oblateness and other gravitating bodies. Relativistic corrections can be necessary in very strong gravity or for high precision. The escape-speed expression assumes no subsequent propulsion and neglects those other influences.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
