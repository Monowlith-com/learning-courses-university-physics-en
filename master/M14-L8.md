# M14-L8 – Superconductivity, Meissner Effect and London Depth {#L8}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M6, M13-L5, M14-L3, magnetic fields and boundary-value equations. [Previous lesson](M14-L7.md).

**Learning objective:** Distinguish zero resistance from the Meissner effect and derive ideal magnetic screening and flux quantization.

## Two Hallmarks of the Superconducting State {#S1}

Below a critical temperature $T_c$, certain materials can enter a superconducting phase. Two observations must be distinguished: a steady current can persist under suitable conditions without measurable electrical resistance, and a weak external field applied during cooling is expelled from the bulk. The latter is the Meissner effect. A merely perfect conductor with $\rho=0$ would instead conserve an initially frozen magnetic flux; this difference indicates a distinct thermodynamic phase. Superconductivity can be suppressed by sufficiently high temperature, field or current density. In finite, imperfect samples magnetic screening does not occur as an infinitely sharp surface step.

## London Equation and Magnetic Screening {#S2}

In the London description the superconducting current responds locally to the electromagnetic vector potential in an appropriate gauge. Combining the second London relation $\nabla\times\mathbf j_s=-n_se^2\mathbf B/m$ with the static Maxwell law $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j_s$ gives, for a homogeneous material,

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf B}{\lambda_L^2},\qquad \lambda_L=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0n_se^2}}.
$$

For a superconducting half-space $x>0$ and a field parallel to its flat surface, constant parameters yield the decaying solution 

$$
B(x)=B(0)e^{-x/\lambda_L}.
$$

The London depth $\lambda_L$ describes magnetic screening, not an electron mean free path. Effective masses and anisotropic response are important refinements in real materials.

## Example: Field Penetration at a Surface {#S3}

For an ideal superconducting surface with London depth $\lambda_L=50\,\mathrm{nm}$, a distance $x=100\,\mathrm{nm}=2\lambda_L$ gives

$$
\frac{B(x)}{B(0)}=e^{-2}\approx0.1353.
$$

The figure shows that the field has dropped to about $36.8\%$ after one London depth and $13.5\%$ after two. This simple model applies only while fields remain within the relevant superconducting regime and vortex penetration can be neglected. A superconducting ring has a phase-based fluxoid constraint; in suitable limits the flux quantization scale is $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$. The charge $2e$ here refers to a Cooper pair.

3. Why does assuming $\rho=0$ alone not explain the Meissner effect?

### Solutions

1. $e^{-1}\approx0.3679$.
2. $e^{-3}\approx0.0498$.
3. Perfect conduction only preserves prior flux; equilibrium magnetic expulsion requires additional physics.

## Type I, Type II and Microscopic Explanation {#S5}

Ideal type-I superconductors have a critical field beyond which superconductivity is destroyed in the equilibrium picture. Type-II materials can support a mixed phase of quantized vortices between lower and upper critical fields, where a single exponential profile is incomplete. Microscopic BCS theory often explains conventional pairing through an effective phonon-mediated attraction. Not all superconductors follow this simplest pairing mechanism, and London theory alone cannot determine the critical temperature. Finite screening currents and London penetration do not contradict zero resistance. Meissner screening, critical fields and flux pinning are experimentally distinct issues.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}
The Meissner effect characterizes a distinct phase; London equations describe its magnetic screening in an elementary limit.
