# M38-L2 – Geostrophic Wind and Rotating Flows {#L2}

**Course area:** M38 – Master: Geophysics, Atmospheric Physics and Climate Models

**Level:** Master · advanced specialization

**Prerequisites:** M6: fluid mechanics; M19: rotating frames; M38-L1 · [Previous lesson](M38-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Balance horizontal pressure gradients against the Coriolis term, and identify limits near the equator and in frictional layers.

## Guiding question and context {#S1}

Earth rotation deflects large-scale atmospheric and oceanic flow. In east–north–up coordinates, ideal geostrophic horizontal velocity follows isobars. This is not an extra physical force or a model of every gust; it is a steady momentum balance when friction and local acceleration are small.

## Physical model and derivation {#S2}

With $\mathbf v_g=(u_g,v_g,0)$, $f=2\Omega\sin\varphi$ and the horizontal pressure gradient, geostrophic balance is $f\,\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf v_g=-\nabla_h p/\rho$. Thus $u_g=-\partial_y p/(\rho f)$ and $v_g=\partial_x p/(\rho f)$. A northward pressure decrease yields an eastward geostrophic wind in the Northern Hemisphere, where $f>0$. Vertical hydrostatic balance $\partial_z p=-\rho g$ is a distinct approximation, not another form of the horizontal relation.

$$
f\,\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf v_g=-\frac{\nabla_h p}{\rho},\qquad u_g=-\frac{\partial_y p}{\rho f}.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

For illustrative $f=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $\rho=1.2\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\partial_y p=-1.2\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}}$, one obtains $u_g=+10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The positive sign means east. The Coriolis acceleration points south and balances the northward pressure-gradient acceleration. The diagram shows directions, not operational weather data.

## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $u_g$ change when $|f|$ doubles at fixed pressure gradient?
2. Where does the pressure-gradient force point when $p$ decreases northward?
3. Why can the geostrophic formula not be evaluated by dividing by $f=0$ at the equator?

### Solutions

1. Its magnitude halves, with sign determined by $f$.
2. Northward, opposite to the pressure gradient.
3. The dominant balance changes; both the approximation and division by $f$ fail.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

This balance is most appropriate for large-scale, rotation-dominated, nearly frictionless flow away from the equator. Boundary layers, fronts and strong curvature require ageostrophic terms. Signs assume the stated coordinate convention.
