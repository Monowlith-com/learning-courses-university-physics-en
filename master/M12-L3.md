# M12-L3 – Potential Steps, Tunnelling and Scattering {#L3}

**Course area:** M12 – Bachelor: Advanced Quantum Mechanics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Schrödinger equation, complex exponentials and current conservation; M12-L2. [Previous lesson](M12-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Match wave solutions at interfaces and distinguish quantum transmission from classical barrier crossing.

## A Quantum Particle Encounters a Barrier {#S1}

Classically, a particle with energy $E<V_0$ cannot cross a potential barrier of height $V_0$. In quantum mechanics the stationary wavefunction generally has a nonzero amplitude inside the classically forbidden region; for finite barrier width this allows nonzero transmission. The particle need not 'borrow energy' to cross. Tunnelling follows directly from the Schrödinger equation and boundary conditions. A forbidden region extending indefinitely in the propagation direction has no transmitted outgoing wave beyond it; finite width is essential.

## Boundary Matching and Current Flux {#S2}

For a rectangular barrier $V(x)=V_0$ at $0<x<a$, and $V=0$ outside, with $0<E<V_0$, the outside wavenumber is $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and the decay constant inside is $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. Outside one combines incident, reflected and transmitted travelling waves; inside the solution contains both $e^{\kappa x}$ and $e^{-\kappa x}$. At finite potential steps both $\psi$ and $\partial_x\psi$ are continuous. Probability current is

$$
j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\partial_x\psi)
$$

Because both outer regions have equal potential, $T=j_{\mathrm{trans}}/j_{\mathrm{in}}$. For a rectangular barrier the exact expression is 

$$
T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}
$$

## Example: Exponential Penetration {#S3}

For an electron with $V_0-E=1.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ one obtains $\kappa\simeq5.12\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. At barrier width $a=0.50\,\mathrm{nm}$ the attenuation factor is $e^{-2\kappa a}\simeq5.96\times10^{-3}$. This exponential factor is **not** itself the exact transmission probability: the prefactor depends on $E$ and $V_0$, and small $\kappa a$ invalidates the simple asymptotic approximation. A complete numerical transmission needs both energy values and the matching conditions. The figure displays a rectangular potential and schematic incident and decaying amplitudes.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. How does $e^{-2\kappa a}$ change if $a$ doubles?
2. Why is $\psi=0$ throughout the barrier generally invalid?
3. Is the attenuation factor already the exact $T$?

### Solutions

1. The exponent doubles and the factor is squared.
2. It generally violates interface matching for a nonzero incident wave.
3. No. The full transmission expression includes interface factors.

## From Ideal Model to Devices {#S5}

Electron tunnelling is central to scanning tunnelling microscopy and thin insulating junctions. Transmission can vary exponentially with separation, making very small geometric changes detectable. Real transport is often three-dimensional and depends on effective masses, electronic band structure and interface states. The one-dimensional rectangular barrier is a teaching model rather than a universal formula for tunnel devices. Absorbing potentials and time-dependent processes can require a generalization of the simplest $R+T=1$ current balance.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Quantum transmission through finite barriers follows directly from wave matching and probability-current conservation.
