# M4-L2 – Damped and Driven Oscillations {#L2}

**Course area:** M4 – Bachelor: Oscillations, Waves and Fluids

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Simple harmonic oscillator and complex exponential notation. [Previous lesson](M4-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Compare damping regimes and the frequency response of a linearly driven oscillator quantitatively.

## Dissipation and External Driving {#S1}

Real oscillators lose mechanical energy through friction, fluid drag or internal material losses. A common approximation is a damping force proportional to velocity, $F_d=-b\dot x$. If an additional sinusoidal external force acts, stored energy, dissipation and periodically supplied work compete. **Resonance** is a pronounced dependence of the linear response on the driving frequency; it does not necessarily mean an unbounded amplitude. Damping controls both the free motion and the height and shape of the frequency-response curve.

## Transient and Steady-State Solutions {#S2}

For a linear oscillator of constant mass,

$$
m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos(\Omega t).
$$

With $\gamma=b/(2m)$ and $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ the characteristic polynomial of the free motion is $r^2+2\gamma r+\omega_0^2=0$. In the underdamped case $\gamma<\omega_0$ the free solution oscillates at $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$; $\gamma=\omega_0$ is critical damping. A steady-state ansatz gives displacement amplitude

$$
A(\Omega)=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\Omega^2)^2+(2\gamma\Omega)^2}}.
$$

The transient part decays for positive damping, while the drive sustains the steady-state response.

## Worked Example: Resonance Curve {#S3}

Take $m=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=100\,\mathrm{N/m}$, $b=2.0\,\mathrm{kg/s}$ and $F_0=1.0\,\mathrm N$. Thus $\omega_0=10\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\gamma=1.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. At $\Omega=0$, the steady response amplitude is $A(0)=F_0/k=0.010\,\mathrm m$. At $\Omega=\omega_0$ it is $A(\omega_0)=1/(2\cdot1\cdot10)=0.050\,\mathrm m$. The displacement maximum occurs at $\Omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2}\approx9.90\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ when $\omega_0^2>2\gamma^2$. The diagram therefore places the peak slightly below the undamped natural frequency.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Compute $\gamma$ for $m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $b=4.0\,\mathrm{kg/s}$.
2. For $\omega_0=8.0\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\gamma=1.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, compute $\omega_d$.
3. Why is $A(\Omega)$ finite when damping is present?

### Solutions

1. $\gamma=b/(2m)=1.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
2. $\omega_d=\sqrt{64-1}=\sqrt{63}\approx7.94\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.
3. The positive term $(2\gamma\Omega)^2$ prevents the denominator from vanishing near resonance; dissipative power limits the steady amplitude.

## What Resonance Does Not Mean {#S5}

For weak damping, the quality factor is approximately $Q\approx\omega_0/(2\gamma)$; it characterises a narrow, pronounced resonance. A different measured quantity, such as velocity rather than displacement, can peak at another frequency. Nonlinearity, variable friction and material limits can cause departures from the model. Resonance is not always damaging or always useful: it enables filters and instruments but must also be considered when designing structures.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The response to driving is frequency selective. Coupled oscillators have multiple characteristic modes.
