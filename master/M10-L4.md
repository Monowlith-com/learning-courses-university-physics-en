# M10-L4 – Energy, Poynting Vector and Field Momentum {#L4}

**Course area:** M10 – Bachelor: Advanced Electrodynamics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Vacuum Maxwell equations, dot and cross products. [Previous lesson](M10-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive field-energy conservation, calculate plane-wave intensity and explain field momentum.

## Energy in Electric and Magnetic Fields {#S1}

Field energy is measurable: an antenna receives power, a photovoltaic cell is illuminated, and light can exert a force. In classical field theory, energy is distributed spatially. Its local change is balanced by work on charged particles and energy transport across the boundary of a chosen volume. This local balance distinguishes sources, storage and transfer. For a traveling plane wave, the Poynting vector points along propagation; in near fields or standing waves its local time-dependent pattern can be more complicated.

## Local Balance and Poynting Theorem {#S2}

Electromagnetic energy density in vacuum is $u=(\varepsilon_0E^2+B^2/\mu_0)/2$. Taking appropriate dot products of Ampère–Maxwell and Faraday equations and using $\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B)=\boldsymbol B\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol E)-\boldsymbol E\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol B)$ yields

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol S=-\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol E,\qquad\boldsymbol S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\boldsymbol E\times\boldsymbol B.
$$

The SI units of $\boldsymbol S$ are $\mathrm{W/m^2}$. Volume integration and the divergence theorem turn the spatial term into energy crossing the boundary. The term $\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol E$ is the power delivered by the field to matter.

## Example: Plane-Wave Intensity {#S3}

For a monochromatic plane vacuum wave, $B=E/c$ and $u=\varepsilon_0E^2$. Instantaneous energy flux is $S=\varepsilon_0cE^2$; its time average is

$$
I=\langle S\rangle=\tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2.
$$

With peak electric amplitude $E_0=100\,\mathrm{V/m}$ and $\varepsilon_0c\approx2.654\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A/V}$, the average intensity is $I\approx13.3\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$. The diagram connects flux, an illuminated surface and transferred power. This value uses the *peak* amplitude of a sinusoid, not its rms amplitude.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What does the minus sign before $\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol E$ mean?
2. How does $I$ change if $E_0$ doubles?
3. Find $p$ on an absorbing target at $I=300\,\mathrm{W/m^2}$.

### Solutions

1. Positive work on current removes energy from the electromagnetic field.
2. $I$ increases by a factor of four, because $I\propto E_0^2$.
3. $p=I/c\approx1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa}$.

## Momentum Flux and Physical Limits {#S5}

The field carries momentum density $\boldsymbol g=\boldsymbol S/c^2$ in vacuum. For normally incident radiation fully absorbed by a surface, mean pressure is $p=I/c$; an ideal mirror doubles normal momentum transfer to $p=2I/c$. The Maxwell stress tensor describes spatial fluxes of field momentum and supports force calculations on surfaces. In matter the allocation of field and mechanical momentum must be treated consistently; dispersive and absorbing media require more than a naive use of vacuum formulas. A positive incoming intensity does not establish that all energy is absorbed.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The Poynting vector balances energy and field momentum gives radiation pressure. Time-varying sources can radiate such fields.
