# M23-L6 – Solar Cells, Current–Voltage Curves and the Single-Diode Model {#L6}

**Course area:** M23 – Bachelor: Applied Physics, Photonics and Nanotechnology

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M6: circuits; M14: semiconductors; M5: temperature. [Previous lesson](M23-L5.md).

**Learning objective:** Interpret the implicit I–V relation of an equivalent circuit and evaluate electrical output power.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

Illumination generates electron–hole pairs in a suitable semiconductor, which internal fields and selective contacts separate. A solar cell is therefore neither an ideal current source nor an ideal voltage source. Its I–V curve has a short-circuit current, an open-circuit voltage and an intermediate maximum-power operating point. Temperature, irradiance, contact resistance and leakage all affect operation.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

The standard single-diode equivalent circuit combines a light-generated current source $I_{\mathrm L}$, a diode, a shunt resistance $R_{\mathrm{sh}}$ and a series resistance $R_s$. Taking positive current as delivered to the load gives the implicit relation

$$
I=I_{\mathrm L}-I_0\!\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{V+IR_s}{nV_T}\right)-1\right]-\frac{V+IR_s}{R_{\mathrm{sh}}},\quad V_T=\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{q}.
$$

Here $I_0$ is saturation current, $n$ a dimensionless ideality factor, and $V_T$ is the thermal voltage of a *single* junction at absolute temperature $T$. The internal diode voltage is $V+IR_s$. For $N_s$ series-connected cells, the effective thermal-voltage factor includes the cell count or is expressed using a modified module parameter $a=N_snV_T$. Individual-cell and module parameter sets must not be mixed. Since current appears on both sides, the model generally requires a numerical solution. Maximum electrical output is determined from $P(V)=VI(V)$, not from current or voltage alone.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

An idealised cell operating at $V=0.50\,\mathrm V$ and $I=3.0\,\mathrm A$ supplies $P=VI=1.50\,\mathrm W$. At $T=300\,\mathrm K$, the single-junction thermal voltage is $V_T=k_{\mathrm B}T/q\approx25.85\,\mathrm{mV}$. The diagram uses chosen model parameters; it is not a measured module curve.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $P$ when $V=0.40\,\mathrm V$ and $I=2.5\,\mathrm A$.
2. Compute $V_T$ at $T=300\,\mathrm K$ using $k_{\mathrm B}/q\approx86.17\,\mathrm{\mu V/K}$.
3. Why is the factor $nV_T$ of one cell insufficient for an $N_s$-cell module?

### Solutions

1. $P=0.40\cdot2.5\,\mathrm W=1.0\,\mathrm W$.
2. $V_T\approx0.02585\,\mathrm V$.
3. Series cell voltages add and module parameters must be scaled consistently, for example $a=N_snV_T$.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

The single-diode approximation omits numerous recombination effects and is not universal across technologies. Temperature and irradiance dependencies, shading and array topology require separate modelling. 

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Photovoltaic I–V curves combine a light source with a nonlinear diode and parasitic resistances. Correct sign conventions and parameter scale are essential.
