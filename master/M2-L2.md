# M2-L2 – Complex Numbers and Harmonic Phasors {#L2}

**Course area:** M2 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods for Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Trigonometry, differentiation and vector addition from M1. [Previous lesson](M2-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Convert complex numbers between forms and add phase-shifted signals using phasors.

## Two Oscillations with a Phase Offset {#S1}

Acoustic and electrical signals can have the same frequency but different phase angles. Rather than repeatedly expanding trigonometric identities, a complex phasor combines amplitude and phase in one object. The phasor is a computational representation; the measured signal is the resulting real part. The imaginary unit is $i$ with $i^2=-1$; electrical engineers often use $j$ to avoid confusion with electric current.

## Cartesian and Polar Forms {#S2}

A complex number $z=a+ib$ has magnitude $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and angle $\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$. Euler's formula reads

$$
e^{i\varphi}=\cos\varphi+i\sin\varphi,
\qquad z=|z|e^{i\varphi}.
$$

Addition is easiest in components, multiplication in polar form, because $r_1e^{i\varphi_1}r_2e^{i\varphi_2}=r_1r_2e^{i(\varphi_1+\varphi_2)}$. A real harmonic signal is represented by

$$
x(t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{\tilde X e^{i\omega t}\right\},\qquad \tilde X=Ae^{i\phi}.
$$

Here $A\ge0$ is peak amplitude, $\omega$ angular frequency and $\phi$ phase. The complex phasor $\tilde X$ itself has no time dependence.

## Example: Addition and Differentiation {#S3}

Let two same-frequency signals have $\tilde X_1=3$ and $\tilde X_2=4i$ (with the same physical unit). Their sum is $\tilde X=3+4i$, so $|\tilde X|=5$ and $\phi=\arctan(4/3)\approx53.13^\circ$:

$$
x_1(t)+x_2(t)=5\cos(\omega t+\phi).
$$

Using the same convention, differentiation gives $d x/dt=\operatorname{Re}\{i\omega\tilde X e^{i\omega t}\}$. Hence the derivative phasor is multiplied by $i\omega$, which advances the phase by $+90^\circ$ when $\omega>0$. This rule underlies impedance calculations and linear oscillation models.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Give the magnitude and phase of $1-i$.
2. Add $2+2i$ and $-1+i$.
3. What is the effect of multiplying a phasor by $-i$ when $\omega>0$?

### Solutions

1. $\sqrt2$ and $-45^\circ$.
2. $1+3i$, with magnitude $\sqrt{10}$ and phase $\arctan 3$.
3. Phase shifts by $-90^\circ$, with unchanged amplitude.

## When the Phasor Method Fails {#S5}

Adding fixed phasors directly assumes the **same** angular frequency and linear superposition. Two different frequencies cannot be represented by one constant phasor; nonlinear systems may generate additional frequencies. Fields also differ in whether phasors denote peak or RMS amplitudes: our magnitude explicitly represents the peak amplitude. Products of physical signals cannot, in general, be obtained by simply multiplying their phasors and taking the real part.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Magnitude and phase provide the polar description of a complex number. Phasors add for same-frequency linear signals; next, matrices organise linear relationships between measured quantities.
