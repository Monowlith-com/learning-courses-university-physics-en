# M14-L4 – Bloch Theorem, Band Structure and Brillouin Zones {#L4}

**Course area:** M14 – Bachelor: Solid-State Physics

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M14-L1, stationary Schrödinger equation and Fourier analysis. [Previous lesson](M14-L3.md).

**Learning objective:** Describe Bloch states, interpret the first Brillouin zone, and explain how an energy gap arises at its boundary.

## Electrons in a Periodic Potential {#S1}

Single-electron motion in an ideal periodic potential differs fundamentally from a free particle. The Hamiltonian $H=\mathbf p^2/(2m)+V(\mathbf r)$ has the translational symmetry $V(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=V(\mathbf r)$ for each lattice vector $\mathbf R$. Its eigenstates can be chosen as Bloch waves: plane waves modulated by lattice-periodic functions. In a one-particle approximation the allowed energies form bands $E_n(\mathbf k)$. The first Brillouin zone is a primitive cell of reciprocal space, containing each inequivalent crystal momentum once. The filling of these bands strongly influences whether a material is metallic, semiconducting or insulating.

## Bloch Form and Band Gaps {#S2}

Bloch's theorem writes the wave function as

$$
\psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\quad u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r).
$$

Wave vectors differing by a reciprocal vector $\mathbf G$ have the same lattice-translation phase. In the nearly-free-electron picture a Fourier component of the periodic potential couples free-electron states separated by $\mathbf G$. When these are degenerate at a zone boundary, a nonzero coupling $V_{\mathbf G}$ removes that degeneracy. A simple two-state treatment yields the gap 

$$
\Delta E=2\lvert V_{\mathbf G}\rvert.
$$

Thus a gap arises from coherent periodic backscattering, not from a particle losing energy merely by crossing an atomic plane.

## Example: Zone Boundary and Bragg Coupling {#S3}

For a one-dimensional lattice with $a=0.50\,\mathrm{nm}$ the first zone boundary is at $\lvert k\rvert=\pi/a\approx6.283\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. Let the relevant Fourier component of a weak potential have magnitude $\lvert V_G\rvert=0.20\,\mathrm{eV}$. The two-state gap is then

$$
\Delta E=2(0.20\,\mathrm{eV})=0.40\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$

The illustration contrasts the crossing of uncoupled free-electron branches with the avoided crossing produced by the periodic potential. This is a schematic two-level model at one zone boundary. A real material requires three-dimensional lattice geometry, all relevant ionic potentials and, where needed, spin–orbit coupling, electronic interactions and magnetic order.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find $\pi/a$ for $a=0.25\,\mathrm{nm}$.
2. What gap is produced by $\lvert V_G\rvert=0.10\,\mathrm{eV}$?
3. Why does an isolated, completely filled band not act as an ordinary conductor?

### Solutions

1. $\pi/a\approx12.57\,\mathrm{nm^{-1}}$.
2. $\Delta E=0.20\,\mathrm{eV}$.
3. At equilibrium current contributions from filled-band states cancel.

## Band Picture and Its Assumptions {#S5}

Crystal momentum $\hbar\mathbf k$ is defined modulo reciprocal lattice vectors, rather than always being the free mechanical momentum of an electron. In a completely filled isolated band, velocities at opposing wave vectors ordinarily cancel in the net current. Strong correlations can nevertheless produce insulating behavior even in a partly filled band, beyond simple one-electron band theory. Disorder may localize states. A one-particle band gap is also not automatically identical to an optical excitation threshold in the presence of excitons or other interactions. A band diagram gives the allowed Bloch-state energies; transport requires further assumptions about scattering and occupation.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Periodic potentials yield Bloch states, energy bands and, under appropriate conditions, energy gaps. The next step is electronic transport.
