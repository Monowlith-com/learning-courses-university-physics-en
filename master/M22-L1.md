# M22-L1 – Brownian Motion, Diffusion and Time Scales {#L1}

**Course area:** M22 – Bachelor: Biophysics

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M5: thermodynamics; M13: statistical physics; M17: stochastic simulations.

**Learning objective:** Model molecular diffusion as a random walk, derive mean-square displacement and estimate diffusion times.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

A fluorescently labelled protein moves irregularly even in a liquid with no net flow. Many collisions with surrounding molecules produce its fluctuating trajectory. One observed path therefore does not represent a single deterministic velocity; the distribution of many trajectories is more informative. Thermal motion can smooth a concentration gradient without requiring bulk fluid motion.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

Consider independent, zero-mean displacements with finite variance along one dimension. Variances add: after $n$ steps, $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle=n\sigma^2$. Set $t=n\Delta t$ and define $D=\sigma^2/(2\Delta t)$. This yields

$$
\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle=2Dt,\qquad \partial_t c=D\nabla^2 c.
$$

For independent motion in three dimensions, the corresponding mean-square displacement is $6Dt$. The continuum limit gives the diffusion equation. Under the Stokes assumptions for a rigid sphere in a dilute Newtonian fluid with no slip, $D=k_{\mathrm B}T/(6\pi\eta r)$ approximately; this relation is not universal for intracellular proteins. The typical one-dimensional time to diffuse over a length $L$ scales as $\tau\sim L^2/(2D)$.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

For a freely diffusing ideal probe with $D=0.50\,\mathrm{\mu m^2/s}$ observed for $t=4\,\mathrm s$, the root-mean-square displacement is $\sqrt{\langle\Delta x^2\rangle}=\sqrt{2\cdot0.50\cdot4}\,\mathrm{\mu m}=2.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The diagram depicts the theoretical MSD and the spreading of an idealised concentration profile, not recorded cell trajectories.

## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Find the one-dimensional RMS displacement for $D=0.8\,\mathrm{\mu m^2/s}$ and $t=10\,\mathrm s$.
2. Estimate $\tau$ for $L=10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $D=1\,\mathrm{\mu m^2/s}$.
3. Why can $\langle\Delta x\rangle=0$ while $\langle\Delta x^2\rangle>0$?

### Solutions

1. $\sqrt{2Dt}=\sqrt{16}\,\mathrm{\mu m}=4\,\mathrm{\mu m}$.
2. $\tau\sim L^2/(2D)=100/2\,\mathrm s=50\,\mathrm s$.
3. Positive and negative steps cancel in the mean but contribute non-negative squares to the variance.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

Stokes-Einstein assumes a continuum solvent, negligible inertia, spherical particles and weak interactions. Crowding, binding, active transport and anomalous diffusion can change cellular trajectories. The pictured concentration profile is model-generated.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

For normal diffusion, mean-square displacement grows linearly with time. The squared length scale sets the order of magnitude of diffusion times.
