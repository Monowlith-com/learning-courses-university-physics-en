# M17-L2 – Numerical Integration and Quadrature {#L2}

**Course area:** M17 – Bachelor: Computational Physics and Simulation

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** M2: differentiation/integration; M17-L1: discretization error. [Previous lesson](M17-L1.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive trapezoidal and Simpson quadrature, compare with a reference and assess step-size convergence.

## Riemann Sums and Nodes {#S1}

Physics uses integrals for work $W=\int F(x)\,dx$, normalization, spectral content and integrated fluxes. If a function is known at nodes $x_i=a+ih$, quadrature approximates its area by a weighted sum. For $n$ equal subintervals, $h=(b-a)/n$. The outcome depends on the function's smoothness, noise in measurements and possible discontinuities in the integration range. A high-order formula cannot recover an unresolved sharp peak.

## Trapezoidal and Simpson Rules {#S2}

The composite trapezoidal rule replaces the curve on each subinterval by a line. For sufficiently smooth functions its global error is of order $h^2$. Composite Simpson quadrature integrates quadratic interpolants over pairs of intervals, giving global order $h^4$ under suitable smoothness assumptions; $n$ must be even. Both methods use function values rather than a symbolic antiderivative. Simpson is exact for polynomials through degree three when the function evaluations are exact.

$$
T_h=h\left[\frac{f_0+f_n}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_i\right].
$$

$$
S_h=\frac h3\left[f_0+f_n+4\sum_{i\,\mathrm{odd}}f_i+2\sum_{\substack{i\,\mathrm{even}\\0<i<n}}f_i\right].
$$

## Example: Integrating a Polynomial Exactly {#S3}

For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, the exact integral is $I=1/3$. Four equal intervals give $h=1/4$. Simpson's weights are $1,4,2,4,1$ and the weighted sum is multiplied by $h/3$, yielding exactly $1/3$. Composite trapezoidal quadrature instead gives $0.34375$, an excess of $0.0104167$. This benchmark tests an implementation but says nothing by itself about nonsmooth or rapidly oscillating functions.

**Executable Python example:**

```python
n = 4
h = 1.0 / n
f = lambda x: x*x
y = [f(i*h) for i in range(n+1)]
trap = h*(0.5*y[0]+sum(y[1:-1])+0.5*y[-1])
simpson = h/3*(y[0]+y[-1]+4*sum(y[1:-1:2])+2*sum(y[2:-1:2]))
print(trap, simpson)
```

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is the minimum number of subintervals for Simpson quadrature, and what restriction applies to $n$?
2. Compute the four-interval trapezoidal approximation to $\int_0^1x^2dx$.
3. By which factor should an asymptotic $O(h^4)$ error decrease when $h$ is halved?

### Solutions

1. At least two, with even $n$.
2. $0.34375$; the exact value is $1/3$.
3. Approximately a factor of $16$.

## Convergence, Smoothness and Singularities {#S5}

Refine $h$ repeatedly and compare with an independent reference or a much finer computation. For a method of order $p$, the asymptotic error should decrease by roughly $2^p$ when $h$ is halved. Failure can indicate roundoff, nonsmoothness, endpoint singularities or underresolution. Refinement of actual measured samples does not create data: interpolation between observations adds assumptions and uncertainty. Singular integrands may require a change of variables or splitting the interval.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Quadrature order depends on smoothness and resolution; convergence tests belong to the result.
