# M25-L3 – Algorithms, model cost and numerical scaling {#L3}

**Course area:** M25 – Bachelor: Interdisciplinary applications and scientific practice

**Level:** Bachelor · elective

**Prerequisites:** M17: numerical methods; elementary sums and Python loops. [Previous lesson](M25-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Justify the computational cost of a physical procedure as a function of problem size and verify it with operation counts.

## Guiding question and observation {#S1}

A finer simulation grid has a computational cost: doubling the number of points may double, quadruple or even more strongly increase runtime, depending on the algorithm. Accuracy and memory consumption must be considered alongside scaling. An estimate begins by defining problem size $n$ and the dominant basic operation. It is a model of the algorithm, not a benchmark measurement on specific hardware.

## Physical model and equations {#S2}

A single pass over $n$ measurements performs about $n$ updates and therefore has linear cost. Comparing all ordered pairs requires $n^2$ comparisons. Big-O notation gives an asymptotic upper growth bound up to constant factors; $\Theta$ states a tight asymptotic order. For a two-dimensional grid, define $n$ as the total number of cells rather than silently switching to side length. A numerically stable method may justifiably cost more than a faster unstable approximation.

$$
T_{\mathrm{linear}}(n)\propto n,\qquad T_{\mathrm{pairs}}(n)\propto n^2,\qquad \frac{T_{\mathrm{pairs}}(2n)}{T_{\mathrm{pairs}}(n)}=4.
$$

## Worked example and teaching figure {#S3}

For $n=1000$ measurements, a simple accumulation needs about $1000$ additions, whereas an ordered all-pairs comparison needs $10^6$ comparisons. At $n=2000$ the counts become $2000$ and $4\cdot10^6$. Thus the quadratic method costs four times as much when data size merely doubles. A program can count comparisons explicitly; runtime benchmarks complement but do not replace a complexity argument. ## Self-check {#S4}

### Exercises

1. By what factor does $n^2$ grow when $n$ triples?
2. How many ordered pairs including the diagonal exist for $n=20$?
3. Why can an $O(n)$ method be slower than $O(n\log n)$ for small $n$?

### Solutions

1. A factor of $9$.
2. $20^2=400$.
3. Constant factors, memory access and implementation overhead may dominate asymptotic growth for small inputs.

## Limits of validity and quality {#S5}

Complexity is tied to a precisely specified algorithm. $O(n^2)$ alone gives neither wall-clock seconds nor conclusions about parallelism, memory use or numerical error.

## Summary and outlook {#S6}

Physical modeling needs two arguments: why the equations are appropriate and why the computation remains feasible for the intended problem size.
