# M8-L1 – First-Order Linear Differential Equations {#L1}

**Course area:** M8 – Bachelor: Mathematical Methods

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prerequisites:** Differentiation, product rule, definite integrals and the exponential function. [Previous lesson](M7-L8.md).

**Learning objective:** Derive an integrating factor, impose an initial condition and assess the long-term limit.

## From Physical Law to Differential Equation {#S1}

Many time-dependent physical systems do not respond instantaneously to an input. The temperature of a body, capacitor charge or a simple damping variable may approach equilibrium under appropriate modeling assumptions. The mathematical form is $y'(t)+p(t)y(t)=q(t)$. The unknown $y$ depends on time, while the coefficients $p$ and $q$ are prescribed. The differential equation alone generally specifies a family of solutions. An initial value $y(t_0)=y_0$ selects a definite trajectory when the coefficients satisfy suitable continuity conditions. A physical model must distinguish the evolving state variable from the external drive and must describe which effects have been deliberately neglected.

## Integrating Factor: Derivation {#S2}

Choose the integrating factor $\mu(t)=\exp[\int_{t_0}^{t}p(s)\,\mathrm ds]$. Since $\mu'=p\mu$, the product rule immediately yields $(\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu' y=\mu q$. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t$ gives the initial-value solution:

$$
y(t)=\mu(t)^{-1}\left[y_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}\mu(s)q(s)\,\mathrm ds\right].
$$

For constant coefficients $p=a>0$ and $q=b$, this reduces to $y(t)=b/a+(y_0-b/a)e^{-a(t-t_0)}$. The steady value is $y_\infty=b/a$ and the relaxation time is $\tau=1/a$. Exponential relaxation follows from this particular model and its initial condition; it is not a universal feature of every relaxation process.

## Example: Exponential Approach {#S3}

Consider $y'+2y=4$ with $y(0)=1$, where time is measured in seconds. The factor is $\mu=e^{2t}$. From $(e^{2t}y)'=4e^{2t}$ one obtains $y=2+Ce^{-2t}$; the initial value gives $C=-1$ and therefore

$$
y(t)=2-e^{-2t}.
$$

At $t=1\,\mathrm s$ we find $y\approx1.8647$. As $t\to\infty$, $y$ approaches the value $2$ from below. Direct substitution checks the result: $y'=2e^{-2t}$ and $y'+2y=2e^{-2t}+4-2e^{-2t}=4$. The figure shows this rising curve and its horizontal equilibrium line. Dimensional consistency additionally requires the coefficient multiplying $y$ and represented by the number $2$ to have units $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, while the drive must have units of $y/\mathrm s$.

## Self-assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. Solve $y'+3y=6$ with $y(0)=0$.
2. Find the relaxation time for $y'+4y=8$ when $t$ is measured in seconds.
3. Verify by differentiation whether $y=2-e^{-2t}$ satisfies $y'+2y=4$.

### Solutions

1. $y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t})$.
2. $\tau=1/4\,\mathrm s=0.25\,\mathrm s$.
3. $y'=2e^{-2t}$; adding $2y=4-2e^{-2t}$ gives $y'+2y=4$.

## Existence, Units and Limitations {#S5}

The integrating-factor method solves linear first-order equations with prescribed coefficients, not arbitrary nonlinear dynamics. If $p$ or $q$ is discontinuous, existence requires additional care. A negative constant coefficient $a$ produces growth rather than relaxation, and time-dependent $a(t)$ generally prevents assigning a single constant relaxation time. Physically, one must also verify that the assumed linear response remains appropriate over the entire state interval. A mathematical solution should not be extrapolated over temperatures, voltages or concentrations for which the constitutive relation used to derive the equation has ceased to apply.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

The product rule gives the integrating factor; the initial condition fixes the constant. Several coupled state variables lead next to matrix methods.
