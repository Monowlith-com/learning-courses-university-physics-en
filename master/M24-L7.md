# M24-L7 – Radiation Protection, Justification and Imaging Decisions {#L7}

**Course area:** M24 – Bachelor: Medical Physics and Imaging

**Level:** Bachelor · Elective

**Prerequisites:** M15: interactions of radiation; M16: measurement uncertainty; M24-L1: dose terminology. [Previous lesson](M24-L6.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain clinical justification and optimisation in diagnostic imaging and distinguish physical dose from clinical judgement.

## Question and Observation {#S1}

Not every technically possible examination is medically justified. For procedures involving ionising radiation, clinical benefit and potential detriment must be weighed before exposure. Acquisition conditions should then be optimised to obtain the needed diagnostic information at an appropriate exposure. This is not equivalent to minimising dose without regard to diagnostic image quality.

## Physical Model and Equations {#S2}

Absorbed dose $D$ quantifies locally deposited energy per mass, expressed in gray. Radiation-protection quantities such as organ and effective dose involve additional physical and population-based weighting and cannot be equated to an individual risk prediction. The IAEA international basic safety standards distinguish justification of medical exposure from optimisation of protection. Technical parameters depend on the clinical question, modality, patient characteristics and required image quality.

$$
D=\frac{E_{\mathrm{abs}}}{m},\qquad 1\,\mathrm{Gy}=1\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}.
$$

X-rays and CT use externally generated ionising radiation; PET and SPECT use radioactive tracers, whose distribution and clearance affect exposure. MRI uses static and time-varying magnetic fields and radio-frequency energy; ultrasound uses mechanical waves. Absence of ionising X-rays does not make MRI or ultrasound free of safety requirements. Contraindications, equipment parameters and specialised safety guidelines must still be considered. This lesson does not give clinical instructions.

## Worked Example and Teaching Diagram {#S3}

For a purely mechanical example, hypothetical uniform deposition of $E_{\mathrm{abs}}=0.020\,\mathrm J$ in $m=2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ gives $D=0.010\,\mathrm{Gy}=10\,\mathrm{mGy}$. This does not define an individual's effective dose in Sv or justify an examination. ## Self-Assessment {#S4}

### Exercises

1. What is $D$ for $E_{\mathrm{abs}}=0.005\,\mathrm J$ deposited in $1\,\mathrm{kg}$?
2. Why is the smallest technically attainable dose not always the relevant optimisation target?
3. Which methods do not themselves use ionising X-ray photons: CT, MRI, ultrasound or PET?

### Solutions

1. $D=0.005\,\mathrm{Gy}=5\,\mathrm{mGy}$.
2. Diagnostic utility requires adequate information; too few measured events may render an examination unusable.
3. MRI and ultrasound; PET relies on radioactive emissions and CT on X-ray radiation.

## Validity Limits and Quality {#S5}

These principles do not replace national regulations, physician justification, radiation-protection expertise or equipment-specific safety standards. All numbers are invented and are not examination settings.

## Summary and Outlook {#S6}

Justification addresses why an examination is needed; optimisation addresses how an already justified examination can supply useful information at an appropriate exposure.
